WEEK 1: REPRODUCTIVE BEHAVIOURS IN ANIMALS CONTENT
Meaning of Reproductive Behaviour

Reproductive behaviour encompasses all activities and patterns exhibited by animals before, during, and after mating to ensure successful reproduction and offspring survival. These behaviours are genetically controlled and evolutionarily shaped to maximize reproductive success. They include mate attraction, courtship, mating, nesting, and parental care.
Courtship Behaviour

Courtship is a sequence of behavioural patterns displayed by animals before mating. It serves multiple biological functions essential for reproductive success.
Types of Courtship Displays:

Visual displays: Peacocks displaying elaborate tail feathers; fireflies producing bioluminescent flashes; male birds performing elaborate dances
Auditory signals: Birds singing complex songs; frogs and toads croaking; crickets chirping; whales producing songs
Chemical communication: Moths and butterflies releasing pheromones; ants using chemical trails; mammals marking territories with scent
Tactile interactions: Spiders vibrating webs in specific patterns; birds engaging in mutual preening; mammals nuzzling
Behavioral displays: Bower birds building and decorating elaborate structures; male fiddler crabs waving oversized claws
Functions of Courtship Behaviour:

Species recognition to prevent hybridization
Assessment of mate quality, health, and genetic fitness
Synchronization of reproductive physiology between partners
Reduction of natural aggression to allow mating
Formation and strengthening of pair bonds
Stimulation of hormone release for reproduction
Mating Systems

Animals exhibit diverse mating systems determined by ecological, physiological, and evolutionary factors.
Monogamy (One male + One female)

Definition: Exclusive pairing between one male and one female for at least one breeding season
Types: Serial monogamy (successive single partners) and lifelong monogamy
Examples: Swans, eagles, albatrosses, gibbons, beavers, wolves
Characteristics: High parental investment, bi-parental care, territorial defense
Advantages: Shared parental duties, better offspring survival, resource defense
Disadvantages: Limited genetic diversity, reproductive opportunities restricted

Polygamy (Multiple mating partners)

Polygyny (One male + Multiple females)

Most common mating system in mammals
Examples: Lions, gorillas, elephant seals, red deer, chickens, peacocks
Characteristics: Sexual dimorphism, male competition, harems
Advantages: Strong males pass superior genes, males maximize reproductive output
Disadvantages: Intense male competition, some males excluded from reproduction, minimal paternal care
Polyandry (One female + Multiple males)

Rare in animals, more common in certain bird and fish species
Examples: Jacanas, phalaropes, some tropical fish
Characteristics: Females larger and more aggressive, males provide parental care
Advantages: Females maximize egg production, males ensure some offspring survive
Disadvantages: Paternity uncertainty, lower genetic contribution per male

Promiscuity (Multiple males + Multiple females)

Neither sex forms pair bonds
Examples: Chimpanzees, bonobos, many amphibians and fish
Characteristics: No lasting bonds, sperm competition, little parental care
Advantages: Maximum genetic diversity, no resource investment in partners
Disadvantages: High disease transmission risk, low offspring survival without care
Parental Care in Different Animal Groups

Fishes Most fish species exhibit external fertilization with minimal parental investment. However, some show remarkable care:
Cichlids: Guard eggs and fry, some species are mouthbrooders
Sticklebacks: Males build nests, guard eggs, fan them for oxygenation
Seahorses and pipefish: Males carry fertilized eggs in specialized brood pouches
Tilapia: Mouthbrooding where parent carries eggs and young in mouth
Catfish: Some species guard eggs until hatching
Most marine fish release millions of eggs with no care, relying on numbers for species survival
Amphibians Amphibians show varying degrees of parental care:

Frogs and toads: Most provide no care after egg laying; some guard eggs
Midwife toads: Males carry egg strings wrapped around hind legs until hatching
Poison dart frogs: Transport tadpoles on their backs to water-filled plants; some provide unfertilized eggs as food
Surinam toads: Eggs embedded in female's back skin where they develop
Some salamanders: Guard eggs and may remain with larvae
External fertilization and aquatic larval stage limit extensive care

Birds Birds exhibit the most extensive parental care among non-mammalian vertebrates:

Nest building: Both parents often participate in construction
Incubation: Shared or alternating between parents; maintains optimal egg temperature
Feeding nestlings: Regurgitation, bringing insects, teaching foraging
Protection: Defense against predators, sheltering from weather
Teaching: Flight training, hunting skills, migration routes
Extended care: Many species care for young weeks or months after fledging
Altricial young (helpless at birth) require intensive care
Precocial young (mobile at birth) still receive protection and guidance

Mammals Mammals show the highest level of parental investment:

Gestation: Internal development with direct maternal nourishment
Lactation: Extended milk feeding providing nutrition and antibodies
Protection: Constant vigilance against predators
Teaching: Hunting techniques, social behaviors, territory knowledge
Social learning: Cultural transmission of behaviors
Extended dependency: Prolonged juvenile period in many species
Maternal care dominant: Though some species show paternal involvement (wolves, marmosets, humans)
Play behavior: Facilitates learning and physical development
Examples: Elephants have 22-month gestation and years of maternal care; primates show extensive teaching behaviors

WEEK 2: BIOLOGY OF HEREDITY (GENETICS I)

CONTENT
Concept of Heredity

Heredity is the biological process by which parents transmit genetic characteristics to their offspring through genes. It explains why offspring resemble their parents and other relatives while also showing variations. Heredity accounts for both the similarities and differences observed within families and species. The genetic information passed from one generation to another is encoded in DNA molecules located on chromosomes within the cell nucleus.
Brief History of Genetics - Gregor Mendel's Experiments

Background: Genetics as a scientific discipline began with the work of Gregor Johann Mendel (1822-1884), an Austrian monk who conducted groundbreaking experiments on garden pea plants (Pisum sativum) in the monastery garden between 1856 and 1863.
Why Mendel Chose Garden Peas:

Easy to cultivate and had short generation time
Produced many offspring, providing large sample sizes for statistical analysis
Had distinct contrasting characteristics (tall vs. short, round vs. wrinkled seeds)
Both self-pollination and cross-pollination were easily controlled
Pure-breeding varieties were available
Flowers could be protected from unwanted pollination

Mendel's Experimental Approach: Mendel studied seven contrasting characteristics in pea plants:
Plant height (tall vs. dwarf)
Seed shape (round vs. wrinkled)
Seed color (yellow vs. green)
Pod shape (inflated vs. constricted)
Pod color (green vs. yellow)
Flower position (axial vs. terminal)
Flower color (purple vs. white)

Mendel's Methodology:

Used pure-breeding lines (homozygous parents)
Carefully controlled pollination
Counted and recorded all offspring
Applied mathematical analysis to results
Conducted experiments across multiple generations (P, F₁, F₂, F₃)

Significance: Though published in 1866, Mendel's work was not recognized until 1900 when three scientists (Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns, and Erich von Tschermak) independently rediscovered his principles. Mendel is now called the "Father of Genetics."
Basic Genetic Terminologies

Gene A gene is a unit of heredity; a segment of DNA on a chromosome that codes for a specific protein or trait. Genes determine inherited characteristics such as eye color, blood group, height, and disease susceptibility. Each gene occupies a specific location (locus) on a chromosome. Humans have approximately 20,000-25,000 genes.
Allele Alleles are alternative forms of the same gene occupying the same position on homologous chromosomes. They control the same characteristic but may produce different expressions. For example, the gene for height in pea plants has two alleles: one for tall (T) and one for short (t). Alleles arise through mutation and contribute to genetic variation.
Chromosome Chromosomes are thread-like structures in the cell nucleus composed of DNA and proteins (histones). They carry genes arranged linearly. Humans have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs): 44 autosomes and 2 sex chromosomes. Chromosomes exist as homologous pairs in diploid organisms, with one chromosome from each parent.
Genotype Genotype refers to the genetic makeup or combination of alleles an organism possesses for a particular trait. It represents the genes inherited from both parents. Genotype is
written using letters (e.g., TT, Tt, tt for height). The genotype determines the potential characteristics but may not always be visible.
Phenotype Phenotype is the observable physical, biochemical, and physiological characteristics of an organism resulting from the interaction between genotype and environment. It represents the expression of genes. Examples include height, eye color, blood group, and behavior. The phenotype can be influenced by environmental factors.
Homozygous An organism is homozygous for a trait when it possesses two identical alleles for that characteristic. Written as TT (homozygous dominant) or tt (homozygous recessive). Homozygous organisms breed true, producing offspring with the same phenotype when self- fertilized. Also called pure-breeding.
Heterozygous An organism is heterozygous when it possesses two different alleles for a particular trait (e.g., Tt). The organism exhibits the dominant phenotype but carries the recessive allele. Heterozygous individuals do not breed true and can produce offspring showing both dominant and recessive phenotypes. Also called hybrid.
Dominant Allele A dominant allele is expressed in the phenotype even when present in single copy (heterozygous condition). It masks the effect of the recessive allele. Represented by capital letters (T, R, Y). Only one dominant allele is needed for expression.
Recessive Allele A recessive allele is only expressed phenotypically when two copies are present (homozygous recessive condition). It is masked in the presence of a dominant allele. Represented by lowercase letters (t, r, y). Both alleles must be recessive for the trait to appear.
Locus (plural: Loci) The specific physical location of a gene on a chromosome. Each gene has a specific locus, and alleles of the same gene occupy corresponding loci on homologous chromosomes.
Hybrid An offspring resulting from a cross between parents with different alleles for one or more traits. First-generation hybrids (F₁) from pure-breeding parents are heterozygous.
Mendel's Laws of Inheritance

Law of Dominance (First Law) When two organisms that are pure-breeding for contrasting traits are crossed, only one trait (the dominant trait) appears in the first filial generation (F₁), while the other trait (recessive) remains hidden but is not lost.
Explanation:

In a heterozygous condition, the dominant allele is fully expressed
The recessive allele is present but masked
The recessive trait reappears in F₂ generation

Example: When Mendel crossed pure-breeding tall pea plants (TT) with pure-breeding short pea plants (tt):
P generation: TT (tall) × tt (short)
F₁ generation: All Tt (100% tall)
The tall trait dominated; short trait was hidden but not lost
F₂ generation showed both tall and short plants

Law of Segregation (Second Law) During gamete formation, the paired alleles for a characteristic separate (segregate) so that each gamete receives only one allele from each pair. The alleles reunite randomly during fertilization.
Explanation:

Alleles exist in pairs in diploid organisms
During meiosis, homologous chromosomes separate
Each gamete receives only one allele from each pair
Fertilization restores the diploid number with two alleles per trait
This explains the 3:1 ratio in F₂ generation

Example: F₁ heterozygous tall plant (Tt):

Produces two types of gametes: T and t (in equal proportions)
Each gamete carries only one allele
When F₁ plants self-pollinate: Tt × Tt
F₂ offspring genotypes: TT, Tt, Tt, tt (1:2:1 ratio)
F₂ phenotypes: 3 tall : 1 short

Law of Independent Assortment (Third Law) When two or more pairs of contrasting characteristics are considered together, the inheritance of one pair of alleles is independent of the inheritance of other pairs, provided the genes are on different chromosomes or far apart on the same chromosome.
Explanation:

Different gene pairs segregate independently during meiosis
Applies to genes on different chromosomes (unlinked genes)
Each trait is inherited separately without influencing others
Results in new combinations of traits in offspring
Creates genetic variation

Example: Mendel crossed plants differing in two traits:

P generation: Round yellow seeds (RRYY) × Wrinkled green seeds (rryy)
F₁ generation: All RrYy (100% round yellow)
F₂ generation from F₁ self-cross: 9 round yellow : 3 round green : 3 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green
The 9:3:3:1 ratio demonstrates independent inheritance
Application of Mendel's Laws

Monohybrid Cross (studied in Week 3): A cross involving one pair of contrasting characteristics, demonstrating the Law of Segregation.
Dihybrid Cross (studied in Week 3): A cross involving two pairs of contrasting characteristics, demonstrating the Law of Independent Assortment.
Significance of Mendel's Work:
Established genetics as a scientific discipline
Provided mathematical basis for inheritance
Explained how traits are transmitted across generations
Laid foundation for modern genetics, genetic counseling, and biotechnology
Helped understand genetic diseases and breeding programs

WEEK 3: GENETICS II

CONTENT
Monohybrid Cross

A monohybrid cross is a genetic cross between two individuals that involves one pair of contrasting characteristics controlled by a single gene with two alleles. It demonstrates Mendel's Law of Segregation.
Key Features:

Involves one trait with two contrasting forms
Uses one gene with two alleles
Demonstrates 3:1 phenotypic ratio in F₂ generation
Shows 1:2:1 genotypic ratio in F₂ generation

Example 1: Height in Pea Plants

Cross 1: Homozygous tall × Homozygous short

Let T = allele for tallness (dominant) Let t = allele for shortness (recessive)

P generation: TT (tall) × tt (short) Gametes: T × t F₁ generation: Tt (all tall - 100%) F₁ phenotypic ratio: 4 tall : 0 short (All tall) F₁ genotypic ratio: All Tt (heterozygous) Cross 2: F₁ self-cross (Tt × Tt)
P generation: Tt × Tt Gametes: T, t × T, t Punnett Square:

F₂ genotypes: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt F₂ phenotypes: 3 tall : 1 short (75% tall, 25% short)

Cross 3: Test Cross (Tt × tt)

A test cross is used to determine whether an organism showing the dominant phenotype is homozygous or heterozygous. The organism is crossed with a homozygous recessive individual.
P generation: Tt (tall) × tt (short) Gametes: T, t × t, t Punnett Square:

F₁ genotypes: 2 Tt : 2 tt (1:1 ratio) F₁ phenotypes: 2 tall : 2 short (50% tall, 50% short)

Example 2: Seed Shape in Pea Plants

Let R = round seeds (dominant) Let r = wrinkled seeds (recessive)

P generation: RR (round) × rr (wrinkled) F₁: All Rr (100% round) F₁ × F₁: Rr × Rr F₂: 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr (3 round : 1 wrinkled)
Dihybrid Cross

A dihybrid cross involves two pairs of contrasting characteristics controlled by two different genes located on different chromosomes. It demonstrates Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment.
Key Features:

Involves two traits simultaneously
Uses two genes with four alleles
Demonstrates 9:3:3:1 phenotypic ratio in F₂ generation
Produces 16 possible offspring combinations

Example: Seed Shape and Color in Pea Plants

Let R = round seeds (dominant), r = wrinkled seeds (recessive) Let Y = yellow seeds (dominant), y = green seeds (recessive)
Cross 1: Parental Cross

P generation: RRYY (round yellow) × rryy (wrinkled green) Gametes: RY × ry F₁ generation: All RrYy (100% round yellow)
Cross 2: F₁ Self-Cross (RrYy × RrYy)

Each F₁ plant produces four types of gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry (in equal proportions) Punnett Square (4×4):

F₂ Phenotypic Results:

9 Round Yellow (R_Y_): RRYY, RRYy (×2), RrYY (×2), RrYy (×4)
3 Round Green (R_yy): RRyy, Rryy (×2)
3 Wrinkled Yellow (rrY_): rrYY, rrYy (×2)
1 Wrinkled Green (rryy): rryy

F₂ Phenotypic ratio: 9:3:3:1
F₂ Genotypic Results: 9 different genotypes in specific ratios:

1 RRYY : 2 RRYy : 2 RrYY : 4 RrYy : 1 RRyy : 2 Rryy : 1 rrYY : 2 rrYy : 1 rryy

Interpretation:

Round yellow = 9/16 (56.25%)
Round green = 3/16 (18.75%)
Wrinkled yellow = 3/16 (18.75%)
Wrinkled green = 1/16 (6.25%)
New combinations (recombinants): Round green and wrinkled yellow
Parental types: Round yellow and wrinkled green
Punnett Square Applications

The Punnett Square is a graphical method for predicting the outcomes of genetic crosses. It was developed by British geneticist Reginald Punnett.
Steps for Constructing a Punnett Square:

Determine parent genotypes: Identify the alleles each parent possesses
Determine gamete types: List all possible gametes each parent can produce
Draw the grid: Create a square with gametes from one parent across the top and the other down the side
Fill in boxes: Combine alleles from each gamete to show offspring genotypes
Count genotypes: Tally each unique genotype
Determine phenotypes: Identify the physical expression of each genotype
Calculate ratios: Express genotypic and phenotypic ratios

Applications of Punnett Squares:

Predicting offspring from known parents
Determining probability of inheriting specific traits
Identifying carrier status in genetic diseases
Planning breeding programs in agriculture
Genetic counseling for inherited disorders
Understanding inheritance patterns
Sex Determination in Humans

Sex determination is the biological mechanism that determines whether an organism develops as male or female.
Chromosomal Basis:

Humans have 46 chromosomes: 44 autosomes + 2 sex chromosomes
Females: XX (homogametic - produce one type of egg)
Males: XY (heterogametic - produce two types of sperm)
Sex is determined at fertilization

Mechanism of Sex Determination:

Mother (XX) produces only X-bearing eggs Father (XY) produces two types of sperm: X- bearing (50%) and Y-bearing (50%)
Punnett Square for Sex Determination:

Result:

50% XX (females)
50% XY (males)
Sex ratio: 1:1 (males:females)
Father's sperm determines the sex of the child
Mother always contributes X chromosome

The Y Chromosome:

Smaller than X chromosome
Contains SRY gene (Sex-determining Region Y)
SRY gene triggers male development
Presence of Y = male; absence = female

Abnormalities in Sex Chromosomes:

Turner syndrome: XO (45 chromosomes, female)
Klinefelter syndrome: XXY (47 chromosomes, male)
Triple X syndrome: XXX (47 chromosomes, female)
XYY syndrome: XYY (47 chromosomes, male)
Sex-Linked Traits

Sex-linked traits are characteristics controlled by genes located on sex chromosomes, particularly the X chromosome. These traits show different inheritance patterns in males and females.
Characteristics of X-Linked Traits:

Genes located on the X chromosome
More common in males than females
Affected males cannot pass the trait to sons
Carrier females usually unaffected (heterozygous)
Affected females must inherit mutant allele from both parents

Hemophilia (Bleeder's Disease)

Hemophilia is a genetic disorder where blood fails to clot normally due to deficiency of clotting factors.
Types:

Hemophilia A: Factor VIII deficiency (most common)
Hemophilia B: Factor IX deficiency

Genetics:

X-linked recessive disorder
Let Xᴴ = normal allele, Xʰ = hemophilia allele
Genotypes:
Xᴴ Xᴴ = normal female
Xᴴ Xʰ = carrier female (normal phenotype)
Xʰ Xʰ = hemophiliac female (very rare)
Xᴴ Y = normal male
Xʰ Y = hemophiliac male

Example Cross 1: Normal male × Carrier female

P generation: Xᴴ Y (normal male) × Xᴴ Xʰ (carrier female)

Results:

25% normal females
25% carrier females
25% normal males
25% hemophiliac males
50% of males affected

Example Cross 2: Hemophiliac male × Normal female
P generation: Xʰ Y × Xᴴ Xᴴ Results:
All daughters are carriers (Xᴴ Xʰ)
All sons are normal (Xᴴ Y)
No affected offspring, but daughters carry the gene

Symptoms:

Prolonged bleeding from minor injuries
Spontaneous internal bleeding
Joint pain and swelling from internal bleeding
Blood in urine or stool

Color Blindness (Red-Green Color Blindness)

Color blindness is the inability to distinguish certain colors, most commonly red and green.

Genetics:

X-linked recessive disorder
Let Xᶜ = normal vision, Xᶜ = color blindness
Genotypes:
Xᶜ Xᶜ = normal female
Xᶜ Xᶜ = carrier female (normal vision)
Xᶜ Xᶜ = color-blind female (rare)
Xᶜ Y = normal male
Xᶜ Y = color-blind male

Example Cross: Normal male × Carrier female

P generation: Xᶜ Y × Xᶜ Xᶜ Results:
25% normal females
25% carrier females
25% normal males
25% color-blind males

Types:

Protanopia: red-blind (missing red cones)
Deuteranopia: green-blind (missing green cones)
Tritanopia: blue-blind (rare)

Symptoms:

Difficulty distinguishing red and green
Colors appear faded
Problems with traffic lights
Usually diagnosed in childhood

Why Sex-Linked Traits Are More Common in Males:

Males have only one X chromosome
One recessive allele on X causes expression
Females need two recessive alleles (one on each X)
Males cannot be carriers - either affected or normal
Females can be carriers without showing symptoms

WEEK 4: VARIATION AND EVOLUTION I

CONTENT
Concept of Variation

Variation refers to the differences that exist between members of the same species or between offspring and parents. No two individuals are exactly alike, even identical twins show some differences. Variation is fundamental to evolution and species adaptation.
Definition: Variation is the occurrence of differences among individuals of the same species in terms of physical appearance, physiological processes, behavioral patterns, and genetic constitution.
Significance of Variation:

Provides raw material for evolution
Enables species adaptation to changing environments
Basis for natural selection
Allows survival of species under diverse conditions
Important for selective breeding and genetic improvement
Increases biodiversity

Sources of Variation: The main sources include genetic factors (mutation, meiosis, fertilization) and environmental factors, which will be discussed in detail later.
Types of Variation

Morphological (Structural) Variation

Morphological variation refers to differences in physical structure, form, and appearance of organisms.
Examples:

Height differences in humans (short, medium, tall)
Body shape (ectomorph, mesomorph, endomorph)
Skin color and pigmentation
Eye color (brown, blue, green, hazel)
Hair texture (straight, wavy, curly, kinky)
Facial features (nose shape, lip thickness, ear size)
Fingerprint patterns (loops, whorls, arches)
Leaf shape in plants
Flower color in plants
Body size and proportions

Characteristics:

Most easily observable form of variation
Often controlled by multiple genes (polygenic)
May be influenced by environmental factors
Can be measured and quantified
Used in identification and classification

Physiological Variation

Physiological variation involves differences in biochemical and functional processes within organisms.
Examples:

Blood group (A, B, AB, O)
Metabolic rate differences
Enzyme activity levels
Hormone production rates
Ability to digest certain foods (lactose tolerance/intolerance)
Ability to taste certain substances (PTC tasting ability)
Disease resistance
Hemoglobin types (normal, sickle cell)
Ability to roll tongue
Drug metabolism rates
Body temperature regulation efficiency

Characteristics:

Not directly visible
Requires biochemical or physiological tests to detect
Often genetically determined
Less influenced by environment than morphological traits
Important in medicine and health

Discontinuous Variation

Discontinuous variation produces distinct categories with no intermediates. Individuals fall into clearly defined groups.
Characteristics:

Controlled by one or few genes
Shows clear-cut differences
Not influenced by environment
Qualitative variation
Can be placed in distinct categories
Shows simple Mendelian ratios

Examples:

ABO blood groups (A, B, AB, O - no intermediates)
Ability to roll tongue (can or cannot)
Ability to taste PTC (taster or non-taster)
Attached or free earlobes
Widow's peak (present or absent)
Sex in most organisms (male or female)
Sickle cell trait (present or absent)
Seed shape in peas (round or wrinkled)
Flower color in some plants (red or white, no pink)

Graphical Representation: When plotted on a graph, shows distinct bars (bar chart) with gaps between categories, not a smooth curve.
Continuous Variation

Continuous variation shows a range of phenotypes between two extremes with no distinct categories. Individuals are distributed along a continuum.
Characteristics:

Controlled by many genes (polygenic inheritance)
Shows gradual change from one extreme to another
Strongly influenced by environment
Quantitative variation
Can take any value within a range
Shows normal distribution (bell-shaped curve)

Examples:

Height in humans (from very short to very tall)
Weight/body mass
Skin color (from very light to very dark)
Intelligence (IQ scores)
Shoe size
Hand span
Finger length
Milk yield in cattle
Grain yield in crops
Leaf length in plants
Hair color shades

Graphical Representation: When plotted, shows a smooth bell-shaped curve (normal distribution) with most individuals near the mean and fewer at the extremes.

Causes of Variation

Mutation

Mutation is a sudden permanent change in the genetic material (DNA) of an organism that can be inherited.
Types of Mutation:

Gene Mutation (Point Mutation):

Change in DNA sequence of a single gene
Examples: Sickle cell anemia, hemophilia, albinism
Can be beneficial, harmful, or neutral
Substitution: one base replaced by another
Insertion: extra base added
Deletion: base removed

Chromosomal Mutation:

Changes in chromosome structure or number
Deletion: loss of chromosome segment
Duplication: segment repeated
Inversion: segment reversed
Translocation: segment moved to another chromosome
Aneuploidy: change in chromosome number (e.g., Down syndrome - trisomy 21)
Polyploidy: multiple complete chromosome sets

Causes of Mutation:

Errors during DNA replication
Ionizing radiation (X-rays, gamma rays, UV light)
Chemical mutagens (certain drugs, tobacco, industrial chemicals)
Biological agents (certain viruses)
Spontaneous changes

Effects of Mutation:

Source of new alleles and genetic variation
Usually harmful or neutral, rarely beneficial
Basis for evolution
Can cause genetic diseases
Some mutations are lethal
Beneficial mutations may increase survival

Examples:

Sickle cell mutation: provides malaria resistance in heterozygotes
Antibiotic resistance in bacteria
Lactose tolerance in adult humans
Albinism: lack of melanin pigment

Meiosis (Crossing Over and Independent Assortment)

Meiosis is a type of cell division that produces gametes with half the chromosome number. It introduces variation through two main mechanisms:
Crossing Over (Recombination):

Exchange of genetic material between homologous chromosomes
Occurs during prophase I of meiosis
Creates new combinations of alleles on chromosomes
Produces genetic recombination
Increases genetic diversity of gametes
The closer two genes are on a chromosome, the less likely crossing over occurs between them
Independent Assortment:

Random distribution of homologous chromosomes to daughter cells
Occurs during metaphase I and anaphase I
Each chromosome pair segregates independently
Creates 2ⁿ possible combinations (n = number of chromosome pairs)
In humans: 2²³ = 8,388,608 possible gamete combinations
Different from Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment which refers to genes

Result:

Each gamete has a unique combination of genes
Siblings (except identical twins) are genetically different
Greatly increases genetic variation in sexually reproducing organisms

Fertilization (Random Fusion of Gametes)
Fertilization is the union of male and female gametes to form a zygote.

How It Causes Variation:

Random combination of gametes from two parents
Any sperm can fertilize any egg
Each parent contributes half the genetic material
Creates unique gene combinations in offspring
In humans: 2²³ × 2²³ = 70 trillion possible combinations
No two offspring (except identical twins) are genetically identical

Process:

Sperm carries paternal chromosomes and genes
Egg carries maternal chromosomes and genes
Fertilization restores diploid number
Offspring inherits mixed genetic information
Combination is completely random

Significance:

Ensures genetic diversity in populations
Each child is unique (except identical twins)
Basis for family resemblance with individual differences
Important for species adaptation and survival

Environmental Factors

Environmental factors can influence the expression of genetic traits, causing phenotypic variation even with identical genotypes.
Environmental Factors Include:

Nutrition:

Affects growth, development, and health
Examples: Stunted growth from malnutrition; obesity from overnutrition
Well-nourished individuals may grow taller than genetic potential in malnourished populations
Affects coat color in some animals

Climate and Temperature:

Affects body size (Bergmann's rule: larger bodies in colder climates)
Influences plant growth and flowering
Himalayan rabbits: darker fur in colder body parts
Affects metabolic rates

Light:

Affects plant growth and chlorophyll production
Etiolation in plants grown in darkness (pale, elongated stems)
Affects flowering in photoperiodic plants
Influences skin pigmentation in humans (tanning)

Disease and Parasites:

Can affect growth and development
May cause permanent physical changes
Examples: Stunted growth from childhood diseases
Affects overall health and vigor

Physical Factors:

Exercise affects muscle development
Wind affects plant shape (trees bent by prevailing winds)
Water availability affects plant size
Altitude affects body adaptations

Social and Cultural Factors:
Education and upbringing affect behavior
Language and accent
Cultural practices affect appearance (body modifications)

Important Note:

Environmental variations are NOT inherited
Only genetic variations can be passed to offspring
Acquired characteristics are not inherited (contrary to Lamarck's theory)
Example: Bodybuilder's muscles not inherited by children
Adaptive Coloration in Animals

Adaptive coloration refers to color patterns and markings that help animals survive in their environment. It is an evolutionary adaptation that enhances survival and reproduction.
Types of Adaptive Coloration:

Camouflage (Cryptic Coloration)

Camouflage is coloration that helps an organism blend with its environment, making it difficult to detect.
Mechanisms:

Body color matches background
Pattern disrupts outline
Reduces visibility to predators or prey
May change with seasons or environment

Examples:

Chameleons: Change color to match surroundings
Stick insects: Resemble twigs and branches
Leaf insects: Look exactly like leaves
Flatfish (flounder): Match ocean floor patterns
Arctic hare: White in winter, brown in summer
Green grasshoppers: Blend with grass
Moths: Bark-like patterns when resting on trees
Tiger stripes: Disruptive pattern in tall grass
Leopard spots: Break up outline in dappled forest light

Functions:

Protection from predators (prey animals)
Concealment while hunting (predators)
Increases survival rate
Allows close approach to prey

Warning Coloration (Aposematic Coloration)

Bright, conspicuous colors that advertise danger, toxicity, or unpalatability to potential predators.

Characteristics:

Bright, contrasting colors (red, yellow, black, white)
Bold patterns
Easily visible and memorable
Honest signal of danger

Examples:

Poison dart frogs: Bright blue, red, or yellow colors signal toxicity
Monarch butterfly: Orange and black pattern; contains cardiac glycosides from milkweed
Coral snakes: Red, yellow, and black bands; venomous
Wasps and bees: Black and yellow stripes; can sting
Ladybugs: Red with black spots; distasteful
Skunks: Black and white pattern; spray defense
Some caterpillars: Bright colors indicate toxicity or irritating hairs

Functions:

Warns predators of danger
Reduces predation attempts
Predators learn to avoid brightly colored prey
More effective if several species share similar patterns (Müllerian mimicry)

Mimicry

Mimicry is the resemblance of one organism (mimic) to another organism (model) or to a natural object, providing survival advantage.
Types of Mimicry:

Batesian Mimicry:

Harmless species mimics a harmful or distasteful species
Mimic gains protection without being dangerous
Named after Henry Walter Bates

Examples:

Hoverflies: Mimic wasps and bees but cannot sting
King snake: Mimics venomous coral snake but is harmless
Viceroy butterfly: Mimics toxic monarch butterfly
Some harmless snakes: Mimic color patterns of venomous species

Requirements:

Model must be more numerous than mimic
Model must be truly dangerous or distasteful
Predators must have learned to avoid the model

Müllerian Mimicry:
Two or more harmful species resemble each other
All species benefit from shared warning pattern
Predators learn faster with consistent signals
Named after Fritz Müller

Examples:

Several species of poisonous butterflies share similar color patterns
Different species of stinging wasps have similar black and yellow patterns
Various toxic amphibians share similar bright warning colors

Aggressive Mimicry:

Predator or parasite mimics harmless species to get close to prey
Benefits predator, harms prey

Examples:

Anglerfish: Lure resembles small prey to attract fish
Alligator snapping turtle: Tongue resembles worm to lure fish
Some orchids: Mimic female insects to attract males for pollination

Self-Mimicry:

One part of body resembles another important part
Deflects attacks to less vital areas

Examples:

Butterfly eyespots: False eyes on wings deflect attacks from head
Some fish: False eyespot near tail makes predator attack wrong end

Adaptive Significance:

Reduces predation pressure
Increases survival rate
Allows coexistence of species
Drives evolutionary arms race between predators and prey
Demonstrates natural selection in action

Conditions for Effective Mimicry:

Mimic must closely resemble model in appearance
Mimic and model must share same habitat
Predators must be common to both species
Predators must be capable of learning

WEEK 6: EVOLUTION II

CONTENT
Meaning of Evolution

Evolution is the gradual change in the inherited characteristics of populations of organisms over successive generations, leading to the development of new species from pre-existing ones. It is the unifying principle of biology that explains the diversity of life on Earth.
Key Concepts:

Evolution occurs in populations, not individuals
Changes accumulate over long periods (thousands to millions of years)
All life forms share common ancestry
Evolution is ongoing and continuous
Results in adaptation to environment
Accounts for both similarities and differences among organisms

Levels of Evolution:

Microevolution: Small-scale changes within species (e.g., antibiotic resistance in bacteria)
Macroevolution: Large-scale changes leading to new species (speciation)

Evolution vs. Individual Development:

Evolution involves genetic changes in populations
Individual development (ontogeny) involves growth from embryo to adult
Individuals do not evolve; populations evolve
Evolutionary changes are heritable; developmental changes are not inherited
Theories of Evolution

Lamarckism (Theory of Inheritance of Acquired Characteristics)
Proposed by Jean-Baptiste Lamarck (1744-1829), a French naturalist, in his book Philosophie Zoologique (1809).
Main Principles:

Use and Disuse:

Organs that are used frequently become stronger and larger
Organs that are not used become weaker and smaller, eventually disappearing
Example: Lamarck suggested giraffes stretched their necks to reach high leaves, and this stretching made their necks longer
Inheritance of Acquired Characteristics:

Characteristics acquired during an organism's lifetime can be passed to offspring
Changes induced by environment or behavior are inherited
Example: A bodybuilder's developed muscles would be inherited by their children

Lamarck's Explanation of Giraffe Evolution:

Ancestral giraffes had short necks
They stretched their necks to reach high leaves
Stretching made their necks slightly longer
This acquired longer neck was passed to offspring
Each generation stretched and passed on longer necks
Over many generations, modern long-necked giraffes evolved

Weaknesses of Lamarckism:

Acquired characteristics are NOT inherited (disproved by genetics)
August Weismann's experiment: Cut tails of mice for 22 generations; offspring still had tails
No genetic mechanism for transmitting acquired traits
Changes in somatic (body) cells don't affect gametes (sex cells)
Modern molecular biology shows DNA changes occur randomly, not in response to need
Contributions:

First comprehensive theory of evolution
Recognized that organisms change over time
Acknowledged environmental influence on organisms
Stimulated scientific debate about evolution

Darwinism (Theory of Natural Selection)

Proposed by Charles Darwin (1809-1882), an English naturalist, in his book On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection (1859).
Darwin's theory was developed after his five-year voyage (1831-1836) on HMS Beagle, particularly his observations in the Galápagos Islands.
Darwin's Observations:

Overproduction:

Organisms produce more offspring than can possibly survive
Example: A single cod fish produces millions of eggs
If all survived, the oceans would be filled with cod
Struggle for Existence:

Limited resources (food, water, space, mates)
Competition within and between species
Predation, disease, environmental challenges
Only a fraction of offspring survive to reproduce
Variation:

Individuals within a species show variation
No two individuals are exactly alike
Variations are inherited
Some variations are more advantageous than others
Survival of the Fittest (Natural Selection):
Individuals with advantageous traits are more likely to survive
Survivors reproduce and pass favorable traits to offspring
Unfavorable traits are eliminated
"Fitness" means reproductive success, not physical strength
Adaptation and Evolution:

Over generations, favorable traits accumulate
Population gradually changes
New species eventually form
Organisms become better adapted to their environment

Darwin's Explanation of Giraffe Evolution:

Ancestral population had giraffes with varying neck lengths (variation)
During food shortages, longer-necked individuals could reach more food (struggle for existence)
Longer-necked giraffes survived better and reproduced more (natural selection)
Offspring inherited genes for longer necks
Over many generations, average neck length increased
Eventually, modern long-necked giraffes predominated

Key Principles of Natural Selection:

Variation: Individuals differ
Heredity: Traits are inherited
Differential survival: Some individuals survive better
Differential reproduction: Survivors have more offspring
Accumulation: Favorable traits increase in frequency

Examples of Natural Selection:

Peppered Moths (Biston betularia) in Industrial England:
Before industrialization: Light-colored moths camouflaged on light tree bark; dark moths easily seen and eaten
During industrialization: Soot darkened tree bark; dark moths now camouflaged; light moths conspicuous
Result: Dark moth frequency increased dramatically
After pollution control: Light moths became common again
Demonstrates evolution in action (microevolution)

Antibiotic Resistance in Bacteria:

Random mutations make some bacteria resistant
Antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria
Resistant bacteria survive and multiply
Population becomes predominantly resistant
Happens within human lifespan (rapid evolution)

Darwin's Finches:

13 species of finches on Galápagos Islands
Descended from common ancestor
Different beak shapes adapted to different food sources
Seed-eaters: thick, strong beaks
Insect-eaters: thin, pointed beaks
Demonstrates adaptive radiation

Weaknesses of Original Darwinism:

Darwin couldn't explain source of variation (genetics unknown in his time)
Didn't understand mechanism of inheritance
Thought inheritance was "blending" (now known to be particulate)
Couldn't explain how favorable traits persist

Contributions:
Provided naturalistic explanation for evolution
Supported by extensive evidence
Mechanism (natural selection) is testable
Foundation of modern evolutionary biology
Modern Synthetic Theory of Evolution (Neo-Darwinism)

Developed in the 1930s-1940s by synthesizing Darwin's natural selection with Mendelian genetics, molecular biology, and population genetics.
Key Contributors:

Theodosius Dobzhansky
Ernst Mayr
George Gaylord Simpson
Julian Huxley

Main Principles:

Genetic Variation:

Variation arises from:
Mutations (ultimate source of new alleles)
Sexual reproduction (recombination)
Gene flow (migration between populations)
Provides raw material for evolution

Natural Selection:

Acts on phenotypes (expressed traits)
Differential reproductive success
Changes allele frequencies in populations
Multiple types:
Directional selection (favors one extreme)
Stabilizing selection (favors average)
Disruptive selection (favors both extremes)

Genetic Drift:

Random changes in allele frequencies
Significant in small populations
Independent of fitness
Examples: Bottleneck effect, founder effect

Gene Flow:

Movement of genes between populations
Through migration and interbreeding
Increases genetic variation
Can introduce new alleles

Reproductive Isolation:

Essential for speciation
Prevents interbreeding between populations
Types:
Prezygotic barriers (prevent mating)
Postzygotic barriers (offspring inviable or sterile)

Population is Unit of Evolution:

Evolution occurs in populations, not individuals
Gene pool changes over time
Allele frequencies shift

Modern Synthesis Explanation of Giraffe Evolution:

Random mutations produced variation in neck-length genes
Sexual reproduction created diverse combinations
During environmental pressure (food shortage), longer-necked individuals had survival advantage
Natural selection favored longer-neck alleles
Over generations, frequency of longer-neck alleles increased in gene pool
Reproductive isolation from short-necked populations
Eventually, distinct long-necked species emerged

Advances Over Darwin:

Explains source of variation (mutation)
Describes mechanism of inheritance (genes)
Quantifies evolution (population genetics)
Incorporates molecular biology (DNA)
Explains rapid and gradual evolution
Accounts for genetic drift and gene flow

Evidence Supporting Modern Synthesis:

DNA sequencing shows genetic relationships
Observed evolution in laboratory and nature
Fossil record documents transitions
Mathematical models predict allele frequency changes
Comparative genomics reveals evolutionary history
Evidence of Evolution

Fossil Records

Fossils are preserved remains, impressions, or traces of organisms that lived in the past, typically found in sedimentary rocks.
Formation of Fossils:

Organism dies and is quickly buried in sediment
Soft parts decay; hard parts (bones, shells) remain
Minerals replace organic material (petrification)
Sediment hardens into rock over millions of years
Geological processes may expose fossils

Types of Fossils:

Petrified fossils: Mineralized remains
Molds and casts: Impressions in rock
Trace fossils: Footprints, burrows, coprolites
Amber fossils: Organisms preserved in tree resin
Frozen fossils: Preserved in ice (woolly mammoths)
Tar pit fossils: Preserved in natural tar

What Fossils Reveal:

Chronological sequence: Older fossils in deeper layers
Transitional forms: Organisms with intermediate features
Extinct species: Most species that ever lived are extinct
Change over time: Species characteristics change through geological periods
Common ancestry: Similar structures in related organisms

Examples:

Archaeopteryx (Transitional Fossil):

Lived about 150 million years ago
Features of both reptiles and birds:
Reptilian: Teeth, clawed wings, long bony tail
Avian: Feathers, wishbone, wings
Evidence of reptile-to-bird evolution

Horse Evolution Series:

Traced from Eohippus (Hyracotherium) to modern Equus
Shows gradual changes:
Size increase: Dog-sized to modern horse
Toe reduction: Four toes to one hoe (hoof)
Tooth changes: Small teeth to large grinding molars
Leg lengthening for running
Demonstrates gradual evolution over 55 million years

Whale Evolution:

Land-dwelling ancestors (Pakicetus) to aquatic whales
Transitional forms show:
Gradual leg reduction
Tail fluke development
Nostril movement to top of head (blowhole)
Fossils include Ambulocetus (walking whale) and Basilosaurus

Limitations of Fossil Record:

Incomplete (fossilization is rare)
Soft-bodied organisms rarely fossilize
Gaps due to geological processes
Dating fossils can be challenging
Environmental bias (aquatic organisms fossilize better)

Comparative Anatomy

Study of similarities and differences in anatomy of different species reveals evolutionary relationships.
Homologous Structures:

Structures with same basic structure and embryonic origin but different functions
Indicate common ancestry
Result of divergent evolution
Examples:

Pentadactyl limb: Five-digit limb structure in vertebrates

Human arm: Grasping and manipulation
Whale flipper: Swimming
Bird wing: Flying
Horse leg: Running
Bat wing: Flying
All have same bone pattern: humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals, phalanges
Other examples:

Vertebrate hearts (varying chambers but same origin)
Vertebrate brains (different sizes but similar structure)

Significance: Same structure adapted for different functions shows modification from common ancestor
Analogous Structures:

Structures with different basic structure and origin but same function
Result of convergent evolution
Do NOT indicate close evolutionary relationship

Examples:

Wings:

Insect wing: Membrane supported by veins, no bones
Bird wing: Modified forelimb with feathers and bones
Bat wing: Skin membrane stretched between elongated fingers
All function in flight but have different structures
Eyes:
Vertebrate eye and octopus eye function similarly
Different embryonic origin and structure
Example of convergent evolution
Other examples:

Shark (fish) and dolphin (mammal) body shapes
Cactus (New World) and euphorbia (African) adaptations to desert

Significance: Similar functions in similar environments lead to similar adaptations despite different ancestry
Vestigial Structures:

Reduced or non-functional structures
Were functional in ancestors
Retained through evolution but lost function
Evidence that organisms have changed over time

Examples in Humans:

Appendix: Reduced cecum; was larger in herbivorous ancestors
Wisdom teeth: Third molars; useful when diet was rougher
Coccyx (tailbone): Remnant of tail in ancestors
Ear muscles: For moving ears; functional in many mammals
Body hair: Sparse compared to ancestors
Goosebumps: Erects body hair (useful for warmth/intimidation in furry ancestors)

Examples in Other Organisms:

Whale pelvic bones: Remnants of hind limbs from land ancestors
Snake pelvic girdle: Traces of legs in some species
Flightless bird wings: Ostriches, penguins have reduced wings
Cave-dwelling fish eyes: Blind fish have non-functional eyes
Human embryo tail: Temporary structure during development
Significance: Presence of vestigial structures indicates evolutionary change from ancestors

Comparative Embryology

Study of similarities in embryonic development across different species.

Key Observations:

Vertebrate embryos show remarkable similarities in early stages
More closely related species show greater embryonic similarity
Embryos exhibit features of evolutionary ancestors
Development recapitulates evolutionary history (with exceptions)

Examples:

Vertebrate Embryonic Similarities: All vertebrate embryos show:

Gill slits/pharyngeal pouches: Present in fish, amphibian, reptile, bird, and mammal embryos
In fish: Develop into functional gills
In land vertebrates: Modified into other structures (ear parts, tonsils)
Tail: All vertebrate embryos have tails
Retained in most vertebrates
Reduced to coccyx in humans
Similar body structure: Early embryos of fish, chicken, pig, and human look very similar
Developmental Genes (Hox Genes):

Highly conserved across species
Control body plan development
Same genes in insects, fish, humans
Evidence of common ancestry at molecular level

Significance:
Similarities indicate common ancestry
Embryos preserve evolutionary history
Explains presence of transient structures
Supports idea that complex organisms evolved from simpler forms

Biogenetic Law (Recapitulation Theory):

Proposed by Ernst Haeckel: "Ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny"
Embryonic development repeats evolutionary history
Partially true but oversimplified
Embryos show ancestral features, not adult ancestral forms

Comparative Biochemistry and Molecular Biology

Comparing DNA, proteins, and other molecules reveals evolutionary relationships.

DNA Sequence Comparison:

More similar DNA = more closely related
Can quantify genetic distance between species
Universal genetic code indicates common origin of life

Examples:

Humans and chimpanzees: 98-99% DNA similarity
Humans and gorillas: ~98% similarity
Humans and mice: ~85% similarity
Humans and fruit flies: ~60% of genes have human equivalents

Protein Comparison:

Cytochrome c: Respiratory protein found in all aerobic organisms

Human and chimpanzee: Identical
Human and rhesus monkey: 1 amino acid difference
Human and horse: 12 amino acid differences
Human and yeast: 44 amino acid differences
Hemoglobin: Oxygen-carrying protein

Shows similar patterns of similarity

DNA Hybridization:

Technique to measure DNA similarity
More heat required to separate similar DNA strands
Quantifies evolutionary distance

Amino Acid Sequencing:

Comparing protein sequences
Fewer differences = closer relationship
Molecular clock: Mutations accumulate at relatively constant rate

Significance:

Provides quantitative measure of relatedness
Supports relationships determined by anatomy
Can compare organisms with no fossil record
Reveals common ancestry at molecular level
Used to construct phylogenetic trees

Biogeography

Study of geographical distribution of organisms provides evidence for evolution.

Key Observations:

Island Species:

Islands have unique species found nowhere else (endemic species)
Island species resemble nearest mainland species
Indicates colonization and subsequent evolution in isolation
Examples: Galápagos finches, Hawaiian honeycreepers, Madagascar lemurs

Continental Drift:

Similar fossils on now-separated continents
Example: Identical fossils in South America and Africa
Explains distribution of related species
Supports idea of common ancestors before continental separation

Isolated Ecosystems:

Australia: Unique marsupials evolved in isolation
Madagascar: Lemurs found nowhere else
Geographic isolation leads to unique evolutionary paths

Distribution Patterns:

Related species in adjacent areas
Environmental similarity leads to similar adaptations
Barriers (mountains, oceans) create distinct populations

Significance:

Explains why certain species are found where they are
Geographic isolation drives speciation
Similar environments produce similar adaptations
Continental movement influenced evolutionary patterns
Natural Selection and Adaptation

Natural Selection Mechanisms:

Directional Selection:

Favors one extreme phenotype
Average shifts in one direction
Example: Giraffe neck lengthening, antibiotic resistance

Stabilizing Selection:

Favors intermediate phenotypes
Eliminates extremes
Example: Human birth weight (very small or large babies have lower survival)

Disruptive Selection:

Favors both extreme phenotypes
Eliminates intermediates
Can lead to speciation
Example: Darwin's finches with either large or small beaks

Adaptation: Adaptation is an inherited characteristic that enhances an organism's ability to survive and reproduce in its environment.
Types of Adaptation:

Structural: Physical features (giraffe neck, bird beak)
Physiological: Internal processes (snake venom, antifreeze proteins in Arctic fish)
Behavioral: Actions (migration, hibernation, courtship)

Examples:

Desert plants: Water storage, reduced leaves (cacti)
Polar bears: Thick fur, white coloration, fat layer
Cheetahs: Streamlined body, large heart, non-retractable claws
Camels: Hump (fat storage), ability to drink large amounts, broad feet

Adaptation vs. Acclimatization:

Adaptation: Genetic, inherited, takes many generations
Acclimatization: Physiological adjustment during lifetime, not inherited
Example: Genetic adaptation to high altitude vs. individual adjustment when visiting mountains

Human evolution is the lengthy process of change by which modern humans (Homo sapiens) evolved from ape-like ancestors. This process occurred over approximately 6-7 million years and involves both biological and cultural evolution.
Key Concepts:

Humans belong to the Order Primates, Family Hominidae (great apes)
Humans did not evolve from modern apes but share common ancestors
Human evolution was not linear but involved multiple human species existing simultaneously
Evolution occurred primarily in Africa
Both biological and cultural factors shaped human evolution

Hominid vs. Hominin:

Hominid: Includes all great apes (humans, chimpanzees, gorillas, orangutans)

Modern classification focuses on hominins

Blood group B: IᴮIᴮ or Iᴮi
