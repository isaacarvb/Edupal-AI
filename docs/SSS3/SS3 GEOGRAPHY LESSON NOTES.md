WEEK 1: DENUDATIONAL PROCESSES

MEANING OF DENUDATION

Denudation is a comprehensive term used in geomorphology to describe all the processes that wear away, strip bare, or lower the land surface of the Earth's crust. It is the sum total of processes that cause the disintegration and transportation of rock and soil. Denudation plays a crucial role in landscape development, continuously re-shaping the Earth's topography.

The term is derived from the Latin word denudare, meaning "to strip bare."

COMPONENTS OF DENUDATION

Denudation is not a single process, but an umbrella term that includes three main components operating in sequence:

Weathering: The in-situ (in place) disintegration (breaking apart) or decomposition (chemical decay) of rocks at or near the Earth's surface. It does not involve movement.
Erosion: The removal and transportation of weathered material (like soil, sand, and rock fragments) from one place to another. This process requires a mobile agent.
Mass Wasting (Mass Movement): The downslope movement of a large mass of rock or soil primarily under the influence of gravity. It is a form of transport, but the agent is gravity itself.

The full process involves Transportation (the movement of the eroded material) and
Deposition (the laying down of the material when the agent loses energy).

Running Water (Fluvial Processes)

This is the most widespread and dominant agent of denudation, especially in humid regions like Southern Nigeria.

Erosional Processes:
Hydraulic Action: The sheer force of the moving water hitting the river bed and banks, dislodging material.
Abrasion (or Corrasion): The "sandpaper" effect. The river uses its load (the rocks and sand it is already carrying) to scrape and grind against the bed and banks.
Attrition: Rocks and pebbles within the river's load collide with each other, breaking down into smaller, smoother, and more rounded particles.
Solution (or Corrosion): The chemical action of the river water dissolving minerals (like limestone) from the rocks.
Landforms Created:
Erosional: V-shaped valleys, waterfalls, rapids, potholes, meanders, river cliffs, gorges.
Depositional: Floodplains, levees, deltas (e.g., the Niger Delta), ox-bow lakes, alluvial fans.

Wind (Aeolian Processes)

This is the dominant agent in arid and semi-arid regions (e.g., Northern Nigeria, the Sahel).

Erosional Processes:
Deflation: The process of wind "lifting" and blowing away loose, unconsolidated materials (dust and sand), leaving behind a stony surface (desert pavement).
Abrasion: Wind-blown sand particles sandblast rock surfaces, carving them into new shapes.
Landforms Created:
Erosional: Rock pedestals (a mushroom-shaped rock), Zeugens, Yardangs, Deflation hollows.
Depositional: Sand dunes (barchans, seifs), Loess (fine dust deposited far away).

Moving Ice (Glaciers)
This is dominant in high-latitude (polar) and high-altitude (mountain) regions.

Erosional Processes:
Plucking: The glacier freezes onto rock, and as it moves, it "plucks" large blocks of rock from the valley floor.
Abrasion: The glacier uses the rocks embedded in its base to scrape and polish the valley floor, leaving striations (scratches).
Landforms Created:
Erosional: U-shaped valleys, hanging valleys, cirques (armchair-shaped hollows), arêtes (sharp ridges), fjords (drowned glacial valleys).
Depositional: Moraines (ridges of glacial debris), drumlins, outwash plains.

Waves (Marine Processes)

This action is confined to coastlines (e.g., the Lagos and Calabar coast).

Erosional Processes:
Hydraulic Action: The sheer force of waves crashing against a cliff, compressing air in cracks.
Abrasion: Waves hurling shingle and sand against the cliff face.
Landforms Created:
Erosional: Cliffs, wave-cut platforms, headlands, bays, sea caves, sea arches, sea stacks (e.g., the "Bar Beach" erosion).
Depositional: Beaches, spits (e.g., Lagos Bar Beach), bars, tombolos.

CLIMATIC AND VEGETATION INFLUENCES

Climate: Climate is the single most important factor.
Hot, Wet (Humid) Climate (e.g., Southern Nigeria): Dominant process is Chemical Weathering. Heavy rainfall creates powerful rivers, making fluvial erosion the main denudation agent.
Hot, Dry (Arid) Climate (e.g., Northern Nigeria): Dominant process is Mechanical Weathering (e.g., exfoliation). Lack of vegetation and water means wind erosion is the main agent.
Cold, (Peri)Glacial Climate: Dominant process is Mechanical Weathering
(frost action) and glacial erosion.
Vegetation:
Protective Role: Vegetation (especially forests) is a barrier to denudation.
Plant roots bind the soil together, preventing erosion.
The tree canopy intercepts heavy rain (reducing "splash erosion").
It acts as a windbreak, reducing wind erosion.
Destructive Role: Plant roots can grow into cracks, widening them (biological weathering).

HUMAN ACTIVITIES THAT ACCELERATE DENUDATION

Human activities often remove the protective vegetation cover, dramatically accelerating the rate of denudation and leading to environmental degradation.

Deforestation: Cutting down forests for timber, fuel, or farmland. This exposes the bare soil to rain and wind.
Improper Agricultural Practices:
Overgrazing: Allowing too many animals (cattle, goats) to eat all the grass, leaving the soil bare.
Ploughing along slopes: This creates channels for water to flow, which quickly turn into massive gullies. (e.g., The Agulu-Nanka gully erosion in Anambra State).
Mining and Quarrying: Excavation and digging expose loose rock and soil to erosion.
Construction: Clearing land for roads, buildings, and cities alters natural slopes, compacts the soil, and creates artificial channels for water, increasing runoff and erosion.

CONSERVATION AND MANAGEMENT

Afforestation/Reforestation: Planting trees to stabilize soil.
Contour Ploughing: Ploughing across a slope (not down) to create small "dams" that trap water.
Terracing: Building "steps" into a steep hillside to create flat areas for farming.
Check Dams: Building small dams inside gullies to slow the water and trap sediment.
Cover Cropping: Planting crops (like Pueraria) that cover the soil and protect it.

WEEK 2: WEATHERING

MEANING OF WEATHERING

Weathering is the in-situ (in place) breakdown, disintegration (physical breaking), or decomposition (chemical rotting) of rocks and minerals at or near the Earth's surface.

It is a static process—it does not involve any movement or transportation of the broken material. It is the "preparation" phase for erosion.

TYPES OF WEATHERING

MECHANICAL (PHYSICAL) WEATHERING

This involves the breakdown of rocks into smaller pieces (known as regolith) without any change in their chemical or mineral composition. It is dominant in hot, arid (desert) or very cold (polar) climates.

Processes in Mechanical Weathering:

Insolation Weathering (Thermal Expansion / Exfoliation):
Process: In hot deserts, rocks are non-conductors. During the day, the outer surface of the rock heats up and expands. At night, the temperature plummets, and the outer surface cools and contracts. This repeated expansion and contraction creates stress, causing the outer layers to crack and "peel" off like an onion skin. This specific peeling is called exfoliation.
Example: A common process on large, exposed rock domes (inselbergs) like
Zuma Rock (Niger) or Olumo Rock (Ogun).

Frost Action (Freeze-Thaw):
Process: Dominant in cold, mountainous regions.
Water seeps into cracks (joints) in a rock.
At night, the water freezes and expands by about 9-10% in volume.
This expansion acts like a powerful wedge, widening the crack.
Repeated cycles of freezing and thawing cause the rock to shatter.
Result: A field of sharp, angular rocks called "scree" at the base of a slope.

Salt Crystal Growth:
Process: In arid or coastal areas, water containing dissolved salts seeps into the pores of rocks. The water evaporates, leaving the salt crystals behind. These crystals grow over time, exerting pressure on the rock pores and causing the rock to disintegrate.
Pressure Release (Unloading):
Process: Deeply buried rocks (like granite) are under immense pressure. When erosion removes the thousands of tonnes of rock above them, the "pressure is released." This causes the rock to expand, crack, and break apart in large sheets.

CHEMICAL WEATHERING

This involves the decomposition of rocks through chemical reactions that change the minerals. It is dominant in hot, humid (wet) climates (like Southern Nigeria) because:

Water (the main agent) is abundant.
Heat (Van't Hoff's rule) speeds up the rate of all chemical reactions.

Processes in Chemical Weathering:

Solution: The process where water (a universal solvent) dissolves certain minerals.
Example: Rock salt (halite) or gypsum dissolve completely.
Carbonation:
Process: Rainwater (H₂O) dissolves carbon dioxide (CO₂) from the air, forming a weak carbonic acid (H₂CO₃).
This acid is very effective at dissolving rocks that contain calcium carbonate, such as limestone and chalk.
Result: This process creates "karst" landscapes, including limestone pavements, caves, sinkholes, stalactites, and stalagmites.

Hydrolysis:
Process: A chemical reaction between water (specifically its H+ and OH- ions) and minerals in the rock. This is the most important weathering process for silicate rocks (like granite).
Example: Hydrolysis breaks down Feldspar (a hard mineral in granite) and converts it into Kaolin (soft clay). This weakens the entire rock, allowing it to crumble.
Oxidation:
Process: The reaction of minerals (especially iron) with oxygen, usually in the presence of water. It is "rusting."
Example: Iron compounds in a rock "rust," turning the rock a reddish-brown colour and making it crumble. This is what gives laterite soils their characteristic red colour.
Hydration: The absorption of water into a mineral's structure, causing it to expand and weaken (e.g., anhydrite + water  gypsum).

BIOLOGICAL WEATHERING

This involves the breakdown of rocks by living organisms.

Root Action (Mechanical): Plant roots (especially tree roots) grow into cracks in rocks. As the root thickens, it acts as a powerful wedge, forcing the rock apart.

Burrowing Animals: Animals like rabbits, rodents, ants, and termites burrow into the ground, moving material and exposing fresh rock surfaces to other forms of weathering.
Chemical Action:
Lichen and Moss: These organisms grow on bare rock. They produce weak organic acids (chelation) that slowly dissolve the minerals, breaking down the rock surface.
Man: Human activities like mining, quarrying, and construction are a major (and very fast) form of biological weathering.

FACTORS AFFECTING WEATHERING

Climate (The Most Important):
Hot/Wet (Humid Tropical): High temperatures and high rainfall = Deep, rapid Chemical Weathering (e.g., hydrolysis, oxidation).
Hot/Dry (Arid): High temperatures, low rainfall = Strong Mechanical Weathering (e.g., exfoliation).
Cold/Wet (Temperate/Polar): Cold, fluctuating temperatures = Strong Mechanical Weathering (e.g., frost action).
Rock Type (Geology):
Rock Structure: Rocks with many joints (cracks) or layers will weather faster.
Mineral Composition: Rocks with "soft" or "reactive" minerals (like limestone, which dissolves) will weather faster than rocks with "hard" or "resistant" minerals (like quartz).
Topography (Relief):
Steep Slopes: Weathered material (regolith) is removed quickly by gravity, exposing fresh rock to more weathering.
Gentle Slopes: Allow water to collect and regolith to accumulate, leading to deeper chemical weathering.
Vegetation: Vegetation (as seen in biological weathering) can both protect the rock (by covering it) and break it apart (with roots and acids).
Time: The longer a rock is exposed to the elements, the more it will weather.

WEATHERING AND SOIL FORMATION

Weathering is the first and most essential step in creating soil.

Role: Weathering breaks down the parent rock (the "bedrock") into smaller mineral particles called regolith.
This regolith (which is a mix of sand, silt, and clay) forms the inorganic base of the soil.
When this regolith is mixed with organic matter (decaying plants and animals), air, and
water, it becomes fertile soil.
Mechanical Weathering  produces sandy, coarse soils.
Chemical Weathering  produces fine-grained, clay-rich soils.

WEEK 3: MASS MOVEMENTS

DEFINITION OF MASS MOVEMENTS (MASS WASTING)

Mass Movement (or Mass Wasting) is the downslope movement of a large mass of rock, soil, and debris (called regolith) under the direct influence of gravity.

Key Distinction: Unlike erosion, mass movement does not require a "transporting agent" like running water, wind, or ice. Gravity alone is the primary force, though water often acts as a trigger by lubricating the material.

CATEGORIES AND TYPES OF MASS MOVEMENTS

Mass movements are classified by their speed, material, and the type of motion.

SLOW MOVEMENTS
CREEP (Soil Creep):
Description: The slowest (imperceptible) and most widespread form of mass movement. It is the gradual, downhill movement of soil and regolith.
Process: Caused by cycles of wetting/drying or freezing/thawing, which cause soil particles to lift and then resettle slightly downhill.

Evidence (You can't see it happen, but you see its effects): Tilted trees ("J-shaped" trunks), bent fences, tilted utility poles, and cracks in roads.

o
SOLIFLUCTION:
Description: A specific type of creep in cold, sub-polar regions.
Process: In summer, the top layer of frozen ground (permafrost) thaws, but the water cannot drain because of the frozen ice below. The waterlogged, thawed layer becomes a "mush" and flows slowly downslope.

FAST MOVEMENTS

FLOWS: The material moves like a viscous fluid.
Earthflow: A mass of earth (soil) flows slowly downslope, often leaving a "tongue-shaped" lobe.
Mudflow: A rapid (fast) flow of mud and water, common in arid regions after a sudden, intense rainstorm. They follow river channels.
Debris Flow / Debris Avalanche: A very fast, destructive flow containing a mix of water, mud, rocks, and trees.
SLIDES: The material moves as a coherent block along a defined flat surface (a "slip plane").
Rockslide: A block of bedrock detaches and slides rapidly down a flat joint or bedding plane.
Debris Slide: A slide of unconsolidated soil and rock fragments.
SLUMPS (Rotational Slides):
Description: A common type of slide where a mass of soil or rock moves
downward and outward along a curved (spoon-shaped) slip surface.
Result: The top of the slump "rotates" backward, leaving a crescent-shaped scarp (cliff) at the top and a lumpy "toe" at the bottom.

FALLS:
Description: The fastest type of mass movement. It is the free-fall of detached rock fragments from a steep cliff.
Rockfall: Individual rocks fall and accumulate at the bottom as a "talus" or "scree" slope.

FACTORS CONTRIBUTING TO MASS MOVEMENTS

These factors can be pre-conditioning (what makes the slope unstable) or triggering (the specific event that starts the movement).

PHYSICAL FACTORS

Slope Gradient (Steepness): This is the main factor. Gravity has a stronger "pull" on steeper slopes.
Water Content (The #1 Trigger): Water is the primary trigger.
It lubricates the soil particles, reducing friction.
It adds weight to the slope.
It increases pore water pressure, which pushes soil particles apart and "floats" the slope, drastically reducing its strength.
Rock Type and Structure:
Rock Type: Weak, unconsolidated materials (like clay, shale, sand) are far more likely to fail than hard, crystalline rock (like granite).
Rock Structure: Layers (bedding planes) that dip parallel to the slope, or the presence of joints and faults, create "planes of weakness" that are prone to sliding.
Vegetation Cover (Lack of): Plant roots (especially from trees) anchor the soil, holding it together. Removing vegetation makes a slope much more unstable.
Natural Triggers:
Earthquakes: The shaking can instantly trigger massive landslides and rockfalls.
Volcanic Eruptions: Can melt ice and create mudflows (lahars).

HUMAN ACTIVITIES (TRIGGERS / EXACERBATION)

Human activity is a major cause of slope failure.

Deforestation: As discussed in Week 1, clearing forests on slopes for farming or logging is a primary cause of landslides.
Construction and Excavation:
Road Cuts: Cutting into the "toe" (bottom) of a slope to build a road removes its support, often causing the material above to slide.
Loading: Piling excavated material on the top of a slope, or building a heavy house, adds weight and increases the driving force.
Mining and Quarrying: Blasting and excavation create steep, unstable slopes.
Water Leakage: Water leaking from irrigation ditches, broken pipes, or septic tanks can saturate a slope, triggering a failure.

IMPACTS OF MASS MOVEMENTS

Social: Loss of life and property; displacement of communities.
Economic: Destruction of infrastructure (roads, bridges, buildings), loss of valuable farmland, high cost of repair and mitigation.
Environmental: Clogging of rivers with sediment (damaging aquatic life), scarring of the landscape, creation of "landslide dams" (which can later burst and cause flash floods).

CONTROL AND PREVENTION

Slope Grading: Re-shaping the slope to a less steep, more stable angle.
Drainage Control: Installing drains (pipes, channels) to remove water from the slope, reducing pore pressure.
Retaining Structures: Building strong retaining walls, gabions (wire cages filled with rocks), or terraces to hold the slope in place.
Vegetation (Re-afforestation): Planting trees, grasses, and shrubs. The roots anchor the soil. This is the cheapest and most sustainable long-term solution.
Land-Use Planning: Using GIS (Week 11) to map hazardous areas and prohibiting
construction on unstable slopes.

WEEK 4: CLIMATIC CHANGE

MEANING OF CLIMATE CHANGE

Climate Change refers to a significant, long-term, and persistent alteration in the average statistical patterns of weather for a region, or for the Earth as a whole.

This is not the same as Weather, which is the short-term state of the atmosphere (e.g., "It is raining today").
This is also not the same as Climate Variability, which is a natural fluctuation (e.g., El Niño, a 3-year drought).
Climate Change is a trend over decades or centuries, such as the current, rapid Global Warming (the increase in Earth's average surface temperature).

CAUSES OF CLIMATE CHANGE

NATURAL CAUSES (HISTORICAL)

The Earth's climate has always changed naturally.

Volcanic Eruptions: Massive eruptions (e.g., Mount Tambora, 1815) blast huge amounts of ash and sulphur dioxide into the stratosphere. These particles reflect sunlight, leading to a short-term global cooling.
Solar Radiation Variations (Sunspots): The sun's energy output varies in an 11-year "sunspot cycle." This has a minor, but measurable, effect on climate.
Orbital Changes (Milankovitch Cycles): These are very slow, long-term (thousands of years) changes in the Earth's orbit, tilt, and "wobble." They are the primary driver of the Earth's natural Ice Ages and warm interglacial periods.

ANTHROPOGENIC (HUMAN-INDUCED) CAUSES

The current, rapid climate change is overwhelmingly caused by human activities since the Industrial Revolution (c. 1760).
The Enhanced Greenhouse Effect:
The Greenhouse Effect is a natural and essential process. Gases in the atmosphere (like CO₂, H₂O) act like a "blanket," trapping some of the sun's heat and keeping the Earth warm enough for life.

The Enhanced Effect is the problem. Human activities are making this "blanket" thicker.

Emission of Greenhouse Gases (GHGs):
Carbon Dioxide (CO₂): The main driver. Comes from burning fossil fuels (coal, oil, and gas) for electricity, transport (cars, trucks), and industry (cement). Also from deforestation (cutting and burning trees).
Methane (CH₄): A more potent, but shorter-lived, GHG. Comes from agriculture (e.g., rice paddies, cattle/livestock), landfills (rotting waste), and oil and gas extraction (gas flaring).
Nitrous Oxide (N₂O): Comes from nitrogen-based fertilizers used in agriculture.
Deforestation: Forests are "carbon sinks" that absorb CO₂. Cutting them down (1) stops this absorption and (2) releases all the carbon stored in the trees.
Land-Use Change: Converting forests to cities or farms changes the "albedo" (reflectivity) of the Earth's surface.

EVIDENCE OF CLIMATE CHANGE

Rising Global Temperatures: Global average temperatures have risen by over 1.1°C since 1880. The last 10 years (2015-2024) are the warmest decade ever recorded.

Rising Atmospheric CO₂: Direct measurements (e.g., from Mauna Loa, Hawaii) show CO₂ levels have risen from ~280 parts per million (ppm) pre-industry to over 420 ppm today, the highest in human history.

Melting Ice and Glaciers: Glaciers in mountain ranges (Alps, Himalayas) are rapidly retreating. The Arctic sea ice is shrinking, and the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets are losing mass (melting) at an accelerating rate.
Sea-Level Rise: The global mean sea level is rising due to:
Thermal Expansion: Warm water expands.
Melting Land Ice: Water from melting glaciers flows into the sea.
This is a critical threat to all coastal cities (like Lagos).

IMPACTS OF CLIMATE CHANGE (NIGERIA/AFRICA)

Desertification (In the North):
Increased temperatures and more severe droughts are causing the Sahara Desert to expand southward ("desertification").
Lake Chad has shrunk by over 90% since the 1960s, destroying the livelihood of millions in Nigeria, Niger, Chad, and Cameroon.

Coastal Flooding (In the South):
Rising sea levels, combined with storm surges, are causing massive coastal erosion and flooding in low-lying cities like Lagos (Victoria Island), Port Harcourt, and Bayelsa.
Farmer-Herder Conflicts (In the Middle Belt):
This is a key socio-economic impact.
Desertification (Push factor) forces Fulani herders to move south in search of pasture and water.
They (Pull factor) enter the "Middle Belt," where they come into conflict with settled farmers, leading to violence, displacement, and a major threat to food security.
Impact on Agriculture:
More frequent droughts and floods destroy crops.
Erratic rainfall (e.g., rain coming too late, or too hard) disrupts the planting season.
Health Impacts: Higher temperatures can lead to more heatstroke and expand the range of diseases like malaria (carried by mosquitoes).

MITIGATION AND ADAPTATION
Mitigation (Addressing the Cause): Actions to reduce greenhouse gas emissions.
Global: The Paris Agreement (2015), where all nations agreed to Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) to limit warming.
Local:
Shifting from fossil-fuel generators to Renewable Energy (Solar, Wind, Hydro).
Afforestation: Planting trees (e.g., Nigeria's "Great Green Wall" project in the North).
Improving Energy Efficiency (e.g., using LED bulbs).
Adaptation (Addressing the Effect): Actions to cope with the impacts that are already happening.
Example: Building sea walls (like the "Great Wall of Lagos" for Eko Atlantic) to protect against sea-level rise.
Example: Using solar-powered irrigation (Week 1, Term 2) to adapt to droughts.
Example: Planting drought-resistant crop varieties.

WEEK 6: ECOWAS

INTRODUCTION

The Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS) is a regional economic and political bloc of 15 West African countries. It is one of the "building blocks" of the African Union (AU).

HISTORICAL BACKGROUND AND ESTABLISHMENT

Context: After independence, West Africa was fragmented along linguistic and colonial lines (Anglophone vs. Francophone). This hindered trade and cooperation.
Formation: Led by Nigeria (under Yakubu Gowon) and Togo (under Gnassingbé Eyadéma), the 15 states signed the Treaty of Lagos on May 28, 1975.
Founding Member States: Benin, Burkina Faso, Côte d'Ivoire, Gambia, Ghana, Guinea, Guinea-Bissau, Liberia, Mali, Mauritania (withdrew in 2000), Niger, Nigeria, Senegal, Sierra Leone, Togo. (Cape Verde joined later).
Headquarters: Abuja, Nigeria.

OBJECTIVES OF ECOWAS

The primary goal is to promote economic cooperation and integration.

Economic Integration: To create a Common Market, removing tariffs and trade barriers (via the Trade Liberalization Scheme - ETLS) and establishing a Common External Tariff (CET). The ultimate (but difficult) goal is a single currency (Eco).
Free Movement: To allow the free movement of persons, goods, and services within the region (leading to the ECOWAS Passport and visa-free travel).
Peace and Security: To maintain regional peace, stability, and security. This has become one of its most important functions.
Regional Cooperation: To harmonize policies in agriculture, energy (e.g., West African Power Pool - WAPP), and transport.

STRUCTURE AND ORGANS

Authority of Heads of State and Government: The supreme decision-making body (the 15 Presidents).
Council of Ministers: The "cabinet," composed of ministers (e.g., Foreign Affairs, Finance) from each state.
ECOWAS Commission: The "secretariat" or civil service, based in Abuja. It implements decisions and is led by the President of the Commission.
Community Parliament: A representative body (mostly advisory) that debates regional issues.
Community Court of Justice: A judicial body that interprets the ECOWAS treaty and, crucially, handles human rights cases.
ECOMOG (ECOWAS Monitoring Group): The (non-standing) military peacekeeping arm.
ACHIEVEMENTS

Peacekeeping (ECOMOG): This is its greatest success. ECOMOG, (though largely funded and staffed by Nigeria) successfully intervened to end brutal civil wars in Liberia and Sierra Leone. It also intervened to uphold democracy in The Gambia (2017).
Free Movement of Persons: The "Protocol on Free Movement" is a major achievement, allowing citizens to travel, trade, and live in other member states for up to 90 days without a visa.
Democratic Standards: ECOWAS has developed a "zero-tolerance" policy for military coups. It has (in the past) successfully suspended and sanctioned member states to pressure a return to democracy.

CHALLENGES

Political Instability: The biggest current challenge. The recent wave of military coups in Mali, Guinea, Burkina Faso, and Niger (the "Sahel Coup Belt") has created a major political crisis and undermined the bloc's authority.
Monetary Union Failure: The goal of launching the single currency, the Eco, has been postponed for decades. The Francophone countries (which use the CFA Franc) and Anglophone countries have failed to meet the "convergence criteria."
Non-Payment of Dues: Many member states fail to pay their membership fees, which starves the organization of funds (except for Nigeria, which pays the largest share).
Over-dominance of Nigeria: Nigeria's size (over 60% of the region's GDP and population) makes smaller states fear "domination."
Smuggling / Non-Implementation: Despite the free trade "on paper," many countries use porous borders and corrupt officials to undermine the rules (e.g., smuggling rice, cars, or petrol).

WEEK 8: TRADE

MEANING OF TRADE

Trade is the fundamental economic activity of buying, selling, or exchanging goods and services. It is the engine of an economy, allowing for the movement of goods from producers (who have a surplus) to consumers (who have a need).

TYPES OF TRADE

Internal (Domestic) Trade: Trade within the boundaries of a single country.
Wholesale: Buying in bulk from the producer and selling in smaller bulk to the retailer.
Retail: Selling in small units directly to the final consumer.
Example: Cocoa from Ondo State is sold to a factory in Lagos.
External (International) Trade: Trade across national borders.
Import: Buying goods from other countries (e.g., Nigeria buys cars from Japan).
Export: Selling goods to other countries (e.g., Nigeria sells crude oil to India).
Entrepôt: An "intermediary" trade. (e.g., Singapore imports raw rubber, processes it, and re-exports it as tires).
Barter Trade: The (ancient) direct exchange of goods for other goods, without the use of money.

IMPORTANCE OF TRADE IN ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT

Source of Foreign Exchange (Forex): International trade (exports) is the primary way a nation earns foreign currency (e.g., USD) needed to pay for its imports.
Encourages Specialization: Trade allows a country to specialize in what it does best (its "comparative advantage") and import the rest. (e.g., Nigeria specializes in oil, Ghana specializes in cocoa).
Creates Employment: The entire trade value chain (producers, transporters, bankers, marketers, retailers) creates millions of jobs.
Wider Market & Economies of Scale: Trade gives producers access to a global market, allowing them to produce in bulk (economies of scale), which lowers the cost per item.
Access to Variety & Technology: Imports allow citizens to access goods, services, and new technologies (e.g., smartphones, computers) that are not available locally.
Fosters International Relations: Trade builds economic and political links between countries.

HISTORICAL TRADE ROUTES IN WEST AFRICA

Trans-Saharan Trade Routes (Ancient):
Who: West African empires (Ghana, Mali, Songhai) trading with North African Berbers.
Route: Across the Sahara Desert.
Key Goods: The "Gold-for-Salt" trade. West Africa exported gold, ivory, and slaves north. North Africa imported salt, cloth, and horses south.
Impact: This trade built the wealth of Ghana, Mali, and Songhai and introduced Islam to West Africa.
Coastal Trade Routes (Trans-Atlantic):
Who: Coastal African kingdoms (e.g., Oyo, Benin, Asante) trading with Europeans (Portuguese, British, etc.).
Route: From coastal ports (e.g., Lagos, Badagry, Elmina) across the Atlantic.
Key Goods: Initially gold, ivory, and pepper. This was tragically replaced by the
Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade (1500s-1800s).
Impact: This trade reoriented the entire West African economy away from the interior (Sahara) and towards the coast, a pattern that still exists.

CHALLENGES FACING TRADE IN WEST AFRICA

Poor Infrastructure: This is the biggest challenge. Inadequate roads, congested ports (e.g., Apapa), and a lack of railways make it extremely slow and expensive to move goods.
Over-reliance on Primary Products: Most West African exports are raw materials (e.g., cocoa, oil, cashews). We fail to process these goods, so we lose the "value-added" profit. (We sell cocoa for $2,000/tonne and buy chocolate for $20,000/tonne).
Trade Barriers (Tariffs & Bureaucracy):
Despite ECOWAS, there are still too many tariffs, corrupt officials, and endless "checkpoints" on the roads that harass traders and demand bribes.
Insecurity: Banditry, piracy (in the Gulf of Guinea), and terrorism (in the Sahel) make trade routes unsafe.
Political Instability: Coups and border closures (e.g., Nigeria's 2019 land border closure) create uncertainty and disrupt supply chains.

WEEK 9: TOURISM

DEFINITION AND KEY COMPONENTS

Tourism is the act and process of spending time away from one's usual environment (for leisure, business, or other purposes) for a period of not more than one consecutive year. It is a major global service industry.

Key Components of the Tourism Industry:

Attractions: The "pull factor." The reason to visit (e.g., natural wonders, cultural sites, festivals, beaches).
Accommodation: Where tourists stay (e.g., hotels, resorts, guesthouses).
Transport: How tourists get to and around the destination (e.g., airlines, airports, roads, tour buses).
Amenities & Support Services: All other services tourists need (e.g., restaurants, tour guides, banks, security, healthcare).

TYPES OF TOURISM

Domestic Tourism: Travel within one's own country (e.g., a family from Lagos visiting Yankari Game Reserve).
International Tourism: Travel outside one's country.
Eco-Tourism: Responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment and
improves the well-being of local people (e.g., Obudu Mountain Resort).
Cultural/Heritage Tourism: Travel to experience the history, art, festivals, and culture of a place (e.g., visiting the Osun-Osogbo Sacred Grove, the Eyo Festival, or Benin City).
Business Tourism (MICE): Meetings, Incentives, Conferences, and Exhibitions. (e.g., a large conference at the Eko Hotel).
Religious Tourism: Travel for pilgrimage (e.g., to Mecca, Jerusalem, or to large religious camps in Nigeria).

IMPORTANCE OF TOURISM

Generates Foreign Exchange: International tourists (business and leisure) bring in "hard" currency (USD, EUR).
Creates Employment: A powerful job creator. It creates jobs directly (hotel staff, tour guides) and indirectly (farmers who supply the hotel, taxi drivers, craft-makers).
Stimulates Infrastructure: Governments are encouraged to build better roads, airports, and power systems to support a tourist-friendly environment.
Preserves Culture & Environment: Eco-tourism and Cultural Tourism provide a direct economic incentive for a community to protect its local forest (like Yankari) or preserve its festival (like Osun-Osogbo), because it is now a source of income.

CHALLENGES LIMITING TOURISM IN NIGERIA

DEFINITION OF REMOTE SENSING

Remote Sensing is the science and art of acquiring information about an object, area, or phenomenon without being in direct physical contact with it.

Your eyes are a remote sensor.
A camera is a remote sensor.
Satellite Remote Sensing is the specific process of using satellites (platforms) equipped with sensors to capture data about the Earth's surface and atmosphere.

PRINCIPLES OF REMOTE SENSING

The process works in a simple, standardized way:

Spectral Signature: The key principle. Different objects on Earth's surface (e.g., water, vegetation, soil, buildings) reflect and absorb energy (light) in unique ways. A sensor can identify an object by this unique "signature."
o Example: Healthy vegetation absorbs red light and strongly reflects Near- Infrared (NIR). A sensor can see this NIR reflection (which our eyes cannot) and instantly map all healthy crops.

This graph illustrates how different materials reflect energy across various wavelengths:

Vegetation: Reflects strongly in the Near-Infrared (NIR) and Green wavelengths, but absorbs Blue and Red light (for photosynthesis).
Water: Reflects most in the Blue wavelength and absorbs energy strongly as wavelengths increase, especially in the NIR.
Soil: Generally shows an increasing reflectance from Blue to NIR, with drier soil being more reflective than wet soil.

TYPES OF SENSORS

Passive Sensors:
How: They are passive. They need an external energy source, so they record
natural energy, which is usually reflected sunlight.
Example: Your eyes, a normal camera, Landsat, Sentinel-2.
Limitation: Cannot see through clouds. Cannot work at night.
Active Sensors:
How: They are active. They emit their own energy (a pulse of radiation) and then
record the "backscatter" (the reflection).
Example:
RADAR (Radio Detection and Ranging): Sends out radio waves.
LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging): Sends out laser pulses.
Advantage: Can work at night and (especially RADAR) can "see" through clouds.

TYPES OF RESOLUTION

Spatial Resolution: The detail or pixel size. (e.g., 30m (Landsat - one pixel is 30m x 30m) vs. 1m (Ikonos - high resolution)).
Temporal Resolution: The frequency or "revisit time." (e.g., "Landsat revisits every 16 days.")

MAJOR SATELLITES

Landsat (USA): The "workhorse" for 50+ years. Monitors land use, deforestation, agriculture.
Sentinel (EU): A modern series. Sentinel-2 is like Landsat. Sentinel-1 is a RADAR (active) satellite, excellent for flood mapping.
SPOT (France): High-resolution, used for mapping.
NigeriaSat-1, NigeriaSat-2, NigeriaSat-X: Nigeria's own Earth Observation satellites, run by NASRDA (National Space Research and Development Agency). They are used for mapping, disaster management, and security.

APPLICATIONS OF SATELLITE REMOTE SENSING

Agriculture: Monitoring crop health, predicting drought, and estimating yield.
Environmental Monitoring:
Tracking deforestation (e.g., in the Amazon and Nigeria).
Tracking desertification and the shrinking of Lake Chad.
Monitoring oil spills in the Niger Delta.
Disaster Management:
Flood Mapping: RADAR sensors can see the water through the clouds during a flood, showing exactly which areas are inundated.
Assessing damage from fires.
Urban Planning: Mapping urban sprawl (how cities are growing) and land-use change.
Climate Change: Monitoring sea-level rise and the melting of ice caps.

WEEK 11: GIS APPLICATION

MEANING OF GIS

A Geographic Information System (GIS) is a powerful computer-based system for capturing, storing, checking, manipulating, analyzing, and displaying (visualizing) geographically referenced data (spatial data).

Key Idea: GIS is more than just a map. It is a database that is linked to a map. It connects what (the data) with where (the location).
Example: A map is just a picture. A GIS is a system where you can click on a building (spatial data) and see its attributes (non-spatial data: owner's name, number of floors, year built, land value).

COMPONENTS OF GIS

Hardware: The computer, monitor, GPS units, scanners, and plotters.
Software: The program that runs the system (e.g., ArcGIS (commercial) or QGIS
(free/open-source)).
Data: The "fuel" of the GIS. (See 11.4).
People: The GIS Analyst—the trained human "brain" who runs the analysis.
Procedures: The methods and rules for conducting the analysis.

TYPES OF GEOGRAPHIC DATA IN GIS

This is the most fundamental concept in GIS.

Raster Data:
What: A grid of pixels (or cells), just like a digital photograph.
How: Each pixel has a single value.
Best for: Representing continuous data (things that have a value everywhere), such as:
Satellite images
Elevation (a map of height)
Temperature maps
Rainfall maps
File types: .jpg, .tif, .img
Vector Data:
What: A system that represents features using coordinates (X, Y).
How: It uses three types of features:
Points: A single coordinate (e.g., a hospital, a well, a crime location).
Lines (or Arcs): A series of connected points (e.g., a road, a river, a pipeline).
Polygons: A closed set of lines (e.g., a building footprint, a Local Government Area (LGA) boundary, a lake, a farm plot).
Best for: Representing discrete features with clear boundaries.

FUNCTIONS OF GIS (SPATIAL ANALYSIS)

GIS is not just for making maps; it's for analyzing them. This is done by overlaying layers.

Overlay Analysis: This is the core function. It involves "stacking" different data layers.
Question: "Where is the best place to build a new hospital?"
Analysis: Overlay a layer of Roads (for accessibility), a layer of Population Density (to be near people), and a layer of Existing Hospitals (to find an underserved area). The place that meets all three criteria is the answer.
Buffering:
Question: "Which houses are at risk from this leaking pipeline?"
Analysis: Create a buffer (a polygon) of "500 metres" around the pipeline layer. Any house inside this buffer is at risk.
Network Analysis:
Question: "What is the fastest route for a fire truck to get from the station to the fire?"
Analysis: The GIS "knows" the road network, speed limits, and one-way streets.

APPLICATIONS OF GIS (IN NIGERIA)

Urban Planning: Mapping urban sprawl, planning new roads, zoning land (residential vs. commercial).
Health Services:
Epidemiology: Mapping disease outbreaks. (This was famously used by Dr. John Snow to find the source of a cholera outbreak in 1854 London). It is used in Nigeria to track malaria, Lassa fever, and COVID-19 hotspots.
Site Selection: Finding the best location for a new clinic (see "Overlay Analysis").
Disaster Risk Reduction: Mapping flood-prone areas (e.g., in Lagos or Lokoja) and planning evacuation routes.
Natural Resource Management: Mapping deforestation, monitoring oil spills, managing forest reserves.
Security: Mapping crime "hotspots" to deploy police patrols more efficiently.

CHALLENGES OF GIS TECHNOLOGY

High Cost: Commercial software (like ArcGIS) and high-powered computers are expensive.
Lack of Skilled Personnel: There is a major shortage of trained GIS analysts in Nigeria.
Data Availability and Quality: This is the biggest challenge. "Garbage In, Garbage Out" (GIGO). If the data (e.g., a population map, a road map) is old, inaccurate, or non- existent, the GIS analysis will be useless.
