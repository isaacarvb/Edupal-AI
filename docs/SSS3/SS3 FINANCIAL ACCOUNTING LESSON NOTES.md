WEEK 1: CONSIGNMENT ACCOUNT – MEANING AND FEATURES

MEANING OF CONSIGNMENT

Consignment is a business arrangement where one person, the Consignor, sends goods to another person, the Consignee, who acts as an agent to sell the goods on the Consignor's behalf. The Consignee receives a commission for their services.

It is NOT a sale. It is a "sale or return" arrangement. The key feature is that ownership (title) and risk (e.g., from fire or theft) of the goods remain with the Consignor until the Consignee sells them to a final customer.

PARTIES INVOLVED

The Consignor (Principal): The owner of the goods. They send the goods and bear all the risks associated with them. Their motive is to reach a wider market and increase sales.
The Consignee (Agent): The agent who receives the goods, stores them, and sells them on behalf of the consignor. Their motive is to earn a commission.

KEY FEATURES OF A CONSIGNMENT

Relationship: The relationship is that of a Principal and Agent, not a Debtor and Creditor.
Ownership: Ownership of the goods remains with the Consignor until the Consignee sells them.
Risk: All risks of loss or damage to the goods (e.g., from theft, fire, or spoilage) are borne by the Consignor, unless the Consignee is negligent.
Unsold Stock: Any unsold goods (Closing Stock) at the end of the period still belong to the Consignor and will be shown in their Balance Sheet as "Stock on Consignment."
Expenses: All expenses incurred by both the Consignor (e.g., freight) and the Consignee (e.g., storage, advertising) are ultimately paid by the Consignor.
Remuneration: The Consignee is paid a commission (a percentage of sales) for their services.

KEY DOCUMENTS IN CONSIGNMENT

Proforma Invoice:
Who sends it? The Consignor sends this to the Consignee when the goods are dispatched.
What is it? A document that looks like an invoice but is "for form only." It is not a sales invoice and does not create a debt.
Purpose: It details the goods being sent (quantity, description, price) so the Consignee knows what to expect and what price to sell at.
Account Sales:
Who sends it? The Consignee sends this to the Consignor (e.g., at the end of the month).
What is it? This is the most important document. It is a periodic summary report from the agent to the principal.
Purpose: It details all transactions for the period:
Total sales made (Gross Proceeds).
Less: Expenses paid by the Consignee.
Less: Commission earned by the Consignee.
Equals: The net amount due to the Consignor, which is usually sent with the report as a bank draft (remittance).

TERMS USED IN CONSIGNMENT

Ordinary Commission: A standard percentage of the total sales (Gross Proceeds) paid to the consignee for their service of selling.
Del Credere Commission: An additional commission paid by the consignor to the consignee. In return, the consignee guarantees all credit sales.
If Del Credere is paid: The Consignee is responsible for any Bad Debts. Bad Debts become the Consignee's personal expense.
If Del Credere is not paid: The Consignor is responsible for any Bad Debts. Bad Debts are an expense of the consignment.
Expenses:
Non-Recurring Expenses: One-time costs incurred to get the goods from the consignor's factory to the consignee's warehouse. These are added to the cost of stock.
Examples: Freight/Carriage, Insurance-in-transit, Import/Customs Duties, Dock Dues.
Recurring Expenses: Costs incurred after the goods have arrived at the consignee's warehouse. They are not added to the cost of stock.
Examples: Storage/Warehouse rent, Advertising, Sales staff salaries.
Losses:
Normal Loss: A loss that is expected, unavoidable, and inherent to the nature of the goods (e.g., evaporation, weight loss from drying, leakage).
Accounting Treatment: The loss is not given a value. Instead, the cost of the lost units is absorbed by the remaining good units (i.e., the cost per unit of the good units is inflated).
Abnormal Loss: A loss that is unexpected and avoidable (e.g., fire, theft, flood, accident).
Accounting Treatment: The loss must be valued (at cost + proportionate non-recurring expenses) and removed from the Consignment Account (credited). It is then transferred to the P&L Account as a loss. This is done to show the true profitability of the consignment itself.

WEEK 2: CONSIGNMENT ACCOUNT – ACCOUNTING TREATMENT

INTRODUCTION

To determine the profit or loss from a consignment, the Consignor prepares a set of ledger accounts. The Consignee keeps simpler records.

LEDGER ENTRIES (IN THE BOOKS OF THE CONSIGNOR)

The Consignor is the owner and wants to know the profit. They open three main accounts:

Consignment Account (e.g., "Consignment to Abuja Account")

Purpose: This is the main account. It is a special Trading and Profit & Loss Account for the specific venture. Its final balance represents the profit or loss on that consignment.
Double Entry:
DEBIT this account with all Costs and Expenses:
Cost of goods sent (from Goods Sent on Consignment A/c)
All expenses paid by Consignor (from Cash/Bank A/c)
All expenses paid by Consignee (from Consignee's A/c)
All commissions (Ordinary and Del Credere) (from Consignee's A/c)
CREDIT this account with all Income and Abnormal Gains:
Total sales proceeds (from Consignee's A/c)
Value of any Abnormal Loss (from Abnormal Loss A/c)
Value of Unsold Stock (Closing Stock) (from Stock on Consignment A/c)
Balancing Figure: A debit balance is a Net Loss. A credit balance is a Net Profit.

Consignee's Account (e.g., "Mr. B (Abuja) Account")

Purpose: This is a Personal Account (a Debtor/Creditor). It shows how much the Consignee owes the Consignor (or vice versa) at any point in time.
Double Entry:
DEBIT the Consignee with what he owes the Consignor:
Total Sales (Gross Proceeds). This is the total amount the Consignee is responsible for.
CREDIT the Consignee with what he has paid or is entitled to:
Expenses paid on behalf of the consignor.
Commission(s) earned.
Any Bad Debts (if no del credere commission is paid).
Any advance payment or bank draft (remittance) sent.
Balancing Figure: The balance (usually debit) is the amount still owed by the Consignee. After the final remittance, this account should be zero.

Goods Sent on Consignment Account

Purpose: A temporary "holding" account to record the cost of goods sent.
Double Entry:
CREDIT with the cost of goods sent (the corresponding debit is in the Consignment A/c).
At year-end, this account is closed by transferring its balance to the Purchases Account or Trading Account (to show that these goods were not normal sales).

VALUATION OF UNSOLD STOCK (CLOSING STOCK)

This is the most critical calculation. Stock must be valued at Cost + Proportionate Non- Recurring Expenses.

Principle: The value of the stock should include its original cost plus all costs incurred to get it to its present location and condition.
Formula:
Total Cost of Goods = Cost of all units sent
Total Non-Recurring Expenses = (Consignor's Expenses) + (Consignee's *Non- Recurring* Expenses)
Total Cost to Warehouse = (1) + (2)
Cost per Unit = (Total Cost to Warehouse) / (Total Units Sent)
Value of Closing Stock = Cost per Unit × Units Unsold
TREATMENT OF ABNORMAL LOSS

Principle: Abnormal losses (theft, fire) are not part of the normal cost of the venture. They must be removed from the Consignment Account so they don't distort the profit.
Valuation: The loss is valued exactly the same way as closing stock (Cost + Proportionate Non-Recurring Expenses).
Double Entry:
Dr. Abnormal Loss Account
Cr. Consignment Account
If insured: Dr. Insurance Claim Account, Cr. Abnormal Loss Account (for the amount recovered). The remaining balance is a loss to the P&L.

WEEK 3: JOINT VENTURE ACCOUNT

MEANING AND FEATURES OF JOINT VENTURES

Meaning: A Joint Venture (JV) is a short-term, temporary business arrangement where two or more persons (called "Co-Venturers") agree to combine their resources to execute a specific project or transaction.
Example: Two car dealers (A and B) might pool their money to buy a batch of 50 imported cars, sell them, and split the profits. Once the 50 cars are sold, the venture is over.
Features:
Specific Purpose: It is for a single, defined venture, not a continuous business.
Temporary: The agreement is dissolved once the project is complete.
Profit/Loss Sharing: Co-venturers share profits and losses based on their agreed- upon ratio (or equally, if no ratio is set).
Resource Pooling: Venturers can contribute cash, goods, or even just their skills.

ACCOUNTING PROCEDURE 1: SEPARATE SET OF BOOKS METHOD
This is the "full" accounting method, used for large JVs.

How it works: The JV is treated as a separate entity. A new, separate Joint Bank Account is opened, and a full set of books is kept for the venture.
Ledger Accounts to be Opened:
Joint Venture Account: The P&L account for the venture.
Co-Venturer Accounts: The "capital" accounts for each member.
Joint Bank Account: The cash book for the venture.
Double Entry:

1. Venturers contribute capital:
Dr. Joint Bank
Cr. Co-Venturer's A/c (with their contribution)
2. JV buys goods or pays expenses:
Dr. Joint Venture A/c (it's an expense)
Cr. Joint Bank (money is paid out)
3. Venturer supplies goods from their own stock:
Dr. Joint Venture A/c (it's a "cost" to the venture)
Cr. Co-Venturer's A/c (the venture "owes" them for it)
4. JV makes a sale:
Dr. Joint Bank (money comes in)
Cr. Joint Venture A/c (it's income)
5. Find Profit/Loss:
Balance the Joint Venture Account.
Dr. Joint Venture A/c (with the profit)
Cr. Co-Venturer's A/c (share the profit)
6. Final Settlement:
Pay back the venturers.
Dr. Co-Venturer's A/c (to close their account)
Cr. Joint Bank (the final payout)
ACCOUNTING PROCEDURE 2: MEMORANDUM JOINT VENTURE ACCOUNT METHOD

This method is used for smaller JVs. No separate books are kept. Each venturer records only their own transactions.

How it works:
Step 1: Each venturer keeps a single account in their own personal ledger, titled "Joint Venture with [Other's Name] Account."
In Venturer A's Books: "JV with B Account"
Dr. with all his own costs (purchases, expenses).
Dr. with his share of the profit.
Cr. with all sales he made.
Cr. with the final remittance he receives (or Dr. if he pays).
Step 2 (The "Memo"): To find the profit, one person (or both) prepares a
"Memorandum Joint Venture Account."
This is not a ledger account. It's a worksheet (a simple T-account) that combines all venturers' costs and sales to find the profit/loss.
Step 3: The calculated profit is then shared, and each venturer makes the final entry (Step 1, profit entry) in their own books.

WEEK 4: CONTRACT ACCOUNT

MEANING AND FEATURES

Meaning: A Contract Account is a specialized ledger account used in Contract Costing. Contract Costing is a system used by companies (contractors) that engage in long-term, large-scale construction or engineering projects.
Examples: Building a bridge, a highway, a stadium, or a ship.
Features:
Long-Term: The projects (contracts) usually span multiple accounting years.
On-Site: The work is done at the client's (contractee's) site, not in the contractor's factory.
High Value: The projects are very expensive.
Payments: The contractor is paid in installments (progress payments) as work is completed.

DIFFERENCE BETWEEN CONTRACT AND JOB COSTING

FeatureContract CostingJob CostingProject SizeVery large, high value.Usually small, lower value.DurationLong-term (months or years).Short-term (days or weeks).LocationWork done at the client's site.Work done in the factory/workshop.ExamplesBuilding a dam, highway.Printing 1,000 calendars, repairing a car.
IMPORTANT TERMS IN CONTRACT ACCOUNTING

Contract Price: The total, fixed price agreed upon for the entire project.
Contractor: The company doing the work (e.g., Julius Berger).
Contractee: The client who is paying for the work (e.g., the Government).
Work-in-Progress (WIP): The total cost of work done on a contract so far.
Architect's Certificate (or Surveyor's Certificate): A certificate issued by the client's architect or surveyor certifying the value of the work completed to date. This is the "proof" that the work is satisfactory.
Value of Work Certified: The value of the work approved by the architect. This is the "sales" figure for the period.
Progress Payment (or Cash Received): The amount of cash the client pays to the contractor. This is usually the Value of Work Certified minus a retention fee.
Retention Money: A percentage (e.g., 10%) of the value of work certified, which is held back by the contractee (client) as a security deposit. It is released at the end of the contract after a "defects liability period" to ensure the contractor has fixed all faults.
Notional Profit: The "on-paper" profit for an incomplete contract.
o Notional Profit = Value of Work Certified - Cost of Work to Date

WEEK 6: DEPARTMENTAL ACCOUNT

MEANING AND PURPOSE

Meaning: Departmental Accounts are accounts prepared by a business that has been divided into two or more distinct departments.
Example: A large store like Shoprite, which has a "Grocery" department, a "Butchery" department, a "Bakery," and an "Electronics" department.
Purpose: The main purpose is to find the profit or loss for each department separately. This is done by preparing a single Columnar Trading, Profit and Loss Account.

REASONS FOR DEPARTMENTAL ACCOUNTS

Performance Evaluation: To see which departments are profitable and which are making a loss.
Decision Making: Helps management decide whether to expand a profitable department, change the strategy for a losing one, or shut it down.
Remuneration: To calculate the commission or bonus for the staff and managers of each department based on their specific performance.
Stock Control: Helps in controlling the stock levels for each different type of product.
Information: Provides detailed information on the sales, costs, and profitability of each product line.

ALLOCATION AND APPORTIONMENT OF EXPENSES

This is the main challenge in preparing departmental accounts.

Allocation (Direct Expenses):
These are expenses that can be directly and exclusively traced to one department. They are easy to account for.
Examples:
Purchases for Dept A.
Sales of Dept A.
Salary of the Dept A manager.
Advertising specifically for Dept A's products.
Apportionment (Indirect/Shared Expenses):
These are common expenses that benefit all departments and must be split or
shared among them using a fair and logical basis.
The "Basis of Apportionment" is the rule used to split the cost.

WEEK 8: BRANCH ACCOUNT

MEANING AND TYPES OF BRANCHES

Meaning: A Branch is a section of a business that operates at a different geographical location from the Head Office (HO), but is under the same ownership and control.
Purpose: To expand sales, cover a wider territory, and serve more customers.
Types of Branches:
Dependent Branch (our focus):
Does not keep its own full set of accounts.
It is like a simple sales office.
All goods are supplied by the HO.
All expenses are (usually) paid by the HO.
All cash received from sales is remitted (sent back) to the HO.
The HO does all the bookkeeping.
Independent Branch:
A large branch (e.g., a "Lagos HO" with a "London Branch") that is big enough to act as an independent business.
It does keep its own full set of books.
It just sends a Trial Balance to the HO at year-end for consolidation.

SYSTEMS OF BRANCH ACCOUNTING (FOR DEPENDENT BRANCHES)

Debtors System: The simplest method. Used when the branch is small. The HO treats the branch as a single Debtor.
Stock and Debtors System: A more complex, "control" system designed to monitor stock movements in detail (at selling price) to prevent fraud or theft.
Final Accounts System: Used by the HO to incorporate the Trial Balance of an
independent branch.

PREPARATION OF BRANCH ACCOUNTS (DEBTORS SYSTEM)

This is the most common method for SS3 level.
Core Idea: The Head Office opens one single ledger account for the branch, e.g., "Kano Branch Account".
Purpose: This one account is a "memo" account that acts as both a P&L Account and an Asset/Liability Account.
Goods at Cost: We will assume the HO sends goods to the branch at cost price.

How to Prepare the Kano Branch Account (in HO Books):

This account is like a "snapshot" of the branch. Debit what the Branch has (assets) or cost the HO. Credit what the Branch owes or earned.

WEEK 9: PUBLIC SECTOR ACCOUNTING

MEANING AND OBJECTIVES

Meaning: Public Sector Accounting is the accounting process used by the Government
(Federal, State, LGA) and its various ministries, departments, and agencies (MDAs).
Objectives: The primary objective is NOT profit. The objectives are:
Accountability: To show the public (taxpayers) that the funds entrusted to the government were used properly, legally, and for their intended purposes.
Stewardship: To demonstrate that the government has been a good "steward" (caretaker) of public resources.
Transparency: To provide clear, open, and accessible financial reports.
Planning & Budgeting: To provide a basis for planning the next year's budget.

SOURCES OF REVENUE AND TYPES OF EXPENDITURE

Sources of Government Revenue:
Federal:
Oil Revenue: (Royalties, Petroleum Profit Tax, NNPC proceeds) - The largest source.
Non-Oil Revenue: Company Income Tax (FIRS), Customs & Excise Duties, VAT.
State:
Internally Generated Revenue (IGR): Personal Income Tax (PAYE), Land Use Charge.
Federal Allocation (FAAC): The state's share of the national oil revenue.
Local Government:
IGR: Market rates, tenement rates, business registration fees.
Federal/State Allocation.
Types of Government Expenditure:
Recurrent Expenditure: Day-to-day "running costs." They are consumed within the year.
Examples: Salaries & Wages, purchase of stationery, fuel for vehicles, travel, office maintenance.
Capital Expenditure: Spending on long-term "assets" that last for more than one year.
Examples: Construction of a new road, building a new hospital or school, buying a new computer or bus.

STRUCTURE OF GOVERNMENT ACCOUNTING

Consolidated Revenue Fund (CRF): The "main bank account" of the Federal Government. The Constitution states that all revenues collected (unless specified otherwise) must be paid into this fund. No money can be withdrawn from it except by an Appropriation Act (The Budget) passed by the National Assembly.
Accountant-General of the Federation: The "Chief Accountant" of the government. They manage the CRF, make payments, and prepare the government's financial statements.
Auditor-General for the Federation: The "External Auditor" of the government. They are independent and check the accounts prepared by the Accountant-General. Their job is to report to the National Assembly on whether the government spent the money legally and correctly.
