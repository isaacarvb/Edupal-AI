## Pre-Colonial Nigeria

### Pre-Colonial Nigerian States and Societies
**Class:** JSS1  |  **Topic ID:** `JSS-HIS-001-01`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Describe the major pre-colonial Nigerian states and their organization
- Explain the economic, political and social structures of pre-colonial societies
- Trace major trade routes and trade activities in pre-colonial Nigeria
- Identify the cultural achievements of pre-colonial Nigerian civilizations
- Explain the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade and its impact on Nigeria

#### 📖 Notes
- Nok Culture (500 BC–AD 200): earliest known civilisation in Nigeria; iron technology; famous terracotta sculptures found in Plateau State
- Oyo Empire (14th–19th century): Yoruba; powerful cavalry-based military; Alaafin as ruler; Oyo Mesi (council of chiefs) as checks on Alaafin; extensive trade network
- Benin Kingdom (12th century–1897): Bini/Edo; the Oba as divine ruler; renowned bronze and ivory art (Benin Bronzes — now disputed in British Museum); strong military; destroyed by British Punitive Expedition 1897
- Hausa States (Kano, Katsina, Zaria, Gobir, etc.): northern Nigeria; independent city-states; trade centres; Islam introduced in 14th century; conquered by Fulani jihad (Usman dan Fodio 1804) → Sokoto Caliphate
- Sokoto Caliphate (1804): Usman dan Fodio launched jihad to reform Islam; created largest empire in 19th century Africa; Emirs as local rulers; still influential today
- Igbo societies: decentralised (no single king); governed by age grades, title societies (Ozo), oracle systems (Chukwu oracle); Arochukwu Confederacy important in slave trade
- Tiv, Idoma, Ijaw, Efik and other communities: diverse structures; Efik of Calabar dominant in coastal trade
- Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade (16th–19th century): approximately 3.5 million Nigerians enslaved; Badagry and Calabar as major ports; consequences — depopulation, economic disruption, warfare between communities, psychological trauma
- Legitimate trade: after abolition, shift to palm oil, cotton, groundnut, timber; 'Oil Rivers' in the Delta

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Draw a map of Nigeria marking the locations of the major pre-colonial states and kingdoms studied
2. Research: what happened to the Benin Bronzes? Write a 200-word letter from Nigeria's government to the British Museum requesting their return
3. Timeline activity: arrange 10 key pre-colonial events in chronological order from Nok Culture to the Sokoto Caliphate

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> BECE tests names of major states, their locations, rulers' titles (Alaafin — Oyo; Oba — Benin; Emir — Hausa/Fulani), and the Trans-Atlantic slave trade impact
> Know: Oyo = Yoruba; Benin = Bini/Edo (NOT Benin Republic country); Sokoto = Fulani/Hausa; Arochukwu = Igbo
> Trap: the Benin Kingdom is in present-day Edo State, Nigeria — NOT the same as the Republic of Benin (country)

## Colonial Nigeria

### British Colonialism, Amalgamation and Nationalism
**Class:** JSS2  |  **Topic ID:** `JSS-HIS-002-01`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Describe how Britain colonised Nigeria
- Explain the amalgamation of Nigeria in 1914
- Identify major nationalist leaders and their contributions
- Describe the political parties and movements that led to independence
- Explain how Nigeria gained independence on October 1, 1960

#### 📖 Notes
- British colonialism: began in earnest with 1861 annexation of Lagos; conquest of Yorubaland (1893), Niger Delta (1880s–1900), Benin Kingdom (1897 Punitive Expedition), Hausa-Fulani states (1903 — Kano and Sokoto), Igbo territory (various campaigns 1900–1920s)
- Royal Niger Company (1879–1900): trading company given royal charter to govern the region; surrendered charter 1900
- 1900: Northern and Southern Protectorates established under direct Crown control
- Amalgamation (1914): Governor-General Lord Lugard merged Northern and Southern Nigeria into 'Colony and Protectorate of Nigeria' — January 1, 1914; administered as one entity for administrative efficiency; many Nigerians argue it was a 'marriage of inconvenience'
- Indirect rule: Lugard's method of governance — using existing traditional rulers as local administrators; worked in North (Emirs); less successful in East (no chiefs) and West
- Nigerian nationalist movement: educated Nigerians and traditional rulers resisting colonial exploitation
- Newspapers: Lagos Weekly Record, West African Pilot (Azikiwe) — important tools of nationalism
- NCNC (1944): National Council of Nigeria and the Cameroons; founded by Herbert Macaulay and Nnamdi Azikiwe; pan-Nigerian
- AG (1950): Action Group; founded by Obafemi Awolowo; Yoruba-based
- NPC (1949): Northern Peoples' Congress; Ahmadu Bello; Northern-based
- Constitutional developments: Macpherson Constitution (1951), Lyttleton Constitution (1954 — federal structure), Independence Constitution (1960)
- Independence: October 1, 1960; Sir Abubakar Tafawa Balewa as Prime Minister; Nnamdi Azikiwe as Governor-General (later President of Republic 1963)

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Roleplay: an 1885 Berlin Conference where European powers divided Africa — students represent Britain, France, Germany, Belgium; discuss how Nigeria's borders were drawn
2. Make a 'Father of Nigeria' profile card for: Macaulay, Azikiwe, Awolowo, Ahmadu Bello, and Tafawa Balewa — include birth year, state/ethnic group, party, and one achievement
3. Debate: 'Amalgamation of 1914 was a mistake' — was it good or bad for Nigeria? Use historical evidence

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> BECE tests: amalgamation date (1914), Lord Lugard, party founders (NCNC–Azikiwe/Macaulay, AG–Awolowo, NPC–Ahmadu Bello), and Independence date (1st October 1960)
> Know: PM at Independence = Tafawa Balewa; Governor-General = Azikiwe
> Trap: Herbert Macaulay founded the first political party (NNDP 1923) AND co-founded NCNC 1944 — he is called the 'Father of Nigerian Nationalism'

## Post-Independence Nigeria

### Nigerian History from Independence to the Present
**Class:** JSS3  |  **Topic ID:** `JSS-HIS-003-01`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Trace major events in Nigerian political history from 1960 to present
- Explain the causes and consequences of the Nigerian Civil War
- Identify major military leaders and their contributions/controversies
- Describe Nigeria's return to democracy in 1999
- Evaluate the achievements and challenges of Nigerian democracy

#### 📖 Notes
- 1st Republic (1960–1966): parliamentary democracy; PM Tafawa Balewa; 1963 Republican Constitution; three regions; challenges: election crisis 1964, Western Region crisis 1965
- January 15, 1966 coup: first military coup by Kaduna Nzeogwu and others; killed many northern leaders and PM Balewa; General Aguiyi-Ironsi became head of state
- July 1966 counter-coup: northern officers killed Ironsi; General Yakubu Gowon became Head of State
- Civil War (1967–1970): Eastern Region declared Republic of Biafra on May 30, 1967 (Lt. Col. Odumegwu Ojukwu); Federal Government offensive; No-Victor-No-Vanquished policy; ended January 15, 1970; estimated 1–3 million deaths (many from starvation)
- Gowon era (1966–1975): created 12 states (1967), 19 states (1976); oil boom ('petrodollars'); ECOWAS founded 1975; ousted in bloodless coup while at OAU summit
- Murtala Muhammed (1975–1976): decisive reforms; capital transfer to Abuja announced; assassinated February 13, 1976 by Lt. Col. Dimka
- Obasanjo 1 (1976–1979): handed over to civilians; 19 states; transition to 2nd Republic
- 2nd Republic (1979–1983): presidential system; President Shehu Shagari (NPN); ended in military coup December 31, 1983 citing corruption
- Buhari/Idiagbon (1983–1985): WAI (War Against Indiscipline); Decree 4 restricted press freedom; overthrown by Babangida
- Babangida era (1985–1993): SAP (Structural Adjustment Programme); June 12 1993 election (MKO Abiola won but annulled); transition to 4th Republic; stepped aside August 1993
- Abacha (1993–1998): most brutal military rule; imprisoned opposition; Abiola died in custody 1998; Abacha died June 8, 1998
- Abubakar (1998–1999): managed transition to civilian rule; Obasanjo won 1999 election
- 4th Republic (1999–present): longest unbroken democratic period; Obasanjo (1999–2007), Yar'Adua (2007–2010), Jonathan (2010–2015), Buhari 2 (2015–2023), Tinubu (2023–present)

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Create a Nigerian political timeline poster: list every head of state/government from 1960 to present with their years, party/branch (civilian/military), and one key action
2. Case study: read accounts of the Nigerian Civil War from different perspectives (Federal, Biafran); identify common ground and differences; write a reflective essay
3. Current affairs: analyse one major challenge facing Nigeria's democracy today (insecurity, corruption, electoral malpractice); suggest constitutional solutions

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> BECE tests major coup dates, heads of state names, Civil War facts (1967–1970, Ojukwu, Gowon, Biafra), and 1999 return to democracy
> Know the order: Balewa → Ironsi → Gowon → Murtala → Obasanjo 1 → Shagari → Buhari 1 → Babangida → Abacha → Abubakar → Obasanjo 2
> Trap: the Civil War ended in 1970 with Gowon's 'No Victor, No Vanquished' speech — it is NOT still ongoing
