# TOPIC 1: Speech Sounds — Vowels, Consonants and Phonology
**Topic ID:** SSS-ENG-001-01

## 1.1 Definition of Phonology

**Phonology** is the branch of linguistics that studies the sound system of a language — the rules governing how sounds combine and function. English has **44 phonemes (sounds)** even though the alphabet has only 26 letters.

A **phoneme** is the smallest unit of sound that can change meaning in a word.
Example: /p/ and /b/ are different phonemes — "pat" and "bat" have different meanings.

---

## 1.2 Vowel Sounds

**Vowels** are sounds produced with an open vocal tract — the airstream flows freely without obstruction.

### Short Vowels (7):
| Symbol | Sound | Example words |
|--------|-------|--------------|
| /ɪ/ | short "i" | bit, sit, him |
| /e/ | short "e" | bed, red, ten |
| /æ/ | short "a" | cat, man, hat |
| /ɒ/ | short "o" | hot, pot, dog |
| /ʌ/ | short "u" | cup, sun, bus |
| /ʊ/ | short "oo" | book, foot, good |
| /ə/ | schwa (unstressed) | about, letter, teacher |

### Long Vowels (5):
| Symbol | Sound | Example words |
|--------|-------|--------------|
| /iː/ | long "ee" | feet, see, key |
| /ɑː/ | long "ar" | car, far, bath |
| /ɔː/ | long "aw" | law, door, board |
| /uː/ | long "oo" | boot, moon, blue |
| /ɜː/ | long "er" | bird, word, nurse |

### Diphthongs (8) — Vowel Glides:
| Symbol | Example words |
|--------|--------------|
| /eɪ/ | day, name, rain |
| /aɪ/ | high, time, my |
| /ɔɪ/ | boy, oil, coin |
| /əʊ/ | go, home, road |
| /aʊ/ | now, out, house |
| /ɪə/ | ear, here, idea |
| /eə/ | air, there, care |
| /ʊə/ | tour, cure, pure |

---

## 1.3 Consonant Sounds

**Consonants** are sounds produced with some obstruction or narrowing of the airstream.

### Classification by Manner of Articulation:

| Manner | Description | Examples |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| **Plosive (Stop)** | Complete blockage then sudden release | /p, b, t, d, k, g/ |
| **Fricative** | Partial blockage; hissing/buzzing sound | /f, v, s, z, ʃ, ʒ, θ, ð, h/ |
| **Affricate** | Plosive + fricative combined | /tʃ/ (church), /dʒ/ (judge) |
| **Nasal** | Air through nose | /m, n, ŋ/ |
| **Lateral** | Air flows around sides of tongue | /l/ |
| **Approximant** | Vowel-like; slight narrowing | /r, w, j/ |

### Voiced vs Voiceless:
- **Voiced:** Vocal cords vibrate — b, d, g, v, z, ʒ, dʒ, ð, m, n, ŋ, l, r, w, j
- **Voiceless:** No vocal cord vibration — p, t, k, f, s, ʃ, tʃ, θ, h

---

## 1.4 Syllables and Word Stress

A **syllable** is a unit of pronunciation with one vowel sound at its centre.

**Counting syllables:**
- "cat" = 1 syllable
- "stu-dent" = 2 syllables
- "com-pu-ter" = 3 syllables
- "in-tel-li-gent" = 4 syllables

### Stress Patterns:
**Word stress** = the emphasis placed on one syllable, making it louder, longer, and higher-pitched.

| Pattern | Examples |
|---------|---------|
| Stress on 1st syllable (most common for nouns/adjectives) | TAble, CLEver, HAven |
| Stress on 2nd syllable (common for verbs) | beLIEVE, reCORD, proGRESS |
| Noun vs Verb stress shift | RE-cord (noun) vs re-CORD (verb); PRE-sent vs pre-SENT |

---

## 1.5 Intonation

**Intonation** = the rise and fall of pitch in connected speech.

| Pattern | Use |
|---------|-----|
| **Falling intonation** | Statements, commands, wh-questions |
| **Rising intonation** | Yes/no questions, lists (all but last item), uncertainty |
| **Fall-rise** | Contrast, implication, unfinished thought |

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** Identify the number of syllables in each word: "beautiful", "environment", "university"

**Solution:**
- beautiful = beau-ti-ful = **3 syllables**
- environment = en-vi-ron-ment = **4 syllables**
- university = u-ni-ver-si-ty = **5 syllables**

---

**Example 2 (WAEC type):** Which word has the stress on the second syllable? (a) campus (b) language (c) attract (d) national

**Solution:** (c) **attract** — at-TRACT. The others all have stress on the first syllable.

---

**Example 3 (JAMB type):** The word "photograph" has its stress on which syllable?

**Solution:** PHO-to-graph → **first syllable**

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** Paper 3 (Oral English) tests: identification of vowel/consonant sounds, rhyming words, syllable counting, word stress, and intonation patterns.

> **JAMB** tests stress patterns (multiple choice), rhyming word identification, and phonetic transcription.

> **Common trap:** Counting syllables by letters, not sounds. "Clothes" has 6 letters but only 1 syllable. "Every" has 5 letters but 3 syllables (ev-er-y).

> **Rhyme test:** Words rhyme based on SOUND, not spelling. "Great" /greɪt/ and "late" /leɪt/ rhyme. "Great" and "eat" do NOT rhyme despite similar spelling.

> **Silent letters:** know, write, gnome, psychology — the underlined letters are silent. They do not form a separate syllable.

# TOPIC 2: Spoken English — Conversation and Oral Comprehension
**Topic ID:** SSS-ENG-001-02

## 2.1 Features of Spoken English

**Spoken English** differs from written English in several important ways:

| Feature | Spoken English | Written English |
|---------|---------------|----------------|
| Medium | Sound waves | Written symbols |
| Errors | Cannot be easily corrected | Can be edited |
| Support | Facial expression, gestures | Punctuation, layout |
| Interaction | Often interactive | Usually one-way |
| Formality | Often informal | Often formal |
| Grammar | Often incomplete sentences | Complete sentences expected |

---

## 2.2 Types of Speech

| Type | Description | Features |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Conversation** | Two-way informal exchange | Turn-taking, contractions, fillers |
| **Discussion** | Structured exchange of ideas | Multiple speakers; one topic |
| **Debate** | Formal argument for/against a motion | Proposers, opposers, strict turns |
| **Interview** | Question-and-answer format | One person questions; other answers |
| **Speech/Address** | One person speaking to many | Prepared; formal; persuasive |
| **Announcement** | Brief public information | Clear, concise, direct |

---

## 2.3 Register and Tone

**Register** = the level of formality appropriate to a situation.

| Situation | Register | Features |
|-----------|---------|---------|
| Addressing a principal | Formal | Full sentences, respectful vocabulary |
| Talking to friends | Informal | Contractions, slang, colloquial language |
| Debate speech | Formal | Organised, evidence-based |
| Telephone conversation | Semi-formal | Polite but relaxed |

---

## 2.4 Features of Oral Comprehension

In oral comprehension passages, listen for:
- Main topic and key message
- Speaker's tone (persuasive, informative, critical)
- Specific details (names, dates, figures)
- Implied meaning

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** A principal addresses students about exam malpractice. What register and features would you expect?

**Solution:**
- Register: **formal**
- Features: complete sentences, no slang, authoritative tone, imperative sentences ("You must..."), third-person references to rules, serious vocabulary ("integrity", "consequences", "discipline")

---

**Example 2 (WAEC type):** Identify the type of speech: "Madam Chairperson, I stand before this house to oppose the motion that technology has done more harm than good."

**Solution:** This is a **debate speech** — it follows the formal convention of addressing the chairperson, stating position (oppose), and referencing "the house."

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** Oral English tests listening comprehension (heard once or twice on tape), identification of speech types, and appropriate register.

> **Common trap:** In debates, the side that AGREES with the motion is the "proposition/proposer." The side that DISAGREES is the "opposition/opposer."

> Know the courtesy titles: "Madam Chairperson," "Honourable members," "Ladies and Gentlemen" for formal speeches.

# TOPIC 3: Reading Comprehension — Techniques and Critical Reading
**Topic ID:** SSS-ENG-002-01

## 3.1 Types of Reading

| Type | Purpose | Method |
|------|---------|--------|
| **Skimming** | Get a quick overview | Read title, subheadings, first and last sentences of each paragraph |
| **Scanning** | Find specific information | Look for key words, numbers, names |
| **Intensive reading** | Deep understanding and study | Read slowly, annotate, question, reread |
| **Extensive reading** | General enjoyment and fluency | Read at natural pace; don't stop for every unknown word |

---

## 3.2 Comprehension Question Types

| Type | What is tested | How to answer |
|------|---------------|--------------|
| **Factual/Literal** | Information directly in the passage | Find and paraphrase the relevant sentence |
| **Inferential** | Meaning not directly stated | Use reasoning and clues from the passage |
| **Vocabulary in context** | Meaning of a word AS USED in the passage | Consider context, then give a synonym/brief meaning |
| **Summary** | Condense specific points | List only key points in your own words |
| **Reference** | What a pronoun/phrase refers to | Trace back to the nearest suitable antecedent |
| **Tone/Attitude** | The writer's feeling about the subject | Identify emotional vocabulary and adjectives |

---

## 3.3 Answering Comprehension Questions — Rules

1. **Read the passage completely** before reading the questions.
2. **Re-read the relevant section** before answering each question.
3. **Answer in complete sentences** (unless specifically told to use note form).
4. **Use your own words** — do not copy whole sentences from the passage.
5. **Include specific evidence** — quote briefly, then explain.
6. **Answer exactly what is asked** — no more, no less.

### Vocabulary Questions:
- Read the sentence the word appears in
- Find what role the word plays (noun/verb/adjective)
- Identify a word that can replace it without changing the meaning
- Give the meaning as it is used in this context, not every possible meaning

---

## 3.4 Identifying the Main Idea

**Main idea** = the single most important point the writer makes about the topic.
- Usually in the first sentence (topic sentence) of the paragraph
- If not stated directly: ask "What is this paragraph mainly about?"
- Different from the **topic** (which is just a noun/phrase)

**Thesis statement:** The central argument of the whole passage, usually in the introduction.

---

## 3.5 Tone and Mood

**Tone** = the writer's attitude towards the subject or reader.
**Mood** = the feeling created in the reader.

| Tone words | Meaning |
|-----------|---------|
| Sarcastic | Mocking; saying the opposite of what is meant |
| Nostalgic | Longing for the past |
| Persuasive | Trying to convince |
| Critical | Finding fault |
| Sympathetic | Showing compassion |
| Ironic | Saying one thing but meaning another |
| Objective | Neutral, fact-based |

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** Read: "The government's decision to ban street trading may seem harsh, but it is a necessary step towards creating an orderly, hygienic city environment."

**Q:** What is the writer's attitude to the ban?
**A:** The writer is **supportive/approving** of the ban. Despite acknowledging it may seem harsh, the use of "necessary step" shows the writer believes the policy is justified.

---

**Example 2 (WAEC type):** "The prodigious scientist published over 200 papers in his lifetime." What does "prodigious" mean as used here?

**A:** The word "prodigious" in this context means **remarkably productive / impressively prolific** — the quantity (200 papers) confirms the idea of extraordinary output.

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** comprehension tests all six question types. Vocabulary and inference questions carry the most marks and cause the most errors.

> Always answer vocabulary questions based on **context use**, not dictionary definition. "Run" can mean jog, manage, or operate — the passage context decides.

> **Common trap:** Quoting long passages as an answer. Extract and paraphrase — WAEC rewards understanding, not copying.

> **Tone vs Mood:** Tone is the WRITER'S attitude. Mood is the READER'S feeling. They can differ.

# TOPIC 4: Essay Writing — Types, Structure and Language
**Topic ID:** SSS-ENG-003-01

## 4.1 Types of Essay

| Type | Purpose | Key features |
|------|---------|-------------|
| **Narrative** | Tell a story | Plot, characters, setting; first or third person; vivid description |
| **Descriptive** | Describe a person, place, or event | Sensory details; spatial or time order; figurative language |
| **Expository** | Explain or inform | Clear facts; logical order; impersonal tone; examples |
| **Argumentative/Persuasive** | Convince the reader of a position | Thesis; supporting arguments; counterargument; evidence |
| **Discursive** | Discuss different sides of an issue | Balanced view; both sides presented |
| **Letter/Formal composition** | Formal communication | Correct format; appropriate register |

---

## 4.2 Essay Structure

Every essay must have **three parts:**

### Introduction:
- **Hook:** Engaging opening sentence (quote, question, surprising fact, anecdote)
- **Background:** Brief context for the topic
- **Thesis statement:** Your main argument or the purpose of the essay

### Body Paragraphs (usually 3–5):
Each paragraph follows the **PEEL structure:**
- **P — Point:** One main idea (topic sentence)
- **E — Evidence:** Supporting detail, example, or quote
- **E — Explanation:** Explain how the evidence supports your point
- **L — Link:** Connect back to the thesis/question

### Conclusion:
- Restate the thesis in fresh words
- Summarise the main points briefly
- Closing thought (prediction, call to action, reflection)

---

## 4.3 Language Features

### Cohesion and Coherence:
A good essay flows logically. Use **linking words:**

| Function | Linking words |
|----------|-------------|
| Addition | furthermore, in addition, moreover, also |
| Contrast | however, on the other hand, although, nevertheless |
| Cause/Effect | therefore, consequently, as a result, hence |
| Example | for instance, for example, to illustrate |
| Sequence | first, next, then, finally, subsequently |
| Conclusion | in conclusion, to summarise, ultimately |

### Register:
- **Formal essays:** Avoid contractions (use "do not" not "don't"), slang, and first person (unless narrative)
- **Narrative essays:** First person ("I") is expected; vivid, personal language

---

## 4.4 WAEC Essay Marking Scheme

WAEC marks essays on:
| Component | Marks |
|-----------|-------|
| Content (ideas and relevance) | 20 |
| Organisation (structure, coherence) | 10 |
| Expression (vocabulary, sentence variety) | 10 |
| Mechanical accuracy (grammar, spelling, punctuation) | 10 |
| **Total** | **50** |

---

## ✏️ Worked Example

**Example (WAEC type):** Write an argumentative essay on: "Social media has done more harm than good." (Approximately 450 words)

**Outline (plan before writing):**
- Intro: Hook about social media's rapid growth → Thesis: Despite some benefits, social media's negative impacts on mental health, academic performance, and social cohesion outweigh its advantages.
- Para 1: Mental health — cyberbullying, anxiety, unrealistic comparisons
- Para 2: Academic performance — distraction, reduced concentration spans
- Para 3: Spread of misinformation — fake news, political manipulation
- Counterargument: Connectivity, business, activism → BUT these benefits don't outweigh harms
- Conclusion: Governments and individuals must regulate use

**Key phrases for argumentative essays:**
- "It is undeniable that..."
- "Critics argue that..., however..."
- "Evidence strongly suggests..."
- "In the final analysis..."

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** Essay: Write 400–450 words. Plan first (5 minutes). Each paragraph = one main point. Do not begin with "I want to write about..."

> Content carries the most marks (20/50) — stay on topic; be relevant; develop your points fully.

> **Common trap:** Writing only one side in a discursive essay. A discursive essay must present BOTH sides before reaching a conclusion.

> **Narrative tip:** Show, don't just tell. Instead of "She was sad," write "Tears rolled silently down her cheeks as she stared at the empty chair."

# TOPIC 5: Parts of Speech — Grammar, Usage and Sentence Construction
**Topic ID:** SSS-ENG-004-01

## 5.1 The Eight Parts of Speech

| Part of speech | Function | Example |
|---------------|---------|---------|
| **Noun** | Names a person, place, thing, or idea | Lagos, courage, table |
| **Pronoun** | Replaces a noun | he, she, they, it, who |
| **Verb** | Action or state of being | run, think, is, become |
| **Adjective** | Modifies a noun or pronoun | tall, three, beautiful |
| **Adverb** | Modifies verb, adjective, or another adverb | quickly, very, yesterday |
| **Preposition** | Shows relationship between nouns | in, on, at, under, beside |
| **Conjunction** | Joins words, phrases, or clauses | and, but, although, because |
| **Interjection** | Expresses sudden emotion | Wow! Ouch! Hurray! |

---

## 5.2 Nouns — Types and Properties

| Type | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| Proper noun | Names a specific person, place, or thing | Emeka, Abuja, Monday |
| Common noun | General names | table, city, teacher |
| Abstract noun | Non-physical concepts | love, freedom, justice |
| Concrete noun | Physically perceptible | chair, water, fire |
| Collective noun | Group of similar things | a flock of birds, a pride of lions |
| Countable noun | Can be counted | book (books), chair (chairs) |
| Uncountable noun | Cannot be counted | water, air, advice, information |

**Forming plurals:** Most nouns add -s (chairs); nouns ending in -s, -sh, -ch, -x add -es (boxes); irregular plurals (man → men, mouse → mice, datum → data).

---

## 5.3 Verbs — Tenses and Forms

| Tense | Form | Example |
|-------|------|---------|
| Simple present | V / V+s | I write / He writes |
| Simple past | V+ed or irregular | I wrote / He ran |
| Simple future | will + V | I will write |
| Present continuous | am/is/are + V+ing | I am writing |
| Past continuous | was/were + V+ing | I was writing |
| Present perfect | has/have + past participle | I have written |
| Past perfect | had + past participle | I had written |
| Future perfect | will have + past participle | I will have written |

**Transitive verbs:** Take a direct object — "She wrote a letter."
**Intransitive verbs:** No direct object — "She wept."

---

## 5.4 Adjectives — Types

| Type | Example |
|------|---------|
| Descriptive | beautiful, large, cold |
| Quantitative | many, few, some, enough |
| Demonstrative | this, that, these, those |
| Possessive | my, your, his, their |
| Interrogative | which, what, whose |
| Numeral | first, three, double |

**Comparison of adjectives:**
- Positive: tall
- Comparative: taller (add -er or "more")
- Superlative: tallest (add -est or "most")

---

## 5.5 Adverbs

Adverbs modify verbs ("She runs quickly"), adjectives ("very beautiful"), or other adverbs ("quite slowly").

**Types:** manner (slowly), time (yesterday), place (here), frequency (always), degree (very)

**Common error:** Using adjective instead of adverb after a verb.
- Wrong: "She sings beautiful."
- Correct: "She sings beautifully."

---

## 5.6 Conjunctions — Types

| Type | Description | Examples |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Coordinating** | Joins equal grammatical units | for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so (FANBOYS) |
| **Subordinating** | Joins a dependent clause to a main clause | because, although, if, when, unless |
| **Correlative** | Used in pairs | both...and, either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also |

---

## 5.7 Common Grammar Rules for WAEC

| Rule | Correct usage |
|------|--------------|
| Subject-verb agreement | "The boys run" (plural); "The boy runs" (singular) |
| Pronoun-antecedent agreement | "Everyone should bring his/her book" |
| Correct tense sequence | "He said that he was tired" (not "is tired") |
| Double negatives | Avoid: "I didn't see nothing" → "I didn't see anything" |
| Correct preposition | "Aim AT a target" not "aim TO a target" |

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** Identify the parts of speech of each underlined word:
"The **young** students **quickly** completed their **difficult** assignment."

**Solution:**
- **young** — adjective (modifies "students")
- **quickly** — adverb (modifies "completed")
- **difficult** — adjective (modifies "assignment")

---

**Example 2 (WAEC type — Error correction):** Correct the errors: "Each of the boys have submitted their assignment."

**Solution:**
"Each of the boys **has** submitted **his** assignment."
- "Each" is singular → verb must be singular "has"
- Pronoun must agree: "his" (or "his/her")

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** Section B tests grammar — expect questions on tense, subject-verb agreement, pronoun reference, word classes, and error identification/correction.

> **JAMB** tests identification of parts of speech and correction of grammatical errors.

> **Common trap:** "None" is usually singular. "None of the students IS present" — though "are" is increasingly accepted in informal use.

> **Collective nouns:** Usually take singular verbs in British English: "The committee has decided." WAEC follows British English conventions.

# TOPIC 6: Literary Devices — Figures of Speech in Literature and Language
**Topic ID:** SSS-ENG-005-01

## 6.1 Definition

**Literary devices** (also called figures of speech or rhetorical devices) are techniques writers use to create effects, convey meaning vividly, and enhance the impact of their writing.

---

## 6.2 Major Figures of Speech

### Comparison Devices:

| Device | Definition | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| **Simile** | Comparing two unlike things using "like" or "as" | "Her voice is like sweet music" |
| **Metaphor** | Stating two unlike things are the same (no "like"/"as") | "Life is a race" |
| **Personification** | Giving human qualities to non-human things | "The sun smiled down on us" |
| **Apostrophe** | Addressing an absent person or abstract idea directly | "O Death, where is thy sting?" |

### Sound Devices:

| Device | Definition | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| **Alliteration** | Repetition of initial consonant sounds | "Peter Piper picked a peck..." |
| **Assonance** | Repetition of vowel sounds within words | "The rain in Spain stays mainly in the plain" |
| **Onomatopoeia** | Words that imitate sounds | buzz, hiss, crash, sizzle |
| **Rhyme** | Repetition of similar end sounds | "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?" |
| **Rhythm** | Regular pattern of stressed/unstressed syllables | Any metered poetry |

### Emphasis Devices:

| Device | Definition | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| **Hyperbole** | Extreme exaggeration | "I've told you a million times!" |
| **Understatement** | Making something seem less significant than it is | "The earthquake caused a bit of damage." |
| **Irony** | Saying one thing but meaning another | "How lovely!" (said about an ugly painting) |
| **Sarcasm** | Bitter/cutting irony | "Oh, great job!" (after a mistake) |
| **Oxymoron** | Two contradictory terms together | "deafening silence," "sweet sorrow" |
| **Paradox** | A statement that seems contradictory but reveals a truth | "I must be cruel to be kind" |

### Structural Devices:

| Device | Definition | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| **Repetition** | Deliberate repeating of a word/phrase | "We shall fight, we shall never surrender" |
| **Anaphora** | Repetition at the beginning of successive clauses | "I have a dream... I have a dream..." |
| **Antithesis** | Contrasting ideas in parallel structure | "To err is human; to forgive, divine" |
| **Chiasmus** | Reversed grammatical structure | "Ask not what your country can do for you..." |

---

## 6.3 Analysing Literary Devices

When asked to comment on a literary device, always:
1. **Identify** the device (name it)
2. **Quote** the relevant part of the text
3. **Explain the effect** — what it does, creates, suggests, or implies

**Formula:** The writer uses [device] in "____" to [effect/purpose].

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** Identify the figure of speech and explain its effect in: "The classroom was a battlefield of ideas."

**Solution:**
This is a **metaphor**. The classroom is directly compared to a battlefield. The effect is to convey the intensity and competition of ideas being debated — suggesting that students argued passionately and forcefully, just as soldiers fight vigorously.

---

**Example 2 (WAEC type):** Comment on the literary device in: "The stars danced playfully in the midnight sky."

**Solution:**
This is **personification** — stars are given the human quality of dancing. It creates a vivid, joyful image of the night sky and gives it a lively, animated quality, making the scene feel magical and celebratory.

---

**Example 3 (JAMB type):** "The news hit him like a thunderbolt." Identify the device.

**Solution:** **Simile** — the word "like" signals the comparison between the impact of the news and a thunderbolt, conveying sudden shock.

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** Literature Paper 1 tests figures of speech in prose, poetry, and drama extracts. Always give the EFFECT, not just the name.

> **JAMB** tests identification of devices (multiple choice) — often you must distinguish between similar devices.

> **Common trap:** Confusing simile and metaphor. Simile uses "like" or "as." Metaphor directly equates two things with no comparison word.

> **Common trap:** Saying personification "makes the text interesting." Be specific — say what QUALITY is being given and what EFFECT this creates.

> Sarcasm is a type of irony — all sarcasm is ironic, but not all irony is sarcastic. WAEC distinguishes between them.
