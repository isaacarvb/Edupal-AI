## Atomic Structure and Bonding

### Atomic Structure and the Periodic Table
**Class:** SSS1  |  **Topic ID:** `SSS-CHM-001-01`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Describe the structure of an atom including subatomic particles
- Explain electronic configuration and orbital notation
- Describe periodic trends across periods and down groups
- Relate the position of an element to its properties
- Explain the significance of the periodic table in chemistry

#### 📖 Notes
- Atomic structure: protons (positive, in nucleus), neutrons (neutral, in nucleus), electrons (negative, in shells)
- Proton number (atomic number Z): defines the element; number of protons = number of electrons in neutral atom
- Mass number A: protons + neutrons; relative atomic mass considers isotopes
- Electronic configuration using shells: 2, 8, 8, 2... (e.g. Na: 2, 8, 1)
- Orbital notation: s, p, d, f orbitals; Aufbau principle, Hund's rule, Pauli exclusion principle
- Isotopes: same Z, different A; same chemical properties; different physical properties (density, mass)
- Periodic table: arranged in order of increasing atomic number; periods (rows) and groups (columns)
- Periods: elements across a period have same number of electron shells
- Groups: elements in same group have same number of valence electrons → similar chemical properties
- Periodic trends across a period (left to right): atomic radius decreases; ionisation energy increases; electronegativity increases; metallic character decreases
- Periodic trends down a group: atomic radius increases; ionisation energy decreases; metallic character increases
- Important groups: Group I (alkali metals), Group II (alkaline earth metals), Group VII (halogens), Group VIII/0 (noble gases)

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Write electron configurations for the first 20 elements and place them correctly on a blank periodic table
2. Explain why Na (Group I) is more reactive than Mg (Group II) using ionisation energy data
3. Compare properties of Group I elements (Li, Na, K, Rb): reactions with water, melting points, density trends

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> WAEC tests electronic configuration, periodic trends, isotopes and the significance of group/period position
> JAMB tests quick electron configuration, trend identification (which element has highest/lowest ionisation energy), and isotope mass calculations
> Trap: confusing period (rows — same number of shells) with group (columns — same number of valence electrons)

### Chemical Bonding
**Class:** SSS1  |  **Topic ID:** `SSS-CHM-001-02`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Explain ionic, covalent and metallic bonding
- Draw electron dot (Lewis) structures for ionic and covalent compounds
- Describe properties of ionic, covalent and metallic substances
- Explain hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces
- Relate bond type to physical properties (melting point, conductivity, solubility)

#### 📖 Notes
- Ionic bonding: transfer of electrons from metal to non-metal; electrostatic attraction between ions; forms giant ionic lattice
- Ionic compound properties: high melting point; conducts electricity when molten or dissolved; hard and brittle
- Covalent bonding: sharing of electron pairs between non-metal atoms
- Types: single bond (1 shared pair), double bond (2 shared pairs), triple bond (3 shared pairs)
- Covalent structures: simple molecular (H₂O, CO₂, NH₃ — low m.p., poor conductors) vs. giant covalent (diamond, SiO₂ — very high m.p.)
- Dative (coordinate) covalent bond: both electrons donated by one atom (e.g. NH₃ → BF₃)
- Metallic bonding: lattice of positive ions in sea of delocalized electrons; explains conductivity, malleability, high m.p.
- Electronegativity and bond polarity: polar covalent bond (ΔEN 0.4–1.7); ionic bond (ΔEN > 1.7); non-polar (ΔEN < 0.4)
- Hydrogen bonding: strong dipole-dipole interaction involving H bonded to N, O or F; explains anomalous properties of water
- Van der Waals forces (London dispersion): temporary dipole interactions; increases with molecular size; explains alkane boiling points
- Lewis (dot-and-cross) diagrams: show valence electrons; ensure each atom achieves noble gas configuration
- VSEPR theory: electron pairs repel each other; used to predict molecular shape (linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, pyramidal, bent)

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Draw Lewis structures for NaCl (ionic), CO₂, H₂O, NH₃ and CH₄ (covalent); predict molecular shapes using VSEPR
2. Compare melting points of NaCl, H₂O, CO₂ and diamond — relate to bond type and intermolecular forces
3. Investigate solubility: test NaCl, sugar, paraffin wax, iodine in water and in hexane; conclude 'like dissolves like'

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> WAEC tests Lewis diagrams, identification of bond types, dative bonds, and properties linked to structure
> JAMB tests bond type identification from electronegativities, molecular shape from VSEPR, and hydrogen bonding explanation
> Trap: students draw Lewis structures without checking the octet rule is satisfied for all atoms except hydrogen (which needs only 2 electrons)

## Chemical Reactions and Stoichiometry

### Mole Concept and Stoichiometry
**Class:** SSS1  |  **Topic ID:** `SSS-CHM-002-01`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Define the mole and Avogadro's number
- Calculate molar mass of compounds and convert between moles, mass and particles
- Write and balance chemical equations
- Apply stoichiometry to calculate masses, volumes and concentrations in reactions
- Determine empirical and molecular formulae from data

#### 📖 Notes
- Mole: the amount of substance containing 6.02 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's number, NA)
- Molar mass: mass of one mole of substance in g/mol; numerically equal to Ar or Mr
- Conversions: n = m/M (n in mol, m in g, M in g/mol); n = N/NA (N = number of particles)
- Balancing equations: conservation of mass (atoms of each element same on both sides); conservation of charge (for ionic equations)
- Molar ratios: coefficients in balanced equation give molar ratio of reactants and products
- Molar volume: 1 mole of any gas occupies 22.4 dm³ at STP (0°C, 1 atm) or 24 dm³ at RTP (25°C, 1 atm)
- Gas stoichiometry: n = V/22.4 at STP; Gay-Lussac's law for gas volume ratios
- Concentration: C = n/V (mol/dm³ or mol/L); C = m/(M × V) directly
- Limiting reagent: the reactant that is completely used up; determines product yield
- Percent yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100%
- Empirical formula: simplest whole-number ratio of atoms; found from mass/percentage composition
- Molecular formula: actual number of atoms; MF = (EF)n where n = Mr(compound) / Mr(EF)

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Calculate the mass of CaCO₃ required to produce 11.2 dm³ of CO₂ at STP using the equation CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂
2. From combustion data (0.3g compound gives 0.44g CO₂ and 0.18g H₂O), find the empirical formula
3. Perform a titration experiment: determine the concentration of NaOH using standard HCl solution; calculate n = CV at equivalence point

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> WAEC consistently tests molar mass calculations, balancing equations, stoichiometry and concentration calculations — show mole map working
> JAMB tests empirical formula determination, limiting reagent identification, and gas volume calculations
> Trap: not multiplying concentration by volume in litres (not cm³); 1 dm³ = 1 L = 1000 cm³ — convert before using C = n/V

### Kinetics, Equilibrium and Energetics
**Class:** SSS2  |  **Topic ID:** `SSS-CHM-002-02`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Explain the factors affecting rate of reaction using collision theory
- Define dynamic equilibrium and apply Le Chatelier's principle
- Calculate and interpret equilibrium constants Kc and Kp
- Distinguish between exothermic and endothermic reactions with energy profiles
- Apply Hess's law to calculate enthalpy changes

#### 📖 Notes
- Rate of reaction: change in concentration/volume/mass per unit time; units: mol/(dm³·s)
- Collision theory: reaction occurs when particles collide with correct orientation and energy ≥ activation energy (Ea)
- Factors affecting rate: concentration (more particles → more collisions), temperature (higher T → more particles have ≥ Ea), surface area, catalyst (lowers Ea), light
- Dynamic equilibrium: forward rate = reverse rate; concentrations constant; only in closed systems
- Le Chatelier's Principle: if a stress is applied, the equilibrium shifts to oppose the stress
- Effect of increasing concentration: shift toward side that uses the added substance
- Effect of increasing pressure: shift toward side with fewer gas moles
- Effect of increasing temperature: shift toward endothermic direction (uses heat)
- Equilibrium constant Kc = [products]^x / [reactants]^y (concentration terms, using stoichiometric powers)
- Exothermic reaction: ΔH < 0; energy released to surroundings; products at lower energy than reactants
- Endothermic reaction: ΔH > 0; energy absorbed from surroundings
- Enthalpy change ΔH: ΔH = H(products) − H(reactants)
- Hess's Law: total enthalpy change is independent of the path taken; ΔH(route A) = ΔH(route B)
- Bond enthalpy: energy to break/form one mole of a bond; ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed)

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Investigate effect of concentration on rate: react marble chips with HCl at 0.5M, 1M and 2M; measure gas volume every 30 seconds
2. Apply Le Chatelier's principle to the Haber process (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃): explain why 450°C and 200 atm are used industrially
3. Calculate ΔH for CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O using bond energies; compare with ΔH from Hess's law cycle

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> WAEC tests collision theory, Le Chatelier's principle (explain shift with reason), Hess's law and energy profile diagrams
> JAMB tests Kc expressions, Le Chatelier effects on equilibrium, and exo/endothermic identification from ΔH sign
> Trap: students shift equilibrium in the wrong direction when temperature increases — temperature increases always favour endothermic side

## Organic Chemistry

### Hydrocarbons — Alkanes, Alkenes and Alkynes
**Class:** SSS2  |  **Topic ID:** `SSS-CHM-003-01`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Name and draw structures of alkanes, alkenes and alkynes using IUPAC nomenclature
- Distinguish between saturated and unsaturated hydrocarbons
- Describe and write equations for combustion, substitution and addition reactions
- Explain isomerism in organic compounds
- Describe industrial importance of crude oil and its fractions

#### 📖 Notes
- Homologous series: a family of compounds with the same general formula, functional group and similar chemical properties; differ by -CH₂-
- Alkanes: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂; single bonds only (saturated); general formula; methane, ethane, propane, butane…
- Alkenes: CₙH₂ₙ; one C=C double bond (unsaturated); ethene, propene, butene…
- Alkynes: CₙH₂ₙ₋₂; one C≡C triple bond; ethyne (acetylene), propyne…
- IUPAC naming: identify longest chain (parent), number from nearest branch/functional group, name substituents
- Structural isomers: same molecular formula, different structural arrangement (e.g. butane and methylpropane C₄H₁₀)
- Combustion: complete (excess O₂) → CO₂ + H₂O; incomplete (limited O₂) → CO + H₂O or soot (C)
- Substitution reactions of alkanes: with halogens in presence of UV light (free radical mechanism)
- Addition reactions of alkenes: Br₂ (test for C=C — decolourises bromine water), H₂ (hydrogenation), H₂O (hydration → alcohols), HX (Markovnikov's rule)
- Polymerisation: addition polymerisation from alkene monomers (polythene from ethene)
- Crude oil fractionation: refinery gas, petrol/gasoline, naphtha, kerosene, diesel, fuel oil, bitumen — separated by boiling point
- Cracking: breaking long-chain alkanes into shorter chains and alkenes; thermal or catalytic

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Draw and name all structural isomers of pentane (C₅H₁₂) and butene (C₄H₈)
2. Test a sample of paraffin (alkane) and ethene gas with bromine water — observe and explain the difference
3. Model the structure of methane (tetrahedral, 109.5°) and ethene (planar, 120°) using clay/toothpicks, relating shape to hybridisation

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> WAEC tests IUPAC naming, reaction types and equations, isomer drawing, and crude oil fractions
> JAMB tests structure identification, combustion equations (balancing), bromine water test, and addition product prediction
> Trap: drawing incorrect structures for isomers; forgetting that alkenes decolourise bromine water but alkanes do not (under normal light)

### Functional Group Chemistry — Alcohols, Carboxylic Acids and Esters
**Class:** SSS3  |  **Topic ID:** `SSS-CHM-003-02`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Name and draw structures of alcohols, carboxylic acids and esters
- Describe chemical reactions of alcohols (oxidation, dehydration, esterification)
- Write and explain esterification and saponification reactions
- Describe properties and uses of each class of compounds
- Explain condensation and addition polymerisation

#### 📖 Notes
- Alcohols: -OH functional group; CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH; methanol, ethanol, propanol
- Classification: primary (1°), secondary (2°), tertiary (3°) — based on how many carbons attached to C–OH
- Ethanol reactions: combustion (fuel); oxidation by K₂Cr₂O₇ (ethanol → ethanal → ethanoic acid); dehydration with H₂SO₄ → ethene; fermentation (yeast, 37°C, anaerobic)
- Carboxylic acids: -COOH group; ethanoic acid (vinegar); propanoic acid, butanoic acid
- Carboxylic acid properties: weak acids; pH 3–6; react with metals, metal hydroxides, carbonates to give salts + CO₂
- Esters: -COO- linkage; formed by reaction of carboxylic acid + alcohol with H₂SO₄ catalyst; named alkyl alkanoate
- Esterification: CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O (reversible); uses: flavours, perfumes
- Saponification: ester + NaOH → soap (sodium salt of fatty acid) + glycerol; basis of soap making
- Fats and oils: esters of glycerol and long-chain fatty acids; saturated (solid, animal) vs. unsaturated (liquid, plant)
- Soap: sodium/potassium salts of long-chain fatty acids; hydrophilic head, hydrophobic tail
- Polymers: nylon (condensation polymerisation — amide bond, loses H₂O); PVC, polythene (addition from monomers)
- Amino acids: bifunctional (-NH₂ and -COOH); proteins are polypeptide chains

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Prepare ethyl ethanoate in the lab: mix ethanol + glacial ethanoic acid + H₂SO₄, warm gently; identify product by smell
2. Test ethanol with acidified K₂Cr₂O₇: observe colour change from orange to green; relate to oxidation to ethanal/ethanoic acid
3. Make a polymer model: link paper clips as monomers to represent addition polymerisation of ethene to polythene

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> WAEC tests esterification equation writing, functional group identification, alcohol classification, and soap chemistry
> JAMB tests ester naming from acid + alcohol reactants, saponification, fermentation equation, and dehydration of alcohols
> Trap: alcohol naming — the parent chain must include the C bearing the OH group; methanol has 1 carbon total, ethanol has 2

## Electrochemistry

### Electrolysis and Electrochemical Cells
**Class:** SSS2  |  **Topic ID:** `SSS-CHM-004-01`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Distinguish between electrolytic and electrochemical (voltaic) cells
- Identify products at the electrodes in electrolysis of common electrolytes
- Apply Faraday's laws of electrolysis in calculations
- Explain the principles of a simple cell, Daniel cell and the lead-acid accumulator
- Describe industrial applications of electrolysis

#### 📖 Notes
- Electrolysis: decomposition of a compound by passing electricity through its molten or dissolved form
- Electrolyte: ionic compound that conducts electricity when dissolved or molten
- Electrodes: anode (+) — oxidation occurs; cathode (−) — reduction occurs
- Electrolysis of dilute H₂SO₄: cathode → H₂ (2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂); anode → O₂ (2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻)
- Electrolysis of concentrated NaCl (brine): cathode → H₂; anode → Cl₂; used industrially (chlor-alkali process)
- Electrolysis of molten NaCl: cathode → Na metal; anode → Cl₂
- Selective discharge: ions discharged preferentially based on position in electrochemical series
- Faraday's 1st Law: mass deposited ∝ charge passed; Q = It
- Faraday's 2nd Law: mass deposited ∝ 1/charge of ion (or M/z ratio)
- Faraday's constant F = 96,500 C/mol; m = (MIt)/(zF)
- Electroplating: thin layer of metal deposited on object using electrolysis; applications: silver-plating, chromium-plating, zinc-plating (galvanising)
- Voltaic/Electrochemical cell: converts chemical energy to electrical energy; e.g. Daniel cell (Zn/ZnSO₄||CuSO₄/Cu)
- EMF and electrode potential: standard hydrogen electrode as reference; electrochemical series
- Lead-acid accumulator: rechargeable battery; Pb(−) and PbO₂(+) in H₂SO₄; 2V per cell; 6 cells = 12V car battery

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Electroplate a copper key with silver using a silver nitrate solution; weigh before and after; calculate charge used
2. Measure the EMF of a Daniel cell; reverse the electrodes and explain why current now flows in opposite direction
3. Calculate the mass of copper deposited if 2A flows for 30 minutes through CuSO₄ solution (M(Cu)=64, z=2)

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> WAEC tests electrode product identification, Faraday's law calculations, and electroplating — always show Q = It then m = MQ/zF working
> JAMB tests selective discharge, brine electrolysis products, and EMF calculation from electrochemical series
> Trap: confusing anode (+ electrode, oxidation) with cathode (− electrode, reduction) — OILRIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain
