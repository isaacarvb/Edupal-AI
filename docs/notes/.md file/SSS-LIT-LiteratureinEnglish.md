# TOPIC 1: Poetry — Forms, Devices and Analysis
**Topic ID:** SSS-LIT-001-01

## 1.1 Definition of Poetry

**Poetry** is a form of literary expression that uses language arranged in concentrated, rhythmic, and often figurative form to evoke emotion, create imagery, and communicate meaning — often in ways that exceed what plain prose can achieve.

---

## 1.2 Elements of Poetry

| Element | Definition |
|---------|-----------|
| **Theme** | The central idea or message of the poem |
| **Tone** | The poet's attitude toward the subject (e.g., sad, celebratory, bitter) |
| **Mood** | The emotional atmosphere created for the reader |
| **Diction** | The choice of words; formal or informal, simple or complex |
| **Imagery** | Descriptive language that appeals to the senses |
| **Voice** | Who is speaking in the poem (the speaker/persona) |
| **Rhythm** | The pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables |
| **Rhyme scheme** | The pattern of end-rhymes (ABAB, AABB, ABCABC, etc.) |
| **Metre** | A regular pattern of rhythm (e.g., iambic pentameter — 10 syllables, 5 iambic feet) |

---

## 1.3 Types of Poetry

| Type | Description | Features |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Lyric poetry** | Expresses personal emotions | Short; first person; subjective |
| **Narrative poetry** | Tells a story | Characters, plot, setting |
| **Dramatic monologue** | Speaker addresses a silent listener | Single voice; dramatic revelation |
| **Epic** | Long narrative poem about heroic deeds | Grand scale; gods and heroes |
| **Ballad** | Narrative poem meant to be sung | Short stanzas; refrain |
| **Ode** | Formal poem of praise | Elevated tone; complex structure |
| **Elegy** | Poem of mourning for the dead | Sorrowful tone; meditative |
| **Sonnet** | 14-line poem | Petrarchan or Shakespearean structure |

---

## 1.4 How to Analyse a Poem (SMILE Method)

- **S — Structure:** How is the poem organised? (stanzas, lines, shape)
- **M — Meaning:** What is the poem literally about? What is its deeper/thematic meaning?
- **I — Imagery and language:** What figures of speech and sensory images are used?
- **L — Language/Diction:** What types of words does the poet choose? What is the effect?
- **E — Effect:** What is the overall effect on the reader? What emotions are evoked?

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** Read this extract and comment on TWO poetic devices used:

*"Death, be not proud, though some have called thee*
*Mighty and dreadful, for thou art not so..."*
(John Donne, "Holy Sonnet 10")

**Solution:**
1. **Apostrophe** — The poet directly addresses Death ("Death, be not proud...") as if it were a person present. This is an apostrophe — addressing an abstraction. The effect is to challenge and confront death, reducing its power by treating it as something that can be reasoned with.

2. **Irony/Paradox** — Donne calls Death "Mighty and dreadful" but immediately states "thou art not so." This ironic reversal undermines the conventional fear of death, suggesting it has less power than people believe.

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** Poetry questions require: (a) identification of devices with quotations from the poem, (b) explanation of the effect of each device, (c) commentary on theme and tone.

> **JAMB** tests identification of devices in short extracts and the meaning of specific lines.

> **Common trap:** Never just NAME a device — always explain the EFFECT. "The poet uses alliteration" earns 0 marks. "The alliteration in 'bleak and bare' creates a harsh, repetitive sound that reinforces the feeling of desolation" earns full marks.

# TOPIC 2: Drama — Structure, Types and Analysis
**Topic ID:** SSS-LIT-002-01

## 2.1 Definition of Drama

**Drama** is a form of literature written for performance. It presents conflict through characters, dialogue, and action. Unlike prose or poetry, drama is meant to be SEEN AND HEARD on stage.

---

## 2.2 Types of Drama

| Type | Definition | Key features |
|------|-----------|-------------|
| **Tragedy** | Serious play ending in the downfall or death of the protagonist | Noble hero; fatal flaw (hamartia); catharsis |
| **Comedy** | Light-hearted play ending happily | Humour, wit, mistaken identity; usually ends in marriage/reconciliation |
| **Tragicomedy** | Combines tragic and comic elements | Elements of both; neither pure tragedy nor comedy |
| **Melodrama** | Sensationalised drama with clear hero and villain | Exaggerated emotions; simple morality |
| **Farce** | Broad comedy; exaggerated situations | Physical comedy; absurdity |
| **Satire** | Drama that mocks social/political issues | Irony; ridicule of vice or folly |

---

## 2.3 Structure of a Play

**Freytag's Pyramid (5-act structure):**
1. **Exposition** — Introduction of setting, characters, and background conflict
2. **Rising action** — Events that build tension and develop the conflict
3. **Climax** — The turning point; highest tension
4. **Falling action** — Events after the climax leading toward resolution
5. **Dénouement (Resolution)** — The conflict is resolved; loose ends tied up

---

## 2.4 Dramatic Devices

| Device | Definition | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| **Soliloquy** | Character speaks thoughts aloud alone on stage | Hamlet's "To be or not to be..." |
| **Aside** | Character speaks to audience; other characters cannot hear | Character reveals secret motive |
| **Dramatic irony** | Audience knows something character(s) does not | In Oedipus: audience knows Oedipus IS the killer |
| **Foreshadowing** | Hinting at future events | Ominous signs before a tragedy |
| **Flashback** | Showing past events within present action | Memories revealed in dialogue |
| **Chorus** | Group that comments on action (Greek drama) | Greek tragedy: chorus interprets events |

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** What is dramatic irony? Give ONE example from any play you have studied.

**Solution:**
**Dramatic irony** occurs when the audience (or reader) knows something important that one or more characters on stage do not know. It creates tension, suspense, or a sense of tragic inevitability.

**Example from Sophocles' *Oedipus Rex*:** The audience knows from the beginning that Oedipus is the person who killed his father and married his mother. Oedipus himself does not know this. As he confidently investigates the murder to save Thebes, the audience watches with dread, knowing that every step brings him closer to self-discovery and destruction. This is dramatic irony — the gap between what Oedipus believes and what we know creates immense tension.

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** Drama tests: (a) identification and effect of dramatic devices; (b) character analysis; (c) theme and dramatic significance of specific scenes.

> Always refer to specific ACTS, SCENES, or INCIDENTS from the text rather than speaking generally.

> **Common trap:** A soliloquy is spoken by a character ALONE on stage. An aside is spoken to the audience while OTHER characters are present (but cannot hear). They are different devices.

# TOPIC 3: Prose Fiction — Techniques and Analysis
**Topic ID:** SSS-LIT-003-01

## 3.1 Definition

**Prose fiction** is imaginative narrative writing in the ordinary form of sentences and paragraphs (unlike poetry or drama). The main forms are the **novel** and the **short story**.

---

## 3.2 Elements of Fiction

| Element | Definition |
|---------|-----------|
| **Plot** | The sequence of events; what happens |
| **Setting** | Time and place of the story |
| **Character** | The people in the story |
| **Theme** | The central message or idea the writer explores |
| **Narrator/Point of View** | Who tells the story and from what perspective |
| **Style** | The writer's distinctive use of language |
| **Conflict** | The struggle that drives the plot |

---

## 3.3 Types of Characters

| Type | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Protagonist** | Main character; the story follows them |
| **Antagonist** | Character/force in conflict with the protagonist |
| **Round character** | Complex, fully developed; changes during the story |
| **Flat character** | One-dimensional; simple; does not change |
| **Static character** | Does not change throughout the story |
| **Dynamic character** | Changes significantly during the story |
| **Foil character** | Character who contrasts with another to highlight qualities |

---

## 3.4 Types of Narration

| Point of View | Description | Example |
|--------------|-------------|---------|
| **First person** | Narrator is part of the story; uses "I" | "I walked into the room..." |
| **Third person omniscient** | Narrator knows all characters' thoughts | "She felt afraid, though she didn't show it" |
| **Third person limited** | Knows only one character's thoughts | Follows one character closely |
| **Second person** | Reader as character; uses "you" | Choose-your-own-adventure style |
| **Unreliable narrator** | Narrator's account is biased or mistaken | May have limited information or distorted view |

---

## 3.5 Types of Conflict

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Man vs Man** | Character conflicts with another character |
| **Man vs Society** | Character conflicts with social norms/institutions |
| **Man vs Nature** | Character struggles against natural forces |
| **Man vs Self** | Internal psychological conflict |
| **Man vs Fate/Supernatural** | Character struggles against forces beyond control |

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** Referring to a prose work you have studied, analyse the protagonist's main conflict and how it is resolved.

**Approach (using Chinua Achebe's *Things Fall Apart*):**
**Protagonist:** Okonkwo

**Main conflict:** Okonkwo's central conflict is internal (Man vs Self) combined with Man vs Society. He is driven by a terror of becoming like his weak, failed father Unoka. This obsession with strength and manliness makes him inflexible, violent, and unable to adapt to change. When the British colonisers arrive and destroy Igbo society, Okonkwo cannot adapt — he commits an act of violence that leads to his clan's abandonment of him.

**Resolution:** Tragically, Okonkwo's conflict is never truly resolved. He hangs himself — an act considered deeply shameful in Igbo culture — rather than submit to colonial rule. His death symbolises both personal failure and the destruction of traditional Igbo values by colonialism.

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** prose tests: character analysis, theme development, narrative technique, and significance of specific incidents.

> **Common trap:** Never say a character is "good" or "bad" — describe them in terms of their traits, motivations, and function in the story. WAEC rewards analysis over simple moral judgment.

# TOPIC 4: African Literature and Nigerian Oral Tradition
**Topic ID:** SSS-LIT-004-01

## 4.1 African Literature

**African literature** encompasses literary works written by African authors, reflecting African experiences, concerns, history, and worldview. It covers literature in English, French (Francophone), Portuguese, and African languages.

### Themes in African Literature:

| Theme | Description | Example works |
|-------|-------------|--------------|
| **Colonialism and its aftermath** | Impact of European rule; identity crisis | *Things Fall Apart* (Achebe) |
| **Tradition vs modernity** | Conflict between old and new ways | *The River Between* (Ngugi) |
| **Identity and belonging** | Searching for cultural and personal identity | *Weep Not, Child* (Ngugi) |
| **Social and political corruption** | Critique of post-independence African leadership | *A Man of the People* (Achebe) |
| **The role of women** | Challenging patriarchal structures | *Purple Hibiscus* (Adichie) |
| **Post-colonial memory** | Processing the trauma of colonialism | *Half of a Yellow Sun* (Adichie) |

---

## 4.2 Nigerian Oral Tradition

**Oral tradition** refers to cultural and literary material passed from generation to generation through spoken word rather than writing.

| Genre | Description | Examples |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| **Folktales** | Stories featuring animals or characters with moral lessons | Tortoise and hare stories; Anansi stories |
| **Myths** | Stories explaining natural phenomena or origins | How the sun came to be; creation myths |
| **Legends** | Stories with a historical basis; about heroes or places | Origin of Ile-Ife; Oduduwa's descent |
| **Proverbs** | Short, wise sayings conveying truths | "When the music changes, so does the dance" |
| **Riddles** | Questions that challenge thinking | Common in Igbo, Yoruba, Hausa traditions |
| **Songs** | Work songs, lullabies, praise songs (oriki) | Yoruba oriki; Hausa waka |
| **Dirges** | Songs of mourning | Performed at funerals |

### Features of Nigerian Oral Tradition:
- Strong moral and ethical content
- Use of animals as characters (especially trickster figures like the tortoise)
- Communal participation — audience is active
- Repetition and formulaic phrases
- Rooted in everyday life and natural environment

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** Identify THREE characteristics of the oral tradition as demonstrated in Nigerian folktales.

**Solution:**
1. **Moral instruction** — Folktales invariably end with a lesson or moral. In the tale of the tortoise and the birds, the tortoise's dishonesty leads to his fall — teaching that deception is self-destructive.
2. **Animal characters** — Animals (especially the tortoise as trickster figure, the spider, the hare) represent human traits like cunning, greed, and courage, allowing stories to comment on human behaviour indirectly.
3. **Communal/participatory telling** — In performance, oral stories involve call-and-response, audience repetition of phrases, songs, or refrains — literature is a shared experience, not a solitary one.

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** Literature tests knowledge of African themes and oral tradition alongside set texts.

> Know at least THREE major Nigerian/African writers: Chinua Achebe (Nigeria), Wole Soyinka (Nobel Prize, Nigeria), Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o (Kenya), Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie (Nigeria).

# TOPIC 5: Studying Set Texts — Exam Approach and Close Reading
**Topic ID:** SSS-LIT-005-01

## 5.1 Understanding What WAEC Asks

WAEC Literature in English is examined in three papers:

| Paper | Content | Mark allocation |
|-------|---------|----------------|
| **Paper 1** | Objective (MCQ) — 30 questions | 30 marks |
| **Paper 2** | Drama and Poetry | 60 marks (choose questions) |
| **Paper 3** | Prose | 60 marks (choose questions) |

---

## 5.2 Types of WAEC Literature Questions

| Type | What is required |
|------|----------------|
| **Character analysis** | Describe a character's traits, development, motivation, role |
| **Theme analysis** | Identify a theme; show how it is developed through events and characters |
| **Stylistic/language analysis** | Identify and comment on the effect of specific language or devices |
| **Compare and contrast** | Compare two characters or themes within or across texts |
| **Commentary on an extract** | Close reading of a given passage; identify features and explain effects |
| **Personal response** | Your opinion of a character's actions or the writer's message — supported with evidence |

---

## 5.3 How to Write a Literature Essay

**Introduction:**
- Name the work and author
- Define the question's key terms
- State your thesis (your argument/main point)

**Body paragraphs (use PEEL):**
- **Point** — your topic sentence (one idea)
- **Evidence** — a quote or specific reference to the text
- **Explanation** — explain how the evidence supports your point
- **Link** — connect back to the question/thesis

**Conclusion:**
- Restate your thesis in different words
- Summarise the main points
- Brief closing thought on the wider significance

---

## 5.4 Close Reading of an Extract

When given a passage to analyse, work through these questions:
1. **What is happening?** — Summarise the event or situation
2. **Who is speaking or being described?** — Identify characters
3. **What tone/mood is created?** — What feeling does the language evoke?
4. **What devices are used?** — Identify at least 2–3 with quotations
5. **What is the significance of this passage?** — How does it relate to theme/character development?

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example (WAEC type — character question):** How does Achebe portray Okonkwo as a tragic hero in *Things Fall Apart*?

**Suggested outline:**
- **Introduction:** Define "tragic hero" (a character of high status destroyed by a fatal flaw). State thesis: Okonkwo embodies the classical tragic hero through his strength, his fatal flaw of inflexibility, and his inevitable destruction.
- **Point 1:** Okonkwo's greatness — a celebrated warrior, winner of wrestling bouts, holder of titles. Quote/reference the opening scenes of his fame.
- **Point 2:** His fatal flaw (hamartia) — fear of weakness and resemblance to his father. This causes him to be violent, rigid, and unable to adapt. Quote: his killing of Ikemefuna, his treatment of his son Nwoye.
- **Point 3:** His downfall — colonial arrival shatters the Igbo world; Okonkwo cannot compromise. His suicide is both defiance and defeat.
- **Conclusion:** Okonkwo is tragic because his greatest virtues (strength, pride, resolve) are the very qualities that destroy him when the world changes.

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** tests your ability to engage closely and critically with specific texts. Evidence (quotation or precise reference) is essential — unsupported general statements earn very few marks.

> **Time allocation:** Paper 2 (Drama/Poetry) — 2 hours; Paper 3 (Prose) — 2 hours. Spend no more than 50–55 minutes per question.

> **Common trap:** Many students write plot summaries instead of analysis. The question asks HOW or WHY or TO WHAT EXTENT — not "what happens in the story." Always answer the question asked.
