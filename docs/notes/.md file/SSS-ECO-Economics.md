# TOPIC 1: Fundamental Economic Concepts
**Topic ID:** SSS-ECO-001-01

## 1.1 Definition of Economics

**Economics** is the social science that studies how individuals, firms, and governments make decisions about the allocation of scarce resources to satisfy unlimited human wants.

**Key insight:** Wants are unlimited but resources (land, labour, capital, enterprise) are **scarce**. This scarcity forces choices.

---

## 1.2 Basic Economic Concepts

| Concept | Definition |
|---------|-----------|
| **Scarcity** | Resources are limited relative to wants |
| **Choice** | Selecting one option among alternatives because of scarcity |
| **Opportunity cost** | The value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made |
| **Scale of preference** | A list of wants arranged in order of priority |
| **Economic goods** | Goods that are scarce and have a price |
| **Free goods** | Goods available without cost (e.g., air) |
| **Utility** | The satisfaction obtained from consuming a good or service |
| **Marginal utility** | Additional satisfaction from consuming one more unit |

---

## 1.3 Factors of Production

| Factor | Description | Reward |
|--------|-------------|--------|
| **Land** | All natural resources (soil, minerals, water) | Rent |
| **Labour** | Human physical and mental effort | Wages/Salary |
| **Capital** | Man-made resources used in production (machines, money) | Interest |
| **Enterprise** | The organising factor; takes risk and combines other factors | Profit |

---

## 1.4 Types of Economic Systems

| System | Definition | Key features |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| **Free market (Capitalist)** | Resources owned privately; prices set by market forces | Competition; profit motive; minimal govt. role |
| **Planned (Command/Socialist)** | Government owns and controls all production | Central planning; no price mechanism |
| **Mixed economy** | Both private sector AND government own resources | Nigeria, UK, USA — most common in practice |

---

## 1.5 Division of Labour and Specialisation

**Division of labour:** Breaking production into separate tasks, each done by a different worker.

**Advantages:** Increased productivity; skill development; time savings; use of machinery.
**Disadvantages:** Monotony; dependence on others; risk of structural unemployment.

**Specialisation:** Concentrating on producing goods/services at which you have a comparative advantage.

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** A student uses free time to either study or play football. If he plays football, he foregoes 2 hours of study which could earn him an extra mark. What is the opportunity cost?

**Solution:** The **opportunity cost** of playing football is the **2 hours of study** (and the potential extra marks from it) — the next best alternative that was given up.

---

**Example 2 (JAMB type):** Which of the following is NOT a factor of production?
(a) Land (b) Money (c) Labour (d) Capital

**Solution:** (b) **Money** — Money is a medium of exchange but not a factor of production. Capital refers to real physical assets (machines, tools), not money per se.

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** tests definitions of opportunity cost, factors of production and their rewards, and economic systems.

> **Common trap:** Money is NOT a factor of production. Capital in economics means physical capital (machinery, buildings), not financial capital (money).

# TOPIC 2: Demand and Supply
**Topic ID:** SSS-ECO-002-01

## 2.1 Demand

**Demand** is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to purchase at various prices, over a given period of time.

**Law of Demand:** As price increases, quantity demanded decreases (inverse relationship), *ceteris paribus* (all other things equal).

**Demand curve:** Downward sloping (left to right).

---

## 2.2 Factors Affecting Demand (Determinants — Shift Demand Curve)

| Factor | Increase in demand | Decrease in demand |
|--------|------------------|------------------|
| Consumer income | Income rises (normal goods) | Income falls |
| Price of substitutes | Substitute price rises | Substitute price falls |
| Price of complements | Complement price falls | Complement price rises |
| Consumer tastes | Fashion trend favours it | Fashion moves away |
| Population | Population increases | Population decreases |
| Future price expectations | Expected price rise (buy now) | Expected price fall (wait) |

---

## 2.3 Elasticity of Demand

**Price Elasticity of Demand (PED):**
PED = % change in quantity demanded / % change in price

| Value | Type | Meaning |
|-------|------|---------|
| PED > 1 | Elastic | Consumers sensitive to price changes (luxury goods) |
| PED < 1 | Inelastic | Consumers not very sensitive (necessities, addictive goods) |
| PED = 1 | Unit elastic | 1% price change = 1% quantity change |
| PED = 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Price change has no effect on demand |
| PED = ∞ | Perfectly elastic | Any price increase → zero demand |

---

## 2.4 Supply

**Supply** is the quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to offer for sale at various prices, over a given period.

**Law of Supply:** As price increases, quantity supplied increases (direct relationship), *ceteris paribus*.

**Supply curve:** Upward sloping.

---

## 2.5 Market Equilibrium

**Equilibrium** occurs where demand equals supply — the market clears.

- If price > equilibrium → **surplus** (excess supply) → price falls
- If price < equilibrium → **shortage** (excess demand) → price rises

**Changes in equilibrium:** A shift in demand OR supply changes the equilibrium price and quantity.

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** The price of a good rises from ₦100 to ₦120, and quantity demanded falls from 500 to 400 units. Calculate the price elasticity of demand and interpret the result.

**Solution:**
% change in price = (120−100)/100 × 100 = **20%**
% change in QD = (400−500)/500 × 100 = **−20%**
PED = −20% / 20% = **−1** (absolute value = 1)

Interpretation: **Unit elastic** — a 1% rise in price leads to exactly a 1% fall in quantity demanded. Total revenue remains unchanged.

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** tests drawing/shifting demand and supply curves, elasticity calculations, and equilibrium analysis.

> **Common trap:** A movement ALONG the curve is caused by a change in PRICE. A SHIFT of the curve is caused by any other factor (income, tastes, price of related goods, etc.).

# TOPIC 3: National Income
**Topic ID:** SSS-ECO-003-01

## 3.1 Definition of National Income

**National Income (NI)** is the total monetary value of all goods and services produced in a country over a given period (usually one year), measured at factor cost.

---

## 3.2 Related Concepts

| Concept | Definition |
|---------|-----------|
| **Gross Domestic Product (GDP)** | Total value of goods and services produced WITHIN a country (by residents and non-residents) |
| **Gross National Product (GNP)** | GDP + Net income from abroad |
| **Net National Product (NNP)** | GNP − Depreciation |
| **National Income (NI)** | NNP at factor cost (NNP − indirect taxes + subsidies) |
| **Disposable income** | Income after direct taxes — what individuals actually have to spend or save |
| **Per capita income** | National income ÷ Population — average income per person |

**Key relationships:**
- GNP = GDP + Net factor income from abroad
- NNP = GNP − Capital consumption (depreciation)
- NI = NNP − Indirect taxes + Subsidies

---

## 3.3 Methods of Measuring National Income

| Method | Approach | Formula |
|--------|---------|---------|
| **Output (Product) Method** | Add up value added at each stage of production | Sum of value added in all sectors |
| **Income Method** | Sum all factor incomes | Wages + Rent + Interest + Profit |
| **Expenditure Method** | Sum all expenditures on final goods | C + I + G + (X − M) |

Where: C = Consumption, I = Investment, G = Government spending, X = Exports, M = Imports

---

## 3.4 Uses and Limitations of National Income Figures

**Uses:**
- Compare living standards between countries
- Measure economic growth over time
- Guide government policy

**Limitations:**
- Does not account for income distribution (inequality)
- Ignores non-monetary production (subsistence farming, housework)
- Different price levels between countries
- Does not reflect quality of life (environment, leisure, health)

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** Given: GDP = ₦500bn, Net factor income from abroad = ₦20bn, Depreciation = ₦30bn, Indirect taxes = ₦10bn, Subsidies = ₦5bn. Calculate the National Income.

**Solution:**
GNP = GDP + Net factor income from abroad = 500 + 20 = **₦520bn**
NNP = GNP − Depreciation = 520 − 30 = **₦490bn**
NI = NNP − Indirect taxes + Subsidies = 490 − 10 + 5 = **₦485bn**

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** tests the chain of calculations from GDP to NI, and the three methods of measurement.

> **Common trap:** GDP measures production WITHIN the country's borders regardless of who produces it. GNP measures production by a country's NATIONALS regardless of where they are.

# TOPIC 4: Inflation and Unemployment
**Topic ID:** SSS-ECO-003-02

## 4.1 Inflation

**Inflation** is a sustained rise in the general price level of goods and services in an economy over time.

**Rate of inflation** is measured using the **Consumer Price Index (CPI).**

### Types of Inflation:

| Type | Cause | Description |
|------|-------|-------------|
| **Demand-pull** | Excess demand | "Too much money chasing too few goods" |
| **Cost-push** | Rising production costs | Higher wages, raw material costs push up prices |
| **Imported inflation** | Rising import prices | Higher cost of foreign goods increases domestic prices |
| **Hyperinflation** | Extremely rapid price increase | Currency collapse (e.g., Zimbabwe 2008) |
| **Creeping inflation** | Slow, steady rise | 1–3% per year; considered manageable |

### Effects of Inflation:

| Effect | On... |
|--------|-------|
| Reduces purchasing power | Consumers; fixed-income earners |
| Redistributes income | Debtors gain; creditors lose |
| Encourages speculation | Business investment may fall |
| Reduces exports | Domestic goods become more expensive abroad |
| Uncertainty for businesses | Planning becomes harder |

### Controlling Inflation:

| Tool | How it controls inflation |
|------|--------------------------|
| Increase interest rates | Reduces borrowing and spending |
| Increase taxes | Reduces disposable income; lowers demand |
| Reduce government spending | Lowers aggregate demand |
| Control money supply | Fewer naira chasing goods |

---

## 4.2 Unemployment

**Unemployment** occurs when people who are willing and able to work at the prevailing wage cannot find jobs.

**Unemployment rate = (Unemployed / Labour force) × 100%**

### Types of Unemployment:

| Type | Cause | Example |
|------|-------|---------|
| **Frictional** | Between jobs; transitional | Graduate looking for first job |
| **Structural** | Skills mismatch; industry decline | Coal miners when coal industry declines |
| **Cyclical (demand-deficient)** | Economic recession (low demand) | Job losses during economic downturn |
| **Seasonal** | Seasonal demand fluctuations | Farm labourers in dry season |
| **Technological** | Automation replaces workers | Factory workers replaced by machines |
| **Voluntary** | Person chooses not to work at current wage | — |

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** Explain the difference between demand-pull and cost-push inflation, giving ONE example of each.

**Solution:**
**Demand-pull inflation** occurs when the total demand for goods and services in an economy exceeds the available supply. Example: A large government cash transfer that boosts consumer spending beyond the economy's productive capacity.

**Cost-push inflation** occurs when the costs of production rise, forcing firms to raise prices to maintain profit margins. Example: A sharp increase in oil prices raises transportation and energy costs across all industries, leading firms to charge more for their products.

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** tests types of inflation, causes and effects of inflation/unemployment, and government policies to address them.

> **Common trap:** Structural unemployment cannot be solved by simply increasing demand (that solves cyclical unemployment). Structural unemployment requires retraining workers for new skills.

# TOPIC 5: Money, Banking and Monetary Policy
**Topic ID:** SSS-ECO-004-01

## 5.1 Money

**Money** is any generally accepted medium of exchange, store of value, and measure of value in an economy.

### Functions of Money:
| Function | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Medium of exchange** | Eliminates the need for barter; accepted for all transactions |
| **Measure of value (unit of account)** | Common standard for pricing goods and comparing values |
| **Store of value** | Wealth can be saved and used later |
| **Standard for deferred payment** | Debts can be stated and repaid in money terms |

### Types of Money:
| Type | Examples |
|------|---------|
| **Commodity money** | Gold, silver, cowries (historical) |
| **Fiat money** | Paper notes, coins (no intrinsic value; value by government decree) |
| **Near money** | Cheques, bills of exchange (not legal tender but widely accepted) |
| **Digital money** | Electronic transfers, mobile money |

---

## 5.2 The Central Bank (CBN) and Monetary Policy

**The Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN)** is the apex bank responsible for controlling the money supply and credit in Nigeria.

### Functions of the CBN:
1. **Issue currency** — sole authority to print naira
2. **Government's banker** — manages government accounts
3. **Banker's bank** — holds commercial banks' reserves
4. **Lender of last resort** — emergency loans to banks
5. **Controller of credit** — uses monetary policy tools
6. **Foreign exchange management** — manages external reserves
7. **Financial stability** — regulates and supervises banks

---

## 5.3 Monetary Policy Tools

| Tool | How it works |
|------|-------------|
| **Cash reserve ratio (CRR)** | % of deposits commercial banks must keep at CBN; raising CRR reduces money supply |
| **Open market operations (OMO)** | CBN buys or sells government securities; selling reduces money supply |
| **Monetary Policy Rate (MPR)** | CBN's benchmark interest rate; raising it makes borrowing more expensive |
| **Liquidity ratio** | % of liquid assets banks must hold; raising it reduces credit available |
| **Selective credit control** | Directs credit away from non-essential sectors to productive ones |

**Tight (contractionary) monetary policy:** Reduce money supply → fight inflation
**Loose (expansionary) monetary policy:** Increase money supply → stimulate growth

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** Explain how the Central Bank of Nigeria can use the cash reserve ratio to control inflation.

**Solution:**
During inflation, the CBN raises the **cash reserve ratio (CRR)** — the percentage of deposits that commercial banks must keep as reserves at the CBN rather than lending out. This reduces the amount of money commercial banks have available to lend. With less credit available, businesses and individuals borrow less, reducing spending. This decrease in demand helps slow down the rise in prices and controls inflation.

---

**Example 2 (JAMB type):** Which of the following is NOT a function of the Central Bank of Nigeria?
(a) Issuing currency (b) Accepting deposits from the public (c) Acting as lender of last resort (d) Managing foreign exchange reserves

**Solution:** (b) **Accepting deposits from the public** — this is a function of commercial banks. The CBN does not deal directly with the general public.

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** tests CBN functions, monetary policy tools, and their effects on money supply/inflation.

> **JAMB** tests the distinction between CBN and commercial bank functions.

> **Common trap:** The CBN does NOT accept deposits from individuals or businesses — only from commercial banks and the government. The CBN's customers are banks and government, not the public.
