## Poetry

### Poetry — Forms, Devices and Appreciation
**Class:** SSS1  |  **Topic ID:** `SSS-LIT-001-01`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Define poetry and distinguish it from prose
- Identify and explain major forms of poetry (lyric, narrative, dramatic, epic, dramatic monologue)
- Analyse poetic devices in unseen and prescribed poems
- Write a critical appreciation of a given poem using a structured framework
- Compare two or more poems on the basis of theme, style and language

#### 📖 Notes
- Poetry: literary art form using compressed, rhythmic language to convey emotion, ideas and images; exploits sound and meaning simultaneously
- Lyric poetry: expresses personal emotion (ode, elegy, sonnet); first-person; subjective
- Narrative poetry: tells a story (ballad, epic); has plot, characters, setting
- Dramatic poetry: spoken by a character/persona in a specific situation; dramatic monologue
- Sonnet: 14-line poem; Shakespearean (3 quatrains + couplet, ABAB CDCD EFEF GG) vs. Petrarchan (octave + sestet, ABBAABBA CDECDE)
- Ballad: narrative folk poem; simple stanzas, refrain, oral tradition origins
- Ode: formal poem addressed to a subject; elevated language; Pindaric, Horatian, Irregular
- Free verse: no regular rhyme or metre; modern poetry; relies on imagery and line breaks
- Poetic devices: alliteration, assonance, onomatopoeia, metaphor, simile, personification, hyperbole, oxymoron, paradox, irony, symbolism, imagery, refrain, enjambment, caesura
- Prosody: metre (iambic pentameter most common in English — 5 iambic feet per line), rhyme scheme (ABAB, AABB, etc.), rhythm
- Tone: poet's attitude (e.g. melancholic, celebratory, satirical, nostalgic)
- Critical appreciation framework: Speaker/persona, Occasion, Audience, Purpose, Subject/Theme, Tone/Mood, Language/Style, Form/Structure, Overall Effect

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Read J.P. Clark's 'Abiku' and Wole Soyinka's 'Abiku' — compare how each poet treats the same Nigerian concept using different forms and devices
2. Write a 400-word critical appreciation of an unseen poem, applying the SOAPS framework with textual evidence
3. Write an original 14-line sonnet on a topic of your choice; include at least 3 named poetic devices

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> WAEC (Essay 1 and 2) and JAMB test: close reading of prescribed texts, identification of devices WITH explanation of their effect, theme analysis
> Always QUOTE from the poem when identifying a device — an identified device without quotation scores no marks in WAEC
> Trap: confusing simile (uses 'like' or 'as') with metaphor (direct comparison without 'like' or 'as') — be precise in labelling

## Drama

### Drama — Elements, Conventions and Performance
**Class:** SSS2  |  **Topic ID:** `SSS-LIT-002-01`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Define drama and distinguish between dramatic text and performance
- Identify and explain the elements of drama
- Analyse characterisation, conflict and theme in prescribed plays
- Distinguish between tragedy, comedy and tragicomedy
- Write a critical essay on a prescribed dramatic text

#### 📖 Notes
- Drama: literary form meant to be performed on stage; combines dialogue, action and spectacle
- Elements of drama: plot, character, theme, dialogue, stagecraft (setting, lighting, costume, sound), conflict
- Plot structure: exposition, rising action, climax (turning point), falling action, denouement
- Types of conflict: protagonist vs. antagonist; person vs. self (internal); person vs. society; person vs. fate
- Characterisation: protagonist (hero/main character), antagonist (villain/opposition), foil (contrast to protagonist); flat vs. round characters; static vs. dynamic
- Tragedy: ends in the protagonist's downfall/death; hamartia (tragic flaw); catharsis (emotional release); Shakespearean vs. Greek tragedy
- Comedy: ends happily; satirical (lampoons society), romantic (love complications), farce (exaggerated humour)
- Tragicomedy: blends elements of both; common in modern African drama
- Dramatic irony: audience knows something characters do not; creates tension
- Soliloquy: character speaking thoughts aloud alone on stage
- Aside: character speaks to audience without other characters hearing
- Stage directions: instructions about setting, movement, delivery — usually in brackets/italics in text
- African drama: Wole Soyinka, J.P. Clark, Ola Rotimi — blend Yoruba, Ijaw traditions with Western form; oral tradition, ritual, ancestor spirits

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Stage a 10-minute excerpt from a prescribed play; class critiques performance on characterisation, conflict, and theme
2. Write a character analysis essay of one major and one minor character in a prescribed play: background, motivation, development, significance
3. Compare how 'Death and the King's Horseman' (Soyinka) and a Western tragedy handle the concept of fatalism/duty

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> WAEC Drama questions test: plot summary accuracy, character analysis with evidence, theme identification and exploration, and dramatic devices
> JAMB tests terminology (soliloquy, aside, dramatic irony), character roles, and conflict types
> Trap: confusing a PLAY'S THEME (central message) with its SUBJECT (topic) — theme is the MEANING or lesson; subject is what it is about

## Prose

### Prose Fiction — Novel and Short Story
**Class:** SSS3  |  **Topic ID:** `SSS-LIT-003-01`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Define prose fiction and distinguish between the novel and short story
- Analyse the narrative elements of prescribed prose texts
- Evaluate the theme, style and significance of prescribed novels
- Write an essay discussing aspects of a prescribed prose text with evidence
- Compare African and non-African prose on themes of colonialism, identity and development

#### 📖 Notes
- Prose fiction: narrative accounts of imaginary events told in free-flowing prose; genre of literature
- Novel: long prose narrative with developed plot, characters and setting; major world form from 18th century
- Short story: brief prose narrative; usually one main conflict, limited characters, single effect
- Narrative point of view: first-person narrator (uses 'I'; subjective), third-person omniscient (knows all characters' thoughts), third-person limited (knows one character), second-person (uses 'you' — rare)
- Narrative techniques: flashback, foreshadowing, stream of consciousness, unreliable narrator, epistolary (told through letters)
- Setting: time (historical period, season, time of day) and place; creates atmosphere; symbolic settings
- Theme: central message; may be about love, conflict, identity, colonialism, corruption, family, social justice
- Style: author's distinctive use of language, syntax, tone, imagery, rhythm; African writers often blend oral tradition
- African novel tradition: Chinua Achebe (Things Fall Apart — colonialism, Igbo culture), Chimamanda Adichie (Purple Hibiscus, Half of a Yellow Sun), Wole Soyinka, Ngugi wa Thiong'o
- Motif: recurring image, symbol or idea that reinforces theme
- Symbolism: objects, characters or events representing abstract ideas (e.g. the yam in TFA = Igbo manhood)
- Critical essay structure: introduction (text, author, central argument), body paragraphs (point + evidence + analysis), conclusion (significance)

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Write a 600-word essay on ONE theme in a prescribed novel, supporting each point with at least two specific textual references
2. Create a character relationship map for a prescribed novel: show how characters are connected and how those relationships develop
3. Compare the treatment of 'cultural conflict' in Achebe's Things Fall Apart and any other prescribed African text

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> WAEC Prose essay tests: direct knowledge of prescribed texts, analytical writing with embedded quotations, and structured argument
> JAMB tests factual knowledge of characters, events and author details from prescribed texts
> Critical rule: never retell the story — ANALYSE it; the examiner has read the book; your job is to evaluate, not summarise
