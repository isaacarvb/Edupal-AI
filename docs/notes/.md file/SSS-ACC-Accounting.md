# TOPIC 1: Introduction to Accounting and Double-Entry Bookkeeping
**Topic ID:** SSS-ACC-001-01

## 1.1 Definition of Accounting

**Accounting** is the process of recording, classifying, summarising, interpreting, and communicating financial information about a business to enable informed decision-making.

**Bookkeeping** is the recording of financial transactions — it is a part of accounting.

---

## 1.2 Types of Accounts (The Three-Account System)

Every transaction affects at least two accounts. There are three types:

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|------|-----------|---------|
| **Real account** | Tangible or intangible assets | Cash, Land, Machinery, Goodwill |
| **Personal account** | Accounts of persons (debtors/creditors) | John's account, ABC Ltd. account |
| **Nominal account** | Expenses, losses, incomes, and gains | Rent account, Sales account, Salary account |

---

## 1.3 The Double-Entry Rule

**Every transaction has two equal and opposite effects.** For every debit, there must be an equal credit.

| Account type | Debit (Dr) increases when... | Credit (Cr) increases when... |
|-------------|------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| **Real (Asset)** | Asset increases | Asset decreases |
| **Personal (Debtor)** | Money owed to us increases | Money owed to us decreases |
| **Personal (Creditor)** | Money we owe decreases | Money we owe increases |
| **Nominal (Expense)** | Expense increases | Expense decreases |
| **Nominal (Income)** | Income decreases | Income increases |

**Golden Rules:**
- Real accounts: Debit what comes in, Credit what goes out
- Personal accounts: Debit the receiver, Credit the giver
- Nominal accounts: Debit all expenses and losses, Credit all incomes and gains

---

## 1.4 The Ledger

**The ledger** is the principal book of accounts where all transactions are classified by account. Each account has a T-format:

```
        [Account Name]
Dr                          Cr
Date | Details | Amount  |  Date | Details | Amount
```

**Balancing a ledger account:**
1. Add both sides
2. The difference is the **balance**
3. Put the balance on the smaller side to make both sides equal (as "Balance c/d")
4. Carry forward the balance to the next period (as "Balance b/d")

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** Record the following transactions in the ledger and balance the accounts.
Jan 1: Started business with ₦50,000 cash.
Jan 5: Bought goods for ₦20,000 cash.
Jan 10: Sold goods for ₦15,000 cash.

**Solution:**

**Cash Account:**
| Dr | | | Cr | | |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jan 1 | Capital | 50,000 | Jan 5 | Purchases | 20,000 |
| Jan 10 | Sales | 15,000 | Jan 31 | Balance c/d | 45,000 |
| | **Total** | **65,000** | | **Total** | **65,000** |
| Feb 1 | Balance b/d | 45,000 | | | |

**Purchases Account:**
| Dr | | | Cr | | |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jan 5 | Cash | 20,000 | Jan 31 | Balance c/d | 20,000 |

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** tests ledger posting, balancing accounts, and application of double-entry rules.

> **Common trap:** Cash received goes on the DEBIT side of the cash account (money comes IN). Cash paid out goes on the CREDIT side.

# TOPIC 2: Trial Balance and Final Accounts
**Topic ID:** SSS-ACC-001-02

## 2.1 The Trial Balance

A **trial balance** is a list of all the debit and credit balances from the ledger at a specific date. It is used to check the arithmetic accuracy of the books.

**Format:**
| Account Name | Dr (₦) | Cr (₦) |
|-------------|--------|--------|
| Capital | — | xxx |
| Sales | — | xxx |
| Purchases | xxx | — |
| Wages | xxx | — |
| Cash/Bank | xxx | — |
| Debtors | xxx | — |
| Creditors | — | xxx |
| **Total** | **xxx** | **xxx** |

**If the totals agree:** The books are arithmetically correct (but errors of principle, omission, reversal, etc., may still exist).

---

## 2.2 The Trading Account

The **trading account** calculates the **gross profit (or loss)**:

| | ₦ | ₦ |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | | xxx |
| Less: Cost of Goods Sold | | |
| Opening stock | xxx | |
| Add: Purchases | xxx | |
| Less: Closing stock | (xxx) | |
| **Cost of Goods Sold** | | (xxx) |
| **GROSS PROFIT** | | **xxx** |

**Gross Profit = Sales − Cost of Goods Sold**

---

## 2.3 The Profit and Loss Account

The **profit and loss account** calculates the **net profit (or loss)**:

| | ₦ |
|---|---|
| Gross Profit | xxx |
| Add: Other incomes (e.g., rent received, discount received) | xxx |
| Less: Expenses (wages, rent paid, insurance, depreciation, etc.) | (xxx) |
| **NET PROFIT** | **xxx** |

**Net Profit = Gross Profit + Other Income − Expenses**

---

## 2.4 The Balance Sheet

The **balance sheet** is a statement of the financial position of a business at a specific date. It is NOT an account — it does not have debit/credit columns.

**Format:**

| **ASSETS** | ₦ | **CAPITAL & LIABILITIES** | ₦ |
|-----------|---|--------------------------|---|
| **Fixed Assets** | | **Capital** | |
| Land & Building | xxx | Opening capital | xxx |
| Machinery | xxx | Add: Net profit | xxx |
| **Current Assets** | | Less: Drawings | (xxx) |
| Stock (closing) | xxx | **Closing capital** | xxx |
| Debtors | xxx | **Long-term Liabilities** | |
| Cash/Bank | xxx | Loan | xxx |
| | | **Current Liabilities** | |
| | | Creditors | xxx |
| **Total Assets** | **xxx** | **Total Cap. & Liabilities** | **xxx** |

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example (WAEC type):** From the following, prepare a Trading and Profit & Loss Account:
Sales: ₦120,000 | Opening stock: ₦10,000 | Purchases: ₦70,000 | Closing stock: ₦15,000
Wages: ₦8,000 | Rent: ₦5,000 | Insurance: ₦2,000

**Solution:**

**Trading Account:**
Sales: ₦120,000
Opening stock: ₦10,000
Add Purchases: ₦70,000 = ₦80,000
Less Closing stock: (₦15,000) = **COGS: ₦65,000**
**Gross Profit: ₦120,000 − ₦65,000 = ₦55,000**

**Profit & Loss Account:**
Gross Profit: ₦55,000
Less: Wages ₦8,000 + Rent ₦5,000 + Insurance ₦2,000 = ₦15,000
**Net Profit: ₦55,000 − ₦15,000 = ₦40,000**

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** tests preparation of trading accounts, P&L accounts, and balance sheets from given trial balance data.

> **Common trap:** Closing stock appears in BOTH the trading account (deducted from COGS) AND the balance sheet (as a current asset). Never double-count it as an expense.

# TOPIC 3: Depreciation of Fixed Assets
**Topic ID:** SSS-ACC-002-01

## 3.1 Definition of Depreciation

**Depreciation** is the systematic allocation of the cost of a fixed (non-current) asset over its useful life. It reflects the reduction in value of an asset due to use, wear and tear, obsolescence, or the passage of time.

**Why depreciation is charged:**
1. To match the cost of using an asset against the revenue it helps generate (matching concept)
2. To give a true and fair view of asset value
3. To provide funds for eventual replacement

---

## 3.2 Methods of Depreciation

### Method 1: Straight-Line Method (Fixed Instalment)

**Formula:** Annual depreciation = (Cost − Residual value) / Useful life (years)

**Feature:** Same amount charged every year.

**Example:** Asset cost ₦100,000, residual value ₦10,000, useful life 5 years
Annual depreciation = (100,000 − 10,000) / 5 = **₦18,000 per year**

| Year | Cost | Depreciation | Acc. Depreciation | NBV |
|------|------|-------------|-----------------|-----|
| 1 | 100,000 | 18,000 | 18,000 | 82,000 |
| 2 | 100,000 | 18,000 | 36,000 | 64,000 |
| 3 | 100,000 | 18,000 | 54,000 | 46,000 |

---

### Method 2: Reducing Balance Method (Diminishing Balance)

**Formula:** Annual depreciation = Rate% × Net Book Value (NBV)

**Feature:** Decreasing amount each year; higher depreciation in early years.

**Example:** Asset cost ₦80,000, depreciation rate 20% p.a.

| Year | NBV (start) | Depreciation (20%) | NBV (end) |
|------|------------|-------------------|-----------|
| 1 | 80,000 | 16,000 | 64,000 |
| 2 | 64,000 | 12,800 | 51,200 |
| 3 | 51,200 | 10,240 | 40,960 |

---

## 3.3 Recording Depreciation

Depreciation is recorded with two accounts:
- **Depreciation account** (expense — debit)
- **Provision for depreciation account** (reduces asset value — credit)

In the balance sheet, assets are shown at **Net Book Value (NBV):**
NBV = Cost − Accumulated Depreciation

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example (WAEC type):** A machine costs ₦60,000. It has a useful life of 4 years and a scrap value of ₦4,000. Calculate the annual depreciation using the straight-line method and show the machine account for year 1 and year 2.

**Solution:**
Annual depreciation = (60,000 − 4,000) / 4 = 56,000 / 4 = **₦14,000 per year**

After Year 1: NBV = 60,000 − 14,000 = ₦46,000
After Year 2: NBV = 46,000 − 14,000 = ₦32,000

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** tests calculation of depreciation using both methods, comparison of methods, and balance sheet presentation of fixed assets.

> **Common trap:** In the balance sheet, assets are shown at Net Book Value (cost minus accumulated depreciation) — NOT at original cost.

# TOPIC 4: Bank Reconciliation Statement
**Topic ID:** SSS-ACC-003-01

## 4.1 Definition

A **bank reconciliation statement** is a statement prepared to explain the difference between the bank balance shown in the cashbook and the balance shown on the bank statement for the same date.

---

## 4.2 Why Differences Arise

| Reason | Effect on Cashbook vs Bank Statement |
|--------|-------------------------------------|
| **Unpresented (outstanding) cheques** | Cashbook shows less (already recorded as payment); bank not yet deducted |
| **Uncredited (outstanding) lodgements** | Cashbook shows more; bank not yet received the deposit |
| **Bank charges/standing orders** | Bank deducted; cashbook not yet updated |
| **Direct credits (e.g., dividends)** | Bank received; cashbook not yet recorded |
| **Dishonoured cheques** | Bank reversed; cashbook not yet adjusted |

---

## 4.3 Preparing a Bank Reconciliation Statement

**Step 1:** Update the cashbook with items on the bank statement not yet in the cashbook (bank charges, direct credits, dishonoured cheques).

**Step 2:** Prepare the reconciliation statement:

```
Bank Reconciliation Statement as at [date]
                                              ₦
Balance as per CASHBOOK (updated)           xxx
Add: Unpresented cheques                    xxx
Less: Uncredited lodgements                (xxx)
Balance as per BANK STATEMENT               xxx
```

OR, starting from bank statement:
```
Balance as per BANK STATEMENT               xxx
Add: Uncredited lodgements                  xxx
Less: Unpresented cheques                  (xxx)
Balance as per CASHBOOK (updated)           xxx
```

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example (WAEC type):**
Cashbook balance: ₦15,000 (Dr)
Bank statement balance: ₦18,500
Unpresented cheques: ₦5,000
Uncredited lodgements: ₦1,500

Prepare the bank reconciliation statement.

**Solution:**
```
Bank Reconciliation Statement
                                              ₦
Balance as per Cashbook                   15,000
Add: Unpresented cheques                   5,000
                                          20,000
Less: Uncredited lodgements               (1,500)
Balance as per Bank Statement             18,500 ✓
```

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** tests preparation of bank reconciliation statements and identification of causes of discrepancy.

> **Common trap:** Unpresented cheques = cheques written by the firm but NOT yet presented to the bank for payment. They are in the cashbook but NOT on the bank statement. They reduce the CASHBOOK balance but NOT yet the BANK balance — so we ADD them to the cashbook balance when reconciling from cashbook to bank.

# TOPIC 5: Correction of Errors and the Suspense Account
**Topic ID:** SSS-ACC-003-02

## 5.1 Types of Errors in Accounting

Some errors affect the trial balance (make it disagree); others do not.

### Errors that DO NOT affect the trial balance:

| Error | Description | Example |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| **Error of omission** | Transaction completely left out | A sale of ₦5,000 never recorded |
| **Error of commission** | Correct amount, posted to wrong account (same type) | Rent paid posted to wages account |
| **Error of principle** | Correct amount, wrong TYPE of account | Capital expenditure posted as revenue |
| **Error of original entry** | Wrong amount in both accounts | ₦500 entered as ₦5,000 in both |
| **Reversal of entries** | Debit and credit swapped | Debit of sales, credit of purchases |
| **Compensating errors** | Two errors that cancel each other out | Overdebit of ₦100 offset by overdebit of ₦100 on other side |

### Errors that DO affect the trial balance:
- One-sided entry (only debit OR credit posted)
- Wrong amount on one side
- Entry posted twice on same side

---

## 5.2 The Suspense Account

When the trial balance does NOT agree, the difference is placed temporarily in a **suspense account**. Once errors are found, they are corrected and the suspense account is closed.

---

## 5.3 Journal Entries for Correcting Errors

To correct errors, use **journal entries** (the book of original entry for corrections):

| Error | Correction |
|-------|-----------|
| Error of omission | Post the missing transaction in full |
| Error of commission | Reverse the wrong posting; post to correct account |
| Error of principle | Reverse from wrong account type; post to correct account |

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example (WAEC type):** The following errors were discovered after the trial balance was prepared. Prepare journal entries to correct each:
(a) A purchase of goods for ₦3,000 from Chidi was not recorded.
(b) ₦500 paid for rent was posted to the salaries account.

**Solution:**
**(a) Error of omission — missing purchase:**
Dr Purchases account ₦3,000
Cr Chidi (Creditors) account ₦3,000
*(Being correction of purchase omitted)*

**(b) Error of commission — rent posted to salaries:**
Dr Rent account ₦500
Cr Salaries account ₦500
*(Being correction of rent expense wrongly posted to salaries account)*

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** tests identification of error types, preparation of journal entries for corrections, and use of the suspense account.

> **Common trap:** An error of COMMISSION posts to the wrong account of the SAME type (e.g., one expense account instead of another). An error of PRINCIPLE posts to the wrong TYPE of account (e.g., capital expenditure treated as revenue).

> Remember: Errors of commission, omission, principle, original entry, reversal, and compensating errors all leave the trial balance still agreeing — they are the "hidden" errors.
