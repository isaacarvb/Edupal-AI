# TOPIC 1: Atomic Structure and the Periodic Table
**Topic ID:** SSS-CHM-001-01

## 1.1 Structure of the Atom

The **atom** is the smallest particle of an element that retains the chemical properties of that element. An atom consists of:

| Particle | Location | Charge | Mass (a.m.u.) | Symbol |
|----------|---------|--------|--------------|--------|
| **Proton** | Nucleus | +1 | 1 | p |
| **Neutron** | Nucleus | 0 | 1 | n |
| **Electron** | Shells (energy levels) | −1 | 0 (negligible) | e |

**Atomic number (Z):** Number of protons (= number of electrons in a neutral atom)
**Mass number (A):** Protons + Neutrons
**Neutron number (N):** A − Z

**Notation:** ᴬ_Z X (e.g., ¹²_6 C — Carbon with mass number 12, atomic number 6)

---

## 1.2 Isotopes

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element with the same atomic number (same number of protons) but different mass numbers (different number of neutrons).

| Isotope | Protons | Neutrons | Mass number |
|---------|---------|---------|------------|
| ¹H (Protium) | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| ²H (Deuterium) | 1 | 1 | 2 |
| ³H (Tritium) | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| ¹²C | 6 | 6 | 12 |
| ¹⁴C | 6 | 8 | 14 |

**Isotopes have the same chemical properties** (same electron configuration) but different physical properties (different masses).

---

## 1.3 Electronic Configuration

Electrons occupy shells (energy levels) around the nucleus:
- Shell 1 (K): maximum 2 electrons
- Shell 2 (L): maximum 8 electrons
- Shell 3 (M): maximum 18 electrons (but first fills to 8 before Shell 4)

**Rule:** Fill from lowest energy level outward.

| Element | Symbol | Z | Configuration |
|---------|--------|---|--------------|
| Hydrogen | H | 1 | 1 |
| Carbon | C | 6 | 2, 4 |
| Nitrogen | N | 7 | 2, 5 |
| Oxygen | O | 8 | 2, 6 |
| Sodium | Na | 11 | 2, 8, 1 |
| Chlorine | Cl | 17 | 2, 8, 7 |
| Calcium | Ca | 20 | 2, 8, 8, 2 |

**Valence electrons:** Electrons in the outermost shell — they determine chemical behaviour.

---

## 1.4 The Periodic Table

Elements arranged in order of increasing atomic number. Elements in the same **group** have the same number of valence electrons and similar properties. Elements in the same **period** have the same number of electron shells.

| Group | Name | Valence electrons | Characteristics |
|-------|------|-----------------|----------------|
| I | Alkali metals | 1 | Highly reactive; react with water |
| II | Alkaline earth metals | 2 | Reactive; less so than Group I |
| VII | Halogens | 7 | Very reactive non-metals; diatomic |
| VIII/0 | Noble gases | 8 (or 2 for He) | Inert; stable |

**Periodic trends:**
- Atomic radius: Decreases across a period; increases down a group
- Electronegativity: Increases across a period; decreases down a group
- Ionisation energy: Increases across a period; decreases down a group

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** An element X has the electronic configuration 2, 8, 6. Identify its group and period, and state whether it is a metal or non-metal.

**Solution:**
- Number of shells = 3 → **Period 3**
- Valence electrons = 6 → **Group VI**
- Six valence electrons → non-metal (this is **Sulphur, S**)

---

**Example 2 (JAMB type):** How many neutrons does ⁴⁰_20 Ca contain?

**Solution:** Neutrons = A − Z = 40 − 20 = **20 neutrons**

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** tests electronic configurations, periodic trends, and identifying elements from configuration.

> **JAMB** tests identification of isotopes, neutron numbers, and group/period placement.

> **Common trap:** Electronic configuration 2, 8, 8, 2 belongs to Calcium (Z = 20), not Magnesium (Z = 12, which is 2, 8, 2).

# TOPIC 2: Chemical Bonding
**Topic ID:** SSS-CHM-001-02

## 2.1 Definition

**Chemical bonding** is the force of attraction that holds atoms or ions together in a compound. Atoms bond to achieve a stable electronic configuration (usually 8 valence electrons — the octet rule).

---

## 2.2 Types of Chemical Bonds

| Bond type | Description | Formed between | Examples |
|-----------|-------------|---------------|---------|
| **Ionic (electrovalent)** | Transfer of electrons from metal to non-metal | Metal + Non-metal | NaCl, MgO, CaCl₂ |
| **Covalent** | Sharing of electrons between non-metals | Non-metal + Non-metal | H₂O, CO₂, CH₄ |
| **Metallic** | Positive ions in a "sea" of delocalised electrons | Metal + Metal | Fe, Cu, Na |
| **Coordinate (dative)** | Both electrons in the shared pair come from one atom | In complex ions/molecules | NH₄⁺, CO |
| **Hydrogen bond** | Weak attraction between H and electronegative atom (N, O, F) | Between molecules | H₂O, NH₃ |
| **Van der Waals forces** | Weak temporary induced dipole forces | Between all molecules | Noble gases, organic molecules |

---

## 2.3 Ionic Bonding

**Formation:** Metal atom LOSES electrons → positive cation. Non-metal atom GAINS electrons → negative anion. Opposite ions attract.

**Example: Sodium chloride (NaCl)**
- Na (2, 8, 1) → Na⁺ (2, 8) + 1e⁻ [loses 1 electron]
- Cl (2, 8, 7) + 1e⁻ → Cl⁻ (2, 8, 8) [gains 1 electron]

**Properties of ionic compounds:**
- High melting/boiling points
- Soluble in water; insoluble in organic solvents
- Conduct electricity when dissolved or melted (not as solid)
- Form crystalline solids

---

## 2.4 Covalent Bonding

**Formation:** Non-metal atoms SHARE electrons to complete their octets.

| Bond | Electron pairs shared | Example |
|------|--------------------|---------|
| Single bond | 1 | H₂, H₂O |
| Double bond | 2 | O₂, CO₂, C₂H₄ |
| Triple bond | 3 | N₂, C₂H₂ |

**Properties of covalent compounds:**
- Low melting/boiling points (weak intermolecular forces)
- Often volatile; many are gases or liquids at room temperature
- Do NOT conduct electricity (no ions)
- Mostly soluble in organic solvents; many insoluble in water

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** Draw the dot-and-cross diagram for water (H₂O) and state the type of bonding.

**Solution:**
Water is a **covalent compound** (O and H are both non-metals).
- Oxygen (2, 6) needs 2 more electrons → shares 1 electron with each of 2 hydrogen atoms
- Each H shares 1 electron with O
- Result: O has 8 electrons around it (octet); each H has 2 electrons (duet — stable for H)
- Two O–H single covalent bonds

---

**Example 2 (JAMB type):** Which of the following conducts electricity in the solid state?
(a) NaCl (b) MgO (c) Copper (d) Sugar

**Solution:** (c) **Copper** — a metal has delocalised electrons that carry charge. NaCl and MgO are ionic (conduct only when molten/dissolved). Sugar is covalent (never conducts).

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** tests dot-and-cross diagrams, properties of ionic vs covalent, and bond formation.

> **Common trap:** Ionic compounds conduct electricity ONLY when molten or in aqueous solution — NOT as solids (ions are fixed in the lattice).

# TOPIC 3: The Mole Concept and Chemical Calculations
**Topic ID:** SSS-CHM-002-01

## 3.1 The Mole

The **mole** is the SI unit of the amount of substance. 1 mole of any substance contains **6.02 × 10²³ particles** (Avogadro's number, Nₐ).

**Molar mass:** Mass of 1 mole of a substance in grams (numerically equal to relative atomic/molecular mass).

---

## 3.2 Key Mole Relationships

| Relationship | Formula |
|-------------|---------|
| Moles from mass | n = mass (g) / molar mass (g/mol) |
| Moles from particles | n = N / Nₐ |
| Moles from volume of gas (at STP) | n = V / 22.4 L (STP: 0°C, 1 atm) |
| Moles from volume of gas (at r.t.p.) | n = V / 24 dm³ (r.t.p.: 25°C, 1 atm) |
| Concentration | c = n / V(L), so n = cV |

---

## 3.3 Chemical Equations and Stoichiometry

A balanced chemical equation shows:
1. Reactants and products
2. Molar ratios (stoichiometric coefficients)

**Example:** 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

This means 2 moles H₂ react with 1 mole O₂ to produce 2 moles H₂O.

**Steps for mole calculations:**
1. Write a balanced equation
2. Identify the molar ratio
3. Calculate moles of known substance
4. Use ratio to find moles of unknown
5. Convert to required unit

---

## 3.4 Empirical and Molecular Formula

**Empirical formula:** Simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound.
**Molecular formula:** Actual number of atoms of each element in a molecule.

**To find empirical formula:**
1. Divide percentage composition by relative atomic mass (RAM)
2. Divide each by the smallest value
3. Round to nearest whole number

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** Calculate the number of moles in 44 g of CO₂.
(C = 12, O = 16)

**Solution:**
Molar mass of CO₂ = 12 + 2(16) = 44 g/mol
n = 44/44 = **1 mole**

---

**Example 2 (WAEC type):** A compound contains 40% C, 6.67% H, and 53.3% O by mass. Find its empirical formula. (C=12, H=1, O=16)

**Solution:**
Divide by RAM: C: 40/12 = 3.33 | H: 6.67/1 = 6.67 | O: 53.3/16 = 3.33
Divide by smallest (3.33): C: 1 | H: 2 | O: 1
Empirical formula: **CH₂O**

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** tests mole calculations, empirical formula determination, and stoichiometric ratios.

> **Common trap:** WAEC commonly uses r.t.p. (24 dm³/mol) while some calculations use STP (22.4 dm³/mol). Read the question carefully.

# TOPIC 4: Chemical Kinetics and Equilibrium
**Topic ID:** SSS-CHM-002-02

## 4.1 Rate of Reaction

**Reaction rate** is the change in concentration of reactants or products per unit time.

**Factors affecting reaction rate:**
| Factor | Effect | Explanation |
|--------|--------|-------------|
| Concentration | Higher concentration → faster rate | More particles per volume → more collisions |
| Temperature | Higher temperature → faster rate | More kinetic energy → more frequent, energetic collisions |
| Surface area | Greater surface area → faster rate | More particles exposed → more contact |
| Catalyst | Catalyst → faster rate | Provides alternative pathway with lower activation energy |
| Pressure | Higher pressure (gases only) → faster rate | Particles closer together → more collisions |

**Activation energy:** Minimum energy required for a reaction to occur.

---

## 4.2 Collision Theory

A reaction occurs when particles:
1. **Collide** with each other
2. Have **sufficient energy** (≥ activation energy)
3. Have **correct orientation**

---

## 4.3 Chemical Equilibrium

**Dynamic equilibrium** exists in a reversible reaction when:
- The forward and backward reactions occur at the same rate
- The concentrations of reactants and products remain constant (but NOT zero)
- Represented by ⇌

**Le Chatelier's Principle:** When a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it will shift to counteract the disturbance and re-establish equilibrium.

| Disturbance | Direction of shift |
|-------------|------------------|
| Increase concentration of reactants | Forward (→) |
| Increase concentration of products | Backward (←) |
| Increase temperature | Toward endothermic side |
| Decrease temperature | Toward exothermic side |
| Increase pressure | Toward side with fewer moles of gas |
| Add catalyst | No shift in position; reaches equilibrium faster |

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** Consider: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)  ΔH = −92 kJ/mol

How will the equilibrium shift if: (a) temperature is increased, (b) pressure is decreased?

**Solution:**
(a) The forward reaction is **exothermic** (ΔH negative). Increasing temperature shifts equilibrium in the **endothermic direction** → equilibrium shifts **backward** → less NH₃.

(b) Left side has 1 + 3 = 4 moles of gas; right side has 2 moles. Decreasing pressure shifts equilibrium toward **more moles of gas** → shifts **backward (←)** → less NH₃.

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** tests Le Chatelier's Principle with the Haber Process (N₂ + H₂ → NH₃) and Contact Process (SO₂ → SO₃).

> **Common trap:** A catalyst does NOT shift the equilibrium position — it only helps equilibrium be reached faster.

# TOPIC 5: Hydrocarbons — Alkanes, Alkenes and Alkynes
**Topic ID:** SSS-CHM-003-01

## 5.1 Definition

**Hydrocarbons** are organic compounds containing only carbon and hydrogen atoms.

---

## 5.2 Homologous Series

A **homologous series** is a family of compounds with:
- The same general formula
- The same functional group
- Successive members differing by –CH₂– (14 mass units)
- Similar chemical properties

---

## 5.3 Alkanes — Saturated Hydrocarbons

| Feature | Details |
|---------|---------|
| General formula | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ |
| Bond type | Single C–C bonds only (no double or triple bonds) |
| Hybridisation | sp³ (tetrahedral geometry, 109.5°) |
| Reactions | Substitution (not addition) |
| Properties | Relatively unreactive; do not decolourise bromine water |

**First five alkanes:**

| Name | Formula | Structural |
|------|---------|-----------|
| Methane | CH₄ | CH₄ |
| Ethane | C₂H₆ | CH₃–CH₃ |
| Propane | C₃H₈ | CH₃–CH₂–CH₃ |
| Butane | C₄H₁₀ | CH₃–(CH₂)₂–CH₃ |
| Pentane | C₅H₁₂ | CH₃–(CH₂)₃–CH₃ |

---

## 5.4 Alkenes — Unsaturated (one double bond)

| Feature | Details |
|---------|---------|
| General formula | CₙH₂ₙ |
| Bond type | One C=C double bond |
| Hybridisation | sp² (120° bond angles at double bond) |
| Reactions | Addition reactions (with H₂, HX, X₂, H₂O) |
| Test | **Decolourises bromine water** (orange → colourless) |

---

## 5.5 Alkynes — Unsaturated (one triple bond)

| Feature | Details |
|---------|---------|
| General formula | CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ |
| Bond type | One C≡C triple bond |
| Hybridisation | sp (180° linear) |
| Reactions | Addition reactions (like alkenes, but need excess reagent) |

---

## 5.6 Combustion of Hydrocarbons

**Complete combustion** (excess oxygen): Produces CO₂ and H₂O
CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

**Incomplete combustion** (limited oxygen): Produces CO (toxic!) or C (soot)
2CH₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO + 4H₂O

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** An alkane has the molecular formula C₅H₁₂. Write the IUPAC name and draw the structure of its straight-chain isomer.

**Solution:**
IUPAC name: **pentane** (5 carbons, all single bonds, straight chain)
Structure: CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃

---

**Example 2 (JAMB type):** Which of the following will decolourise bromine water?
(a) Ethane (b) Propane (c) Ethylene (d) Methane

**Solution:** (c) **Ethylene (ethene, CH₂=CH₂)** — it is an alkene and will undergo addition reaction with Br₂ in bromine water, decolourising it.

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** tests naming alkanes/alkenes, drawing structures, identifying isomers, and writing combustion equations.

> **Test for alkene:** Add bromine water — **decolourises** (goes from orange to colourless). Alkanes do NOT decolourise bromine water.

# TOPIC 6: Functional Groups in Organic Chemistry
**Topic ID:** SSS-CHM-003-02

## 6.1 Definition

A **functional group** is an atom or group of atoms in an organic molecule that is responsible for the characteristic chemical reactions of that molecule.

---

## 6.2 Major Functional Groups

| Class | Functional group | Suffix/Prefix | Example |
|-------|----------------|--------------|---------|
| **Alcohols** | –OH (hydroxyl) | -ol | Ethanol (C₂H₅OH) |
| **Aldehydes** | –CHO (formyl) | -al | Ethanal (CH₃CHO) |
| **Ketones** | C=O (carbonyl, middle of chain) | -one | Propanone (CH₃COCH₃) |
| **Carboxylic acids** | –COOH (carboxyl) | -oic acid | Ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH) |
| **Esters** | –COO– | -anoate | Ethyl ethanoate |
| **Amines** | –NH₂ (amino) | -amine | Methylamine |
| **Haloalkanes** | –X (halogen) | halo- | Chloromethane (CH₃Cl) |

---

## 6.3 Reactions of Functional Groups

### Alcohols:
- **Oxidation:** Primary alcohol → Aldehyde → Carboxylic acid; Secondary → Ketone
- **Esterification:** Alcohol + Carboxylic acid ⇌ Ester + Water (catalyst: conc. H₂SO₄)

### Carboxylic Acids:
- React with bases/carbonates to form salts
- React with alcohols to form esters

### Esters:
- **Hydrolysis:** Ester + Water → Alcohol + Carboxylic acid
- **Saponification:** Ester + NaOH → Alcohol + Carboxylate salt (soap-making)

---

## 6.4 Tests for Functional Groups

| Functional group | Test | Positive result |
|-----------------|------|----------------|
| Aldehyde | Fehling's solution OR Tollens' reagent | Fehling's: blue → brick-red precipitate; Tollens': silver mirror |
| Aldehyde AND ketone | 2,4-DNPH reagent | Orange/yellow precipitate |
| Carboxylic acid | Sodium carbonate | Effervescence (CO₂ produced) |
| Alcohol | Acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ | Orange → green (oxidation) |
| Double bond (alkene) | Bromine water | Orange → colourless |

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** Name the product formed when ethanol reacts with ethanoic acid in the presence of concentrated sulphuric acid.

**Solution:**
Ethanol + Ethanoic acid → **Ethyl ethanoate** + Water
(This is an **esterification** reaction)
Chemical equation: C₂H₅OH + CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O

---

**Example 2 (JAMB type):** Which reagent would distinguish between an aldehyde and a ketone?

**Solution:** **Fehling's solution** (or Tollens' reagent). Aldehydes are oxidised and reduce the reagent (brick-red precipitate with Fehling's; silver mirror with Tollens'). Ketones do NOT reduce these reagents.

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** tests esterification equations, naming functional groups, and tests for specific groups.

> **Common trap:** Both aldehydes and ketones give a positive result with 2,4-DNPH (orange precipitate). Only aldehydes are oxidised by Fehling's/Tollens'.

# TOPIC 7: Electrolysis
**Topic ID:** SSS-CHM-004-01

## 7.1 Definition

**Electrolysis** is the decomposition of an ionic compound (electrolyte) when molten or in aqueous solution, by passing an electric current through it.

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Electrolyte** | Ionic compound that conducts electricity in solution or molten state |
| **Electrode** | Conductor through which current enters/leaves the electrolyte |
| **Anode** | Positive electrode (where oxidation occurs — OIL) |
| **Cathode** | Negative electrode (where reduction occurs — RIG) |

**Memory aid: OILRIG** — Oxidation Is Loss; Reduction Is Gain (of electrons)

---

## 7.2 Electrode Reactions

**At cathode (reduction):** Cations (+) migrate to cathode, gain electrons → reduced
- H⁺ + e⁻ → ½H₂ (hydrogen gas)
- Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (copper deposit)
- Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na

**At anode (oxidation):** Anions (−) migrate to anode, lose electrons → oxidised
- 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ (chlorine gas)
- 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ (oxygen gas)
- 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻

### Preferential discharge:
When more than one ion is present, which is preferentially discharged?

| Cathode (more easily reduced) | Anode (more easily oxidised) |
|-------------------------------|------------------------------|
| Cu²⁺ > H⁺ > Na⁺ | Cl⁻ > OH⁻ (dilute solutions) |

In dilute solutions: H⁺ is discharged at cathode; OH⁻ at anode.
In concentrated NaCl solution: Na⁺ at cathode (but H₂ evolved due to preference); Cl⁻ at anode.

---

## 7.3 Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis

**First Law:** The mass of substance deposited at an electrode is proportional to the quantity of electricity (charge) passed.
m ∝ Q = It

**Second Law:** The masses of different substances deposited by the same quantity of electricity are proportional to their electrochemical equivalents (molar mass / number of electrons transferred).

**Key formula:**
m = (M × I × t) / (n × F)

Where:
- m = mass deposited (g)
- M = molar mass (g/mol)
- I = current (A)
- t = time (s)
- n = number of electrons transferred per ion
- F = Faraday's constant = 96,500 C/mol

---

## 7.4 Applications of Electrolysis

| Application | Process |
|-------------|---------|
| **Electroplating** | Coating a metal with another metal (e.g., silver plating cutlery) |
| **Extraction of reactive metals** | Al from bauxite (alumina); Na from NaCl |
| **Purification of metals** | Electrolytic refining of copper |
| **Manufacture of chemicals** | Cl₂, NaOH from brine (chlor-alkali industry) |

**In electroplating:**
- Anode: metal being deposited (e.g., silver)
- Cathode: object being plated
- Electrolyte: solution of the metal salt

---

## ✏️ Worked Examples

**Example 1 (WAEC type):** Calculate the mass of copper deposited when a current of 2 A is passed for 965 seconds through a solution of copper(II) sulphate. (Cu = 64, F = 96,500 C/mol)

**Solution:**
Cu²⁺ → Cu requires 2 electrons, so n = 2
Q = It = 2 × 965 = 1930 C
m = (M × Q) / (n × F) = (64 × 1930) / (2 × 96,500) = 123,520 / 193,000 = **0.64 g**

---

**Example 2 (WAEC type):** During the electrolysis of dilute H₂SO₄ using platinum electrodes, identify the product at each electrode.

**Solution:**
- **Cathode:** H⁺ ions are reduced → **hydrogen gas (H₂)**
- **Anode:** OH⁻ ions are oxidised → **oxygen gas (O₂)**

---

## 🏆 Exam Focus

> **WAEC** tests Faraday's Laws calculations, electrode identification, and products of electrolysis.

> **Common trap:** The ANODE is POSITIVE (despite the confusing "a" for "anode" vs "nega-tive"). Remember: AN ODE to OXIDATION — Anode = Oxidation.

> In concentrated NaCl (brine) electrolysis: cathode gives H₂, anode gives Cl₂, and NaOH remains in solution. This is the industrial chlor-alkali process — a common WAEC question.
