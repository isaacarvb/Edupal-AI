## Oral English

### Speech Sounds — Vowels and Consonants
**Class:** SSS1  |  **Topic ID:** `SSS-ENG-001-01`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Identify and produce the 44 phonemes of British Received Pronunciation (RP) English
- Distinguish between vowels (pure and diphthongs) and consonants
- Apply correct word stress in polysyllabic words
- Recognize and produce minimal pairs to distinguish phonemes
- Transcribe words using the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)

#### 📖 Notes
- Phoneme: smallest unit of sound in a language that distinguishes meaning
- 20 vowel sounds: 12 pure (monophthongs) + 8 diphthongs
- Pure vowels: /ɪ/ (bit), /iː/ (beat), /e/ (bet), /æ/ (bat), /ʌ/ (but), /ɑː/ (bar), /ɒ/ (bot), /ɔː/ (bought), /ʊ/ (book), /uː/ (boot), /ɜː/ (bird), /ə/ (about)
- Diphthongs: /eɪ/ (day), /aɪ/ (my), /ɔɪ/ (boy), /əʊ/ (go), /aʊ/ (now), /ɪə/ (here), /eə/ (hair), /ʊə/ (pure)
- 24 consonant sounds: plosives /p, b, t, d, k, g/; fricatives /f, v, θ, ð, s, z, ʃ, ʒ, h/; affricates /tʃ, dʒ/; nasals /m, n, ŋ/; approximants /l, r, w, j/
- Voiced vs. voiceless consonants: voiced uses vocal cord vibration (e.g. /v/ voiced, /f/ voiceless)
- Minimal pairs: words differing by one sound — /bɪt/ (bit) vs. /pɪt/ (pit); /sɪt/ (sit) vs. /zɪt/ (zit)
- Word stress: primary (ˈ) and secondary (ˌ) stress; noun vs. verb stress pairs (ˈrecord vs. reˈcord)
- IPA transcription: representing speech sounds in standardized symbols
- Elision, assimilation and liaison: connected speech processes
- Nigerian English interference: confusion of /ɪ/ and /iː/; /ɒ/ and /ɔː/; adding vowels to consonant clusters
- Intonation patterns: falling tone (statements, commands); rising tone (yes/no questions); rise-fall (surprise)

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Listen to a recorded text and transcribe 10 words using IPA symbols, then compare with a classmate's transcription
2. Practise 10 minimal pairs (e.g. ship/sheep, pool/pull, fan/van) in pairs: one student says the word, the other identifies which of the pair was said
3. Record yourself reading a paragraph and identify three words where your pronunciation differs from RP; explain the phonemic difference

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> WAEC Oral English section tests: identifying correct word stress, choosing the option with a different vowel sound, and phoneme identification from IPA symbols or descriptions
> JAMB tests phonemes, rhymes, stress patterns and silent letters — learn the IPA chart and practise applying it to common WAEC/JAMB vocabulary
> Trap: Nigerian mother-tongue interference often causes /ɪ/ (short) and /iː/ (long) confusion — know that 'ship' and 'sheep' are different vowel lengths

### Spoken English — Conversation, Interview and Debate
**Class:** SSS2  |  **Topic ID:** `SSS-ENG-001-02`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Demonstrate appropriate register and etiquette in formal conversations
- Conduct and participate in structured interview sessions
- Construct and deliver a debate argument with supporting evidence
- Apply turn-taking, listening and response skills in group discussions
- Use appropriate stress and intonation in formal spoken contexts

#### 📖 Notes
- Register: informal (casual, slang) vs. formal (professional, academic) — context determines choice
- Conversation conventions: greetings, topic introduction, turn-taking, topic change, closing
- Telephonic conversation: opening, identifying oneself, stating purpose, message-taking, closing
- Job interview: preparation (research, attire), responding to questions (STAR method), body language, questions to ask
- Debate structure: proposition and opposition; first speaker introduces motion; subsequent speakers rebut; summary speaker
- Argument construction: claim → evidence → reasoning → conclusion
- Group discussion roles: chairperson, secretary, contributor, timekeeper
- Formal speeches: vote of thanks, welcome address, valedictory speech — structure and language
- Non-verbal communication: eye contact, posture, gestures
- Paralinguistic features: volume, pace, pause, intonation for emphasis
- Persuasive language: rhetorical questions, anaphora, rule of three
- Critical listening: note-taking, identifying bias, evaluating evidence

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Role-play a job interview for a banking position: one student is the interviewer, another the applicant; class evaluates using a rubric
2. Hold a class debate: 'Social media has done more harm than good in Nigeria' — each side prepares 3 main arguments with evidence
3. Listen to a radio discussion on education, take notes, and present a 2-minute summary to the class

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> WAEC Oral section tests register appropriateness, conversation completion, and debate/discussion language — know formal connectives (however, furthermore, nevertheless)
> JAMB tests formal vs. informal register identification and conversation appropriateness questions
> Key tip: in debate questions, use signposting language ('Firstly…', 'My opponent claims… however…', 'In conclusion…') — examiners look for structure

## Reading Comprehension

### Reading Comprehension Strategies and Vocabulary in Context
**Class:** SSS1  |  **Topic ID:** `SSS-ENG-002-01`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Apply skimming and scanning techniques to locate information efficiently
- Make inferences and draw conclusions from unseen passages
- Determine meaning of unfamiliar words from context
- Identify the main idea, supporting details and author's purpose
- Analyse figurative language, tone and mood in passages

#### 📖 Notes
- Skimming: reading quickly for general idea/main point without reading every word
- Scanning: moving eyes rapidly to locate specific information (dates, names, figures)
- Intensive reading: careful, detailed reading to understand every word and implication
- Main idea vs. supporting details: topic sentence usually comes first or last in a paragraph
- Inference: reading between the lines — using clues in the text to understand what is not stated directly
- Context clues for vocabulary: definition clues, contrast clues, example clues, synonym clues
- Figurative language: metaphor, simile, personification, hyperbole, irony, allusion
- Tone: author's attitude toward subject (formal, satirical, sympathetic, critical, humorous)
- Mood: emotional atmosphere created in the reader (suspense, joy, melancholy)
- Summary writing: identify main points, reduce to 1/3 of original length, use own words, avoid opinions
- Point-Evidence-Explanation (PEE) method for comprehension answers
- Types of passages tested: expository, narrative, descriptive, argumentative, dialogue

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Take an unseen newspaper editorial; skim for 2 minutes, then answer 5 main-idea questions before re-reading closely
2. Underline 5 unfamiliar words in a passage, guess meaning from context, then verify with a dictionary and discuss accuracy
3. Write a 100-word summary of a 300-word passage, then compare with a partner's — identify what was missed or wrongly included

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> WAEC comprehension carries high marks — answer in COMPLETE sentences; reference evidence from the passage; do NOT lift long phrases word-for-word
> JAMB tests vocabulary-in-context options — the correct answer fits contextually, not just definitionally; eliminate options that change the meaning of the sentence
> Strategy: answer questions in order but save the summary/précis for last; if one question stumps you, move on and return

## Writing Skills

### Essay Writing — Narrative, Descriptive, Expository and Argumentative
**Class:** SSS1  |  **Topic ID:** `SSS-ENG-003-01`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Distinguish between narrative, descriptive, expository and argumentative essay types
- Plan and organise essays using outlines
- Write coherent, well-structured essays of appropriate length
- Use appropriate language features for each essay type
- Edit and proofread essays for grammatical accuracy and style

#### 📖 Notes
- Narrative essay: tells a story from personal experience; first-person perspective; past tense; chronological order; vivid detail
- Descriptive essay: paints a picture with words; sensory details (sight, sound, smell, taste, touch); spatial organization
- Expository essay: explains or informs; third-person; logical organization; facts and examples; formal language
- Argumentative essay: takes a clear position; provides evidence and reasoning; acknowledges and rebuts counter-arguments; persuasive language
- Essay structure: introduction (hook + background + thesis), body paragraphs (topic sentence + development + link), conclusion (restate thesis + summary + broader significance)
- Paragraph development: PEEL (Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link)
- Coherence devices: transitional words (however, therefore, in addition, consequently)
- Vocabulary range: avoid repetition; use synonyms, varied sentence structures, active voice
- Letter writing: formal (complaint, application, official request) and informal (to a friend/relative)
- Formal letter layout: sender's address, date, recipient's address, salutation (Dear Sir/Madam), subject line, body, complimentary close, signature
- Speech writing: address audience, use rhetorical devices, persuasive register
- Report writing: headings, numbered sections, objective third-person language

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Write a 450-word narrative essay on 'The day I learned an important lesson', then swap with a partner for peer editing using a checklist
2. Write a formal letter to the Lagos State Ministry of Education requesting more science laboratories for your school
3. Convert a given argumentative essay from biased (one-sided) to balanced by adding a counter-argument paragraph

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> WAEC essay carries 50 marks — 10 for content, 10 for organisation, 10 for expression, 10 for mechanical accuracy; examiners penalise heavily for off-topic, poor paragraphing and grammatical errors
> JAMB tests formal letter format, essay type identification and register appropriateness in a multiple-choice format
> Key: write AT LEAST 450 words for WAEC essay; start with an engaging first sentence; end with a definitive conclusion — do not leave the essay hanging

## Grammar and Language Use

### Parts of Speech, Syntax and Sentence Structure
**Class:** SSS1  |  **Topic ID:** `SSS-ENG-004-01`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Identify and use the eight parts of speech correctly
- Construct grammatically correct simple, compound, complex and compound-complex sentences
- Apply correct subject-verb agreement in all contexts
- Use tense forms accurately in writing and speech
- Identify and correct common grammatical errors

#### 📖 Notes
- Parts of speech: noun (types: proper, common, abstract, collective, countable/uncountable), pronoun, verb (transitive/intransitive, auxiliary, modal), adjective, adverb, preposition, conjunction, interjection
- Phrases: noun phrase, verb phrase, adjective phrase, adverb phrase, prepositional phrase
- Clauses: independent (main) clause vs. dependent (subordinate) clause
- Sentence types by structure: simple (one main clause), compound (two main clauses joined by coordinating conjunction), complex (main + subordinate), compound-complex
- Sentence types by purpose: declarative, interrogative, imperative, exclamatory
- Subject-verb agreement rules: singular subject → singular verb; collective nouns (committee, team) take singular verb; 'neither…nor' and 'either…or' agree with nearer subject
- Tenses: simple (present, past, future), continuous/progressive, perfect, perfect continuous — 12 tense forms
- Active and passive voice: transformation and appropriate use
- Direct and indirect (reported) speech: tense backshift, pronoun changes, time/place adverb changes
- Concord errors common in Nigerian English: 'they was', 'he have', 'the news are'
- Common grammatical errors: dangling modifiers, double negatives, misuse of 'whom'/'who'
- Punctuation: comma splice, full stop, semicolons, colons, apostrophes, inverted commas

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Analyse and label every word in 5 complex sentences by part of speech and function
2. Rewrite 10 sentences with subject-verb agreement errors, explaining the rule applied
3. Convert 5 active sentences to passive and 5 passive to active, noting when passive is preferred (scientific writing)

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> WAEC tests concord, tense, active/passive, reported speech and punctuation — grammar section is 10% of total; learn the 'neither…nor' rule and collective nouns
> JAMB tests every grammar area in multiple choice — know irregular verbs, preposition collocations (depend ON, good AT, interested IN)
> Trap: Nigerian English 'they said I should come' is correct Nigerian idiom but NOT standard English — avoid in formal written contexts

## Literature in Language

### Literary Devices and Appreciation of Poetry and Prose
**Class:** SSS2  |  **Topic ID:** `SSS-ENG-005-01`

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
- Identify and explain major literary devices in poems and prose extracts
- Analyse themes, characterisation and setting in prescribed texts
- Write critical appreciations of unseen poems
- Compare literary texts on the basis of theme, style and language
- Interpret figurative and symbolic language in literary contexts

#### 📖 Notes
- Figures of speech: simile (explicit comparison using 'like' or 'as'), metaphor (implicit comparison), personification, hyperbole, oxymoron, paradox, irony, pun, alliteration, assonance, onomatopoeia
- Narrative techniques: first-person vs. third-person narration; unreliable narrator; stream of consciousness
- Plot structure: exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, denouement/resolution
- Characterisation: direct (narrator tells us) vs. indirect (shown through speech, action, others' reactions)
- Theme: central idea or message; distinguish from subject (topic)
- Setting: time and place; how it creates atmosphere and affects character
- Conflict types: person vs. person, person vs. nature, person vs. self, person vs. society
- Poetry elements: rhyme scheme (ABAB, AABB), metre, rhythm, stanza, line, refrain
- Types of poetry: lyric, narrative, dramatic, epic, sonnet, ballad, free verse, ode
- Tone and mood in poetry: how word choice and imagery create emotional effect
- Symbolism and allegory: objects/characters representing abstract ideas
- Critical appreciation framework: title, context, theme, language/style, tone, structure, overall effect

#### ✏️ Activities
1. Write a critical appreciation of an unseen poem using the framework: title–context–theme–language–tone–structure–effect
2. Identify and explain six literary devices in a given prose extract, quoting the text and explaining the effect of each
3. Compare two poems on the same theme (e.g. war, nature) in a structured essay: what they share and how they differ

#### 🏆 Exam Focus
> WAEC tests unseen poetry appreciation and literary device identification; always QUOTE from the text and explain the EFFECT (not just identify the device)
> JAMB tests recognition of literary terms and identification of devices — know at least 20 devices with examples
> Trap: saying 'this metaphor makes the poem more interesting' is too vague — say 'the metaphor of the eagle for freedom creates a sense of limitless aspiration'
