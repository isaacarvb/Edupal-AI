SPEECH WORK: LISTENING AND SPEAKING – REVISION OF PURE VOWEL SOUNDS
Objective

By the end of this detailed lesson, students should be able to:

Define and explain the concept of a pure vowel (monophthong) versus a diphthong.

Accurately identify and list all twelve (12) pure vowel sounds by their phonetic symbols.

Orally produce all 12 sounds with correct lip and tongue placement.

Distinguish between long and short vowels in spoken and written minimal pairs.

Introduction

Speech sounds are the foundation of clear communication. Vowel sounds are particularly important as they form the "peak" of every syllable. A Pure Vowel, or Monophthong, is a single, unchanging vowel sound produced by a fixed position of the tongue and lips (e.g., the 'ee' in 'see'). This is different from a diphthong, which is a glide from one vowel position to another (e.g., the 'oi' in 'boy').
We will review the 12 pure vowels, grouping them by tongue position (Front, Central, Back) and length (Short vs. Long).
The Short Vowel Sounds (7)

Front Vowels (Tongue forward in the mouth):

/ɪ/: (Short 'i') A high-front vowel.

Examples: pit, sit, him, busy, village

/e/: (Short 'e') A mid-front vowel.
Examples: pen, bed, head, said, many

/æ/: (Short 'a') A low-front vowel.

Examples: cat, man, bag, apple, plaid

Central Vowels (Tongue in the middle): 4. /ʌ/: (Short 'u') A low-mid central vowel. * Examples: cut, luck, son, blood, enough 5. /ə/: (The Schwa) An unstressed, mid-central vowel. This is the most common sound in English. * Examples: about, teacher, doctor, pencil, photograph
Back Vowels (Tongue pulled back): 6. /ɒ/: (Short 'o') A low-back, rounded vowel. * Examples: not, pot, cot, wash, what 7. /ʊ/: (Short 'oo') A high-back, rounded vowel. * Examples: put, foot, could, woman, book
The Long Vowel Sounds (5) Front Vowels:
/iː/: (Long 'ee') A high-front vowel.

Examples: sheep, feet, sea, me, believe

Central Vowels: 2. /ɜː/: (Long 'er') A mid-central vowel. * Examples: bird, learn, fur, work, journey
Back Vowels: 3. /ɑː/: (Long 'ah') A low-back vowel. * Examples: father, car, star, pass, clerk 4.
/ɔː/: (Long 'or') A mid-back, rounded vowel. * Examples: door, board, caught, war, four, all 5.
/uː/: (Long 'oo') A high-back, rounded vowel. * Examples: food, blue, loop, group, through

Practical Drill: Minimal Pairs

Minimal pairs are two words that sound identical except for one sound. This is the best way to practice listening and speaking.
/ɪ/ vs /iː/: ship/sheep, sit/seat, fill/feel, grin/green

/e/ vs /æ/: pen/pan, bed/bad, met/mat

/ʌ/ vs /ɑː/: cut/cart, bun/barn, cup/carp
/ɒ/ vs /ɔː/: pot/port, cot/caught, shot/short

/ʊ/ vs /uː/: pull/pool, full/fool, look/Luke

GRAMMAR: REVISION OF PARTS OF SPEECH – NOUNS, PRONOUNS, VERBS AND ADJECTIVES
Objective

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

Define and provide multiple examples for each of the four parts of speech.

Differentiate between the sub-classes of each part of speech (e.g., abstract vs. concrete nouns, auxiliary vs. lexical verbs).
Identify the function of these parts of speech within a sentence (e.g., noun as a subject, adjective as a modifier).
Correctly apply the rules of adjectives (e.g., order of adjectives).

Introduction

Words are the building blocks of sentences, and each word has a specific job. We call these jobs "Parts of Speech." Today, we review the four most important ones: the "naming" words (Nouns), the "replacing" words (Pronouns), the "action/state" words (Verbs), and the "describing" words (Adjectives).
Content Detail

Nouns (The Naming Words)

Definition: Nouns name people, places, things, animals, or ideas.

Classification:

Proper Nouns: Specific names (capitalized). Examples: Mary, Lagos, Monday, Google.
Common Nouns: General names. Examples: girl, city, day, company.

Abstract Nouns: Ideas, feelings, concepts (cannot be touched). Examples: happiness, courage, time, danger.
Concrete Nouns: Things that can be perceived by the senses. Examples: table, smoke, music, phone.
Countable Nouns: Can be counted. Examples: three books, many chairs.

Uncountable (Mass) Nouns: Cannot be counted (usually). Examples: water, advice, furniture, rice.
Collective Nouns: Names for a group. Examples: team, family, crowd, committee, flock.
Functions: Nouns can be the Subject (who/what does the verb), Object (who/what receives the action), or Complement (renames the subject).
Pronouns (The Replacing Words)

Definition: Pronouns replace nouns to avoid repetition.

Classification:

Personal Pronouns: Represent people/things.

Subject: I, you, he, she, it, we, they. (They went home.)

Object: me, you, him, her, it, us, them. (She saw them.)

Possessive Pronouns: Show ownership.

Determiner (Adjective): my, your, his, her, its, our, their. (That is my book.)
Pronoun: mine, yours, his, hers, its, ours, theirs. (That book is mine.)

Reflexive Pronouns: Reflect back to the subject. Examples: myself, yourself, himself, themselves. (He hurt himself.)
Demonstrative Pronouns: Point to specific things. Examples: this, that, these, those. (This is my pen.)
Interrogative Pronouns: Ask questions. Examples: who, whom, whose, what, which. (Who is at the door?)
Verbs (The Action/State Words)

Definition: Verbs express action or a state of being. No sentence is complete without one.
Classification:

Main/Lexical Verbs: Show the main action/state. Examples: run, eat, think, love.

Auxiliary (Helping) Verbs: Help the main verb (show tense, mood, etc.).

Primary: be, have, do. (She is running. He has eaten.)

Modal: can, could, will, would, shall, should, may, might, must. (You
must go.)

Transitive Verbs: Take a direct object. Example: He kicked the ball. (Kicked what? The ball.)
Intransitive Verbs: Do not take a direct object. Example: The baby slept. (Slept what? No answer.)
Linking (Copular) Verbs: Link the subject to a noun or adjective. Examples: is, are, was, seem, become, feel. (She is a doctor. He seems happy.)
Adjectives (The Describing Words)

Definition: Adjectives modify (describe or limit) nouns or pronouns.

Function: They answer questions like: What kind? How many? Which one? Whose?

Classification:

Descriptive (Qualitative): beautiful, tall, red, happy

Quantitative: some, many, few, little, all

Numeral: one, two, first, second

Demonstrative: this, that, these, those

Possessive: my, your, his, her, its, our, their
Order of Adjectives: When using multiple adjectives, they follow a specific order (OSASCOMP): Opinion (lovely), Size (small), Age (old), Shape (round), Colour (red), Origin (Italian), Material (leather), Purpose (racing) ... Example: "a beautiful small new red leather bag."

READING & COMPREHENSION: REVIEW OF THE SQ3R READING TECHNIQUE

Objective

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

Define SQ3R and state its purpose as an active reading strategy.

Describe, in detail, the actions required for each of the five steps.

Apply the 'Q' (Question) step by turning sample headings into effective questions.

Justify why SQ3R leads to better comprehension and retention than passive reading.

Introduction

Most students read by passing their eyes over the words from beginning to end. This is passive reading, and it's why we forget so much! SQ3R is an active reading strategy. It's a 5-step "treasure map" for your textbooks that forces your brain to engage with the material, making you a hunter for information, not just a passive observer.
The 5 Steps in Detail

S = SURVEY (To get the big picture)

Action: Before you read, skim the chapter for 1-2 minutes. Look at:

Title, Headings, and Subheadings: What is the chapter about?

Introduction & Conclusion/Summary: These give you the main ideas.

Pictures, Charts, or Graphs: Visuals often summarize key points.

Bold or Italicized Words: These are key vocabulary.

End-of-Chapter Questions: These tell you what the author thinks is important.

Purpose: To create a mental "skeleton" or "map" of the chapter before you fill it in with details.
Q = QUESTION (To create a purpose for reading)
Action: Turn every heading and subheading into a question. Use the "5 Ws and H": Who, What, When, Where, Why, How.
Heading: "The Causes of the Nigerian Civil War"

Your Questions: "What were the main causes of the war?" "Who were the key figures?" "When did it start?"
Purpose: This gives your brain a specific mission. You are now actively searching for answers, not just reading words.
R¹ = READ (To find the answers)

Action: Read the section under each heading, actively looking for the answers to the questions you just formed.
Read with a pencil or highlighter.

Mark or underline the answers when you find them.

Make brief notes in the margin.

Pay attention to the bold words and visuals you surveyed.

Purpose: To actively engage with the text and extract the most important information.

R² = RECITE (To build memory)

Action: After finishing a section, look away from the book and try to answer your question(s) aloud or by writing a summary sentence in your own words.
Purpose: This is the most crucial step for memory. It moves information from your short-term memory (which forgets in seconds) to your long-term memory. If you can't say it in your own words, you don't understand it yet.
R³ = REVIEW (To lock in the learning)

Action:

Immediate Review: When you finish the entire chapter, look over your notes and the headings. See if you can still answer your questions.
Later Review: Spend 5-10 minutes reviewing your notes 24 hours later, and again a week later.
Purpose: This overcomes forgetting and builds lasting, permanent knowledge.

Evaluation

How does turning a heading into a question change the way you read the section that follows?

Assignment

Explain why you must do the 'Survey' step first, before you Read.
COMPOSITION: WRITING AN OUTLINE FOR A NARRATIVE ESSAY – HOW I SPENT MY LAST HOLIDAY
Objective

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

Define a narrative essay and its purpose.

Identify the five essential "ingredients" of a good narrative (Plot, Setting, Character, Theme, Point of View).
Understand the function of each part of an outline (Introduction, Body, Conclusion).

Construct a detailed, logical outline for the given topic, including a hook, thesis, and sensory details.
Introduction

A Narrative Essay is a story, but it's more than just a story. It's a story (usually personal) told with a specific point or purpose (a lesson or main idea). An outline is the skeleton or blueprint for your essay. A house built without a plan will collapse, and an essay written without an outline will be disorganized, repetitive, and confusing.
The Key Ingredients of a Narrative

Plot: The sequence of events (what happens first, next, last). It needs a clear beginning, middle (with a climax or main event), and end.
Setting: When and where the story takes place.

Characters: The people in the story (mainly, "I").

Theme: The "so what?" – the main lesson or feeling the reader should get (e.g., "the importance of family," "a new experience can be scary but rewarding").
Point of View: Usually told in the 1st Person ("I", "me", "my").

Content Detail: How to Structure the Outline

We will use the topic: "How I Spent My Last Holiday"
INTRODUCTION (The "Grabber")

A. The Hook: A catchy first sentence to grab the reader's attention.

Types of Hooks: A line of dialogue ("Are we there yet?"), a vivid description ("The smell of my grandmother's cooking always meant the holiday had truly begun."), or a surprising statement ("My last holiday was a complete disaster.").
B. Background/Context: Give brief details. When was the holiday? Where did you go?
Who were you with?

C. Thesis Statement: The main point of your story. It states the topic and hints at the overall feeling or lesson.
Example Thesis: "My last holiday, spent in my village with my cousins, started out boring but turned into an unforgettable adventure that taught me the importance of family."
BODY (The "Story")

This is where the plot unfolds in chronological order (time order). Each paragraph should cover one main idea.
A. Body Paragraph 1: The Beginning / The Setup

Topic Sentence: Introduce the start of the holiday.

Details: The journey, arriving at the destination, first impressions.

B. Body Paragraph 2: The Main Event / Climax

Topic Sentence: Introduce the most important or exciting part of the story.

Sensory Details (Show, Don't Tell):

Sight: What did you see? (e.g., "the bright green mangoes")

Sound: What did you hear? (e.g., "the sound of the river")

Smell: What did you smell? (e.g., "the smoky scent of the firewood")

Actions & Dialogue: What happened? What did people say?
C. Body Paragraph 3: The Winding Down / Resolution

Topic Sentence: Describe what happened after the main event.

Details: The last few days, feelings of happiness or sadness, packing up, the journey home.
CONCLUSION (The "Reflection")

A. Restate Thesis: Reword your thesis statement (from the introduction).

B. Reflection: This is the most important part.

Reflect on the meaning of the story.

Why was this holiday memorable? What did you learn? How did it change you?

C. Concluding Sentence: A final thought that wraps up the essay.

Example: "I returned home not just with souvenirs, but with a new appreciation for my family that I would never forget."

Explain "Show, Don't Tell." Give an example for "I was sad." (e.g., "Tears welled up in my eyes.")
What is the main purpose of the Conclusion in a narrative essay (beyond just ending it)?

LITERATURE IN ENGLISH: REVIEW OF FEATURES OF PROSE (SETTING, THEME, CHARACTERIZATION)
Objective

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

Define prose and differentiate it from poetry.

Define and explain the functions of Setting, Theme, Characterization, and Plot.

Differentiate between Direct and Indirect characterization, providing examples.

Identify different types of characters (e.g., Protagonist, Antagonist, Flat, Round).

Analyze a simple story to identify these features.

Introduction

Prose is the "ordinary" language we use in speaking and writing, structured in sentences and paragraphs (e.g., novels, short stories, textbooks, newspapers). It is different from Poetry, which uses metric rhythm and verse. When we read prose fiction (novels, short stories), we use "tools" to understand what the author is doing. The most important tools are Setting, Character, Plot, and Theme.
Setting

Definition: The time (e.g., 1960, modern-day, Tuesday) and place (e.g., Lagos, a school, a forest) where the story occurs.
Functions of Setting:

Creates Mood/Atmosphere: A dark, stormy night (scary) vs. a bright, sunny beach (happy).
Provides Context: A story set in 1850 will have different technology and social rules than a story set today.
Reveals Character: Is the character's room messy or neat?
Can be an Antagonist (Conflict): A character trying to survive in the desert or on a deserted island.
Characterization

Definition: The art of creating and describing characters in a story.

Methods of Characterization:

Direct Characterization: The author tells you the character's personality.

Example: "Dele was a lazy and unkind boy."

Indirect Characterization: The author shows you the personality through the character's:
Speech (What they say)

Thoughts (What they think)

Effect on others (How others react to them)

Actions (What they do)

Looks (How they dress/appear)

(Acronym: STEAL)

Types of Characters:

Protagonist: The main character (the "hero").

Antagonist: The force or character in conflict with the protagonist (the "villain").

Round Character: Complex, multi-sided, realistic (like a real person).

Flat Character: Simple, one-dimensional (e.g., a "mean teacher" with no other personality).
Dynamic Character: A character who changes or grows during the story.

Static Character: A character who stays the same.
Plot

Definition: The sequence of events in a story; what happens.

The Plot Diagram (Freytag's Pyramid):

Exposition: Introduction of characters and setting.

Rising Action: The build-up of conflict and suspense.

Climax: The peak of the action; the turning point of the story.

Falling Action: The events immediately after the climax.

Resolution (Dénouement): The end, where the conflict is resolved and loose ends are tied up.
Theme

Definition: The central idea, message, or moral of the story. It's a universal truth about life.
Theme vs. Topic:

Topic: A single word (e.g., "Love," "War," "Friendship").

Theme: A statement about that topic (e.g., "Love can conquer all," "War is destructive," "True friendship requires sacrifice").
Examples of Common Themes: Good vs. Evil, Courage, Betrayal, Social Justice, The Importance of Family.

SPEECH WORK: REVISION OF CONSONANT SOUNDS

Objective

By the end of this detailed lesson, students should be able to:

Define a consonant sound and differentiate it from a vowel sound based on airflow.

Identify and pronounce several consonant sounds based on their place and manner of articulation.
Define a consonant cluster and differentiate it from a digraph (e.g., 'sh', 'th').

Accurately identify and pronounce initial (2- and 3-member) and final consonant clusters in given words.
Introduction

In Week 1, we learned that vowel sounds are produced with a free, unobstructed flow of air. Consonant sounds are the opposite. They are produced by partially or completely obstructing the flow of air from the lungs through the vocal tract.
A Consonant Cluster (also called a consonant blend) is a group of two or more consonants that appear together in a word, where each consonant sound is pronounced. This is different from a digraph (like /ʃ/ in 'ship' or /θ/ in 'thin'), where two letters team up to make one new sound.
Cluster (Blend): In "brick" (/brɪk/), you hear both the /b/ and the /r/.

Digraph: In "thick" (/θɪk/), the 't' and 'h' combine to make one sound, /θ/.

Revision of Consonant Sounds (Brief)

Consonants are classified by:

Place of Articulation: Where the obstruction happens (e.g., Bilabial - both lips /p/, /b/,
/m/; Alveolar - tongue-tip on tooth ridge /t/, /d/, /n/, /s/, /z/, /l/).
Manner of Articulation: How the air is obstructed (e.g., Plosives/Stops - air is stopped completely /p/, /t/, /k/; Fricatives - air is forced through a narrow gap /f/, /v/, /s/, /z/; Nasals - air escapes through the nose /m/, /n/, /ŋ/).
Voicing: Whether the vocal cords vibrate (Voiced - /b/, /d/, /g/, /v/) or not (Voiceless - /p/,
/t/, /k/, /f/).

Consonant Clusters: Initial Position

These appear at the beginning of a word.

Two-Member Initial Clusters (s-clusters):

/sp/: spot, speak, spin

/st/: stop, stand, stay

/sk/: skin, sky, skip

/sm/: smile, smoke, small

/sn/: snake, snow, snail

/sl/: slip, sleep, slow

Two-Member Initial Clusters (l-clusters):

/pl/: play, plate, please

/bl/: blue, blow, black

/kl/: clean, class, climb

/gl/: glad, glass, glow

/fl/: fly, flat, floor

Two-Member Initial Clusters (r-clusters):

/pr/: pray, price, proud

/br/: bring, bread, brown
/tr/: tree, try, train

/dr/: dry, dream, drink

/kr/: cry, crab, crown

/gr/: grow, green, great

/fr/: from, free, friend

Three-Member Initial Clusters (All start with /s/):

/spl/: splash, split, splendid

/spr/: spring, spray, spread

/str/: street, strong, straight

/skr/: scream, scrub, script

/skw/ (/skw/): square, squeeze, squid

Consonant Clusters: Final Position

These appear at the end of a word.

/st/: last, fast, test

/sk/: mask, task, desk

/ld/: old, cold, field

/nd/: hand, land, send

/nt/: went, plant, tent

/mp/: jump, lamp, camp

/lps/: helps

/ksts/: texts (/tɛksts/)

GRAMMAR: TRANSITIVE AND INTRANSITIVE VERBS

Objective

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

Define and differentiate between Transitive and Intransitive verbs.

Accurately identify the Direct Object (DO) of a Transitive verb.

Define and identify Ditransitive verbs, distinguishing between the Direct Object (DO) and the Indirect Object (IO).
Identify and use ambitransitive verbs (verbs that can be both) correctly in sentences.

Introduction

In Week 1, we learned that a verb expresses action or a state of being. Now, we will classify verbs based on whether they need an "object" to complete their meaning. An object is a noun or pronoun that receives the action of the verb.

Transitive Verbs (V + DO)

Definition: A transitive verb is an action verb that must have a Direct Object (DO).

Direct Object (DO): The noun or pronoun that receives the action. It answers the question What? or Whom? after the verb.
Formula: Subject + Transitive Verb + Direct Object (S + V + DO)

Examples:

Tolu kicked the ball. (Kicked what? ... the ball. "kicked" is transitive; "ball" is DO.)
The teacher praised him. (Praised whom? ... him. "praised" is transitive; "him" is DO.)
Ayo bought a new bag. (Bought what? ... a new bag. "bought" is transitive; "bag" is DO.)
A sentence with only a transitive verb and a subject is incomplete: Tolu kicked. (Kicked what? It feels unfinished.)
Intransitive Verbs (V)

Definition: An intransitive verb is an action verb (or state of being verb) that does not
take an object. It is complete on its own.

Formula: Subject + Intransitive Verb (S + V)

Examples:

The baby cried. (Cried what? No answer. The verb is complete.)

He arrived early. ("Early" is an adverb telling when, not an object receiving an action.)
Birds fly.

These verbs cannot have a direct object. You cannot "cried something" or "arrived something."
Ambitransitive Verbs (Verbs that can be both)

Definition: Many verbs can be either transitive or intransitive, depending on the context.

Examples:

eat:

Transitive: We ate jollof rice. (Ate what? ... jollof rice.)

Intransitive: We ate at 7 pm. (Ate what? No answer. "at 7 pm" is when.)

read:

Transitive: She is reading a book. (Reading what? ... a book.)

Intransitive: She is reading. (The action is complete on its own.)

grow:

Transitive: The farmer grows maize. (Grows what? ... maize.)
Intransitive: The boy grew quickly. (Grew what? No answer.)

Ditransitive Verbs (V + IO + DO)

Definition: A special type of transitive verb that takes two objects: an Indirect Object (IO) and a Direct Object (DO).
Indirect Object (IO): The person/thing that receives the Direct Object. It answers the question To whom? or For whom?. The IO always comes before the DO.
Formula: Subject + Ditransitive Verb + Indirect Object + Direct Object (S + V + IO + DO)
Examples:

My father gave me the money.

Gave what? ... the money (DO)

Gave the money to whom? ... me (IO)

She bought her sister a gift.

Bought what? ... a gift (DO)

Bought the gift for whom? ... her sister (IO)

The teacher told the class a story.

Told what? ... a story (DO)

Told the story to whom? ... the class (IO)

Objective

Introduction

Every time an author writes something—be it a textbook, a novel, a newspaper article, or an advert—they have a reason or a purpose for writing. As active readers, our job is to figure out why they wrote it. Understanding the purpose helps us understand the main idea and evaluate the text properly. Is the author trying to teach us, change our minds, or just make us laugh?

The Main Purposes for Writing (Acronym: PIE-D)

P = to PERSUADE

Goal: To make the reader agree with an opinion, change their mind, or do
something.

Clues: Uses opinions, emotional language, strong adjectives, rhetorical questions, and a call to action. It presents one side as the best.
Examples: Advertisements, political speeches, opinion articles, debate arguments, letters of complaint.
Passage Example: "It is vital that all students stop buying single-use plastic bottles. They are destroying our environment and polluting our oceans. We must act now! Bring a reusable bottle to school tomorrow."
I = to INFORM

Goal: To teach the reader, explain a topic, or give facts and information.

Clues: Uses facts, statistics, dates, definitions, and a formal, neutral tone. It is objective.
Examples: Textbooks, news reports (not opinion articles), encyclopedias, user manuals.
Passage Example: "The Nile River is the longest river in the world, stretching over 6,650 kilometers. It flows north through eleven African countries before emptying into the Mediterranean Sea."
E = to ENTERTAIN

Goal: To provide enjoyment for the reader.

Clues: Uses a story (plot, characters, setting), suspense, humor, or exciting action.

Examples: Novels, short stories, poems, song lyrics, comics, jokes.

Passage Example: "The old door creaked open. Tunde held his breath, peering into the darkness. A pair of red eyes suddenly glowed from the far corner of the room. He screamed."
D = to DESCRIBE

Goal: To paint a picture in the reader's mind using words.

Clues: Uses vivid sensory details (sight, sound, smell, taste, touch) and strong adjectives and adverbs. (This is closely related to Entertaining and will be our focus in Composition).
Examples: Descriptive essays, detailed passages in novels, travelogues.
Passage Example: "The market was a riot of noise and colour. The air was thick with the smell of overripe mangoes, strong spices, and diesel fumes from the buses roaring past."

How to Find the Purpose

Ask these questions as you read:

What is the Tone? Is the author's attitude serious, humorous, angry, objective? (e.g., An angry tone often means persuade).
What is the Diction? What words does the author choose? Are they emotional words (e.g., terrible, wonderful) or factual words (e.g., percentage, dates)?
What are the Text Features? Does the text contain facts and charts (Inform)? Opinions (Persuade)? Characters and dialogue (Entertain)?

Your outline should be organized by senses or by areas of the place (spatial order).

Details: Describe the overall scene. The crowds (sight), the buildings (sight), the constant noise (sound). Use strong verbs and adjectives.

A. Restate Thesis: Reword your thesis (dominant impression).

B. Reflection: Reflect on the feeling the place left you with.

Example: "Leaving the market felt like stepping out of a fast-moving river into quiet, still water."

Example: "Balogun Market is not just a place to buy things; it is the loud, colourful, beating heart of Lagos."

GRAMMAR: MORE ON ACTIVE AND PASSIVE VOICE

Objective

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

Define Active and Passive voice and identify them in sentences.

Identify the 'agent' in a passive sentence.

Accurately convert sentences from Active to Passive voice for various tenses.

Accurately convert sentences from Passive to Active voice.

Explain the main reasons why a writer would choose to use the passive voice.

Introduction

The "voice" of a verb tells us whether the subject of the sentence is performing the action or
receiving the action.

Active Voice: The subject does the action. (e.g., "The cat chased the mouse.")

Passive Voice: The subject receives the action. (e.g., "The mouse was chased by the cat.")

Identifying Active vs. Passive

Active: Subject + Verb + Object

Example: "The principal punished the students." (Subject 'principal' did the punishing).
Passive: Subject (Receiver) + Verb to be + Past Participle + (by + Agent)

Example: "The students were punished by the principal." (Subject 'students'
received the punishment).
The "doer" of the action in a passive sentence is called the agent, introduced with "by".
How to Convert from Active to Passive

The Object of the active sentence becomes the new Subject.

The Verb is changed to: [Verb 'to be' in the original tense] + [Past Participle].

The Subject of the active sentence becomes the Agent (using "by") or is left out.

Tense Conversion Table (Active → Passive)

TenseActive ExamplePassive ConversionSimple PresentHe writes a letter.A letter is written by him.Simple PastHe wrote a letter.A letter was written by him.Present Cont.He is writing a letter.A letter is being written by him.Past Cont.He was writing a letter.A letter was being written by him.Present PerfectHe has written a letter.A letter has been written by him.Past PerfectHe had written a letter.A letter had been written by him.Future (will)He will write a letter.A letter will be written by him.Modal (can)He can write a letter.A letter can be written by him.
When and Why to Use the Passive Voice

Using the active voice is usually stronger and more direct, but the passive voice is very useful in specific situations:
When the agent (doer) is unknown:

"My bicycle was stolen last night." (We don't know who stole it).

When the agent is unimportant or obvious:

"The president was re-elected." (We know he was re-elected by the voters).
"Rice is grown in Nigeria." (It's grown by farmers).

To emphasize the receiver of the action (the subject):

"The student was praised by the principal." (We want to focus on the student, not the principal).
To sound objective and formal (common in science and news):

"The solution was heated to 50°C." (Instead of "The scientist heated the solution...").

Introduction

Good essays do not appear by magic. They are built. Professional writers use a system called The Writing Process to turn their ideas into a clear, organized, and polished final piece. We will learn this 3-stage process:
Pre-writing (Planning)

Writing (Drafting)

Post-writing (Revising, Editing, and Proofreading)

Stage 1: Pre-writing (The Plan)

This is the 'thinking' stage. You do this before you write your first sentence.

A. Brainstorming: Generating all possible ideas on your topic.

Techniques:

Listing: Simply writing down all ideas, words, and phrases that come to mind.
Mind-Mapping: Starting with the topic in a central bubble and drawing "legs" for main ideas, with smaller "twigs" for details.
B. Outlining: Organizing your brainstormed ideas into a logical structure. This is the "skeleton" of your essay, which we practiced in Weeks 1 and 2. It includes:
I. Introduction (Hook, Thesis)

II. Body Paragraph 1 (Topic Sentence, Details)

III. Body Paragraph 2 (Topic Sentence, Details)

C. The Conclusion: This is the final paragraph that wraps up the essay. It MUST do two things:
Restate the Thesis: Say your main point again, but in different words.

Give a Final Thought: End with a reflection, a lesson learned, or a strong final image.

Move: Would this sentence be better in a different paragraph?

Use the acronym CUPS:

Capitalization: Are all proper nouns and first words capitalized?

Spelling: Are all words spelled correctly?

Part A: Features of Folktales Introduction
A Folktale is a story that has been passed down from one generation to the next through oral tradition (speaking and listening), long before it was ever written down. Every culture has them. The main purpose of folktales is usually didactic—meaning they are designed to teach a moral lesson about life, behavior, or values.

Common Features

Oral Tradition: They are "stories of the people" and often begin with a formula like, "Once upon a time..." and end with "...they lived happily ever after" or a similar phrase.
Didactic (Moral Lesson): This is the most important feature. They teach lessons (e.g., "why the tortoise has a cracked shell" teaches a lesson about greed; "the hare and the tortoise" teaches about consistency).
Anthropomorphism: This is when animals or objects behave like humans. They talk, wear clothes, have jobs, and show human emotions like jealousy, wisdom, or cunning. (e.g., Anansi the Spider, Br'er Rabbit).
Stock Characters: The characters are often simple and flat, representing one idea (e.g., the "wise old woman," the "evil stepmother," the "innocent child," the "cunning trickster").

A Detailed Review of Simple, Continuous, and Perfect Tenses

Objective

Explain the function of each tense (e.g., simple present for habits, present continuous for current action, simple past for finished action).

Introduction

Tense is the grammatical tool we use to place an action in time. In Week 2, we saw how the verb 'to be' and 'past participles' are used to form the passive voice. Today, we will review the main tenses in the active voice, focusing on when and why we use each one.
We will organize the tenses by their aspect (Simple, Continuous, Perfect).

The SIMPLE Tenses

Function: They describe actions as complete facts, habits, or single events.

Simple Present:

Form: Base verb (e.g., play) or Base verb + s (e.g., plays).

Use: (1) For habits and routines ("I brush my teeth every day."); (2) For general truths ("The sun rises in the east.").
Simple Past:

Form: Base verb + ed (e.g., played) or Irregular form (e.g., ate, went).
Use: For a completed action at a specific time in the past ("We watched the film
yesterday.").

Simple Future:

Form: will + Base verb.

Use: For an action that will happen in the future ("I will see you tomorrow.").

The CONTINUOUS (or PROGRESSIVE) Tenses

Function: They describe actions that are in progress, ongoing, or unfinished at a certain time.
Form: [verb 'to be'] + [verb + ing (present participle)]

Present Continuous:

Form: am/is/are + playing

Use: For an action happening right now ("She is reading a book.") or around this time ("I am studying for my exams.").
Past Continuous:

Form: was/were + playing

Use: For an ongoing action in the past that was interrupted by another action ("I
was watching TV when the light went out.").

Future Continuous:

Form: will be + playing

Use: For an ongoing action at a specific time in the future ("This time tomorrow, I
will be writing my exam.").

The PERFECT Tenses

Function: They describe actions that are completed in relation to another point in time.

Form: [verb 'to have'] + [Past Participle (e.g., played, eaten)]
Present Perfect:

Form: has/have + played

Use: For an action that started in the past and continues to the present ("I have lived here for five years.") OR a past action at an unspecified time ("I have seen that film before.").
Past Perfect:

Form: had + played

Use: For a past action that was completed before another past action ("The film
had already started when we arrived.").

Future Perfect:

Form: will have + played

Use: For an action that will be completed before a specific time in the future ("By 8 pm, I will have finished my homework.").

Introduction

First-Person POV:

Pronouns: "I," "me," "my," "we," "us."

Third-Person POV:

Pronouns: "He," "she," "it," "they," "them."

Narrator: The narrator is an outside voice, not a character in the story.

Types:

Limited: The narrator sticks closely to one character (usually the protagonist). We see what they see and know what they think, but not others. (e.g., "Harry Potter").

Production of /p/ (Voiceless Bilabial Plosive)

Voicing: Voiceless (your vocal cords do not vibrate).

Place: Bilabial (you stop the air by pressing your two lips together).

Manner: Plosive (you build up pressure behind your lips and then release it).

Place: Alveolar (you stop the air by pressing the tip of your tongue against the alveolar ridge, which is the hard ridge just behind your top teeth).
Manner: Plosive (you build up pressure behind your tongue and then release it).

Practice: Place your tongue tip just behind your top teeth, build up air, and release. It should sound like /t/, /t/, /t/.
Examples: tan, top, water, late, cat, passed

GRAMMAR: PAST PERFECT TENSE

The Past Perfect Tense

Objective

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

Introduction

In Week 4, we reviewed the simple, continuous, and perfect tenses. Today, we will do a deep dive into one of the most important (and sometimes confusing) tenses: the Past Perfect.
Its main job is to show that one action in the past was finished before another action in the past happened. It's the "past of the past."

Form

The form is simple and never changes: had + [Past Participle]

I had eaten.

She had left.

They had finished.

It had been (passive voice).
Function: The "Past of the Past"

Imagine a timeline of yesterday. Two things happened:

I ate breakfast (at 8:00 AM).

My friend arrived (at 9:00 AM).

Both are in the simple past. But if I want to tell a story about 9:00 AM, I need to show that the eating was already finished.
"When my friend arrived (Simple Past), I had already eaten (Past Perfect)."

The Past Perfect action (had eaten) is "Past Event 1". The Simple Past action (arrived) is "Past Event 2".

Common Use with Conjunctions

before / by the time: The Past Perfect is used for the action that was finished.

"She had finished her homework before her mother came home."

"By the time the police arrived, the thieves had escaped."

after: The Past Perfect is used for the action that happened first.

"After she had finished her homework, she watched TV."

when: (Be careful! 'When' can be tricky)

"When I got to the station, the train had left." (I missed the train. The leaving happened first.)
"When I got to the station, the train left." (I arrived just in time to see it leave. The actions happened at the same time.)

This week, we are shifting from Prose (novels) to Drama (plays). A play is a story written to be

Stage Directions: Notes from the author (often in italics or brackets) that describe the setting or the character's actions and tone. (e.g., [shouting angrily], [He looks at the floor, ashamed]).

S - Speech: What does the character say? Do they speak politely or rudely? Do they use big words or simple ones? Do they talk a lot or stay quiet?
T - Thoughts: We do not know their thoughts (unless they have a soliloquy—a long speech to themselves when they are alone).
E - Effect on Others: How do other characters react to them? Are people afraid of them? Do they respect them?
A - Actions: What does the character do? (These are found in the stage directions). Do they help others? Do they steal? Do they run from a fight?
L - Looks: The author's stage directions might describe their costume or appearance, which gives clues.

Objective

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

Introduction

A conjunction is a word that joins words, phrases, or clauses. They are the "glue" of English. Using them correctly allows you to move from writing simple sentences ("I am tired. I want to sleep.") to more mature, complex sentences ("I want to sleep because I am tired.").
There are three main types of conjunctions:

Coordinating Conjunctions

These join two equal and independent clauses. The easiest way to remember them is the acronym
FANBOYS:

For (meaning 'because')

And (adds information)

Nor (adds a negative)

But (shows contrast)

Or (shows choice)
Yet (shows contrast, like 'but')

So (shows result)

Rule: When joining two full sentences (independent clauses), use a comma before the conjunction.
Example: "I like rice, but my brother prefers beans."

Example: "She studied hard, so she passed the test."

Subordinating Conjunctions

These join an independent clause (a full sentence) with a dependent clause (an incomplete thought). They show the relationship between the two clauses (e.g., time, cause, contrast).
Common Examples: because, since, as, although, though, while, when, after, before, if, unless, until.
Rule 1: Independent Clause + Dependent Clause

Example: "He went to school although he was sick."

Rule 2: Dependent Clause, + Independent Clause (If the sentence starts with the conjunction, use a comma).
Example: "Although he was sick, he went to school."

Correlative Conjunctions

These are pairs of conjunctions that work together.

both... and... ("She likes both rice and beans.")

either... or... ("You can have either the tea or the coffee.")

neither... nor... ("He is neither tall nor short.")

not only... but also... ("He is not only intelligent but also kind.")
whether... or... ("I don't know whether to go or stay.")

A request is the act of politely or formally asking for something or asking someone to do something. In English, we use modal verbs to make our requests sound more polite and less like a direct command.
Using 'Will':

Form: Will you + (base form of verb)...?

Usage: This is a common and polite way to make a request, but it is often more direct or casual. It's common among friends, family, or when asking for a simple, expected action. It can sometimes sound like a polite instruction.
Examples:

"Will you pass the salt, please?" (A simple request at the dinner table)

"Will you be quiet?" (This sounds more like a command)

"Will you help me carry this?" (A direct request for help)

Using 'Could':

Form: Could you + (base form of verb)...?

Usage: This is a very polite and common way to ask for a favour. It is "safer" and more formal than 'will.' It sounds less like a command and more like a genuine question, acknowledging that the other person has the option to say no.
Examples:

"Could you open the window, please?" (More polite than "Will you open...")

"Could you please tell me the time?"

"Could you lend me your pen for a moment?"

Using 'May':

Form: May I + (base form of verb)...?

Usage: This is the most formal of the three and is used specifically to ask for permission. You are not asking the other person to do something for you; you are asking if you are allowed to do something.
Examples:

"May I use your telephone?" (Asking for permission)

"May I be excused?" (Asking to leave the room)

"May I ask a question?" (A very polite way to interrupt or speak to a teacher)

Comparing Formality (Lowest to Highest):

Command: "Open the door." (Rude)

Request with 'Will': "Will you open the door?" (Polite, but direct)

Request with 'Could': "Could you open the door?" (More polite and considerate)

Request with 'May' (Permission): "May I open the door?" (Asking if you are allowed to do it)

Topic: Direct and Indirect (Reported) Speech

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

There are two ways to tell someone what another person said:

Direct Speech: This is when we quote the exact words of the speaker. We use quotation marks ("...") to show the exact words.
Example: Tunde said, "I am hungry."

Example: The teacher asked, "Where is your homework?"

Indirect Speech (or Reported Speech): This is when we report what the speaker said, without using their exact words. We do not use quotation marks. We often use the word 'that' for statements.
Example: Tunde said that he was hungry.

Example: The teacher asked where my homework was.

Rules for Changing Direct to Indirect Speech: When we change from Direct to Indirect, three main things must often change:
Change of Pronouns: Pronouns change to reflect the new speaker.

Direct: He said, "I am sick."

Indirect: He said that he was sick.

Direct: She said, "My book is lost."

Indirect: She said that her book was lost.

Change of Tense (The "Back-shift"): The verb tense in the reported clause usually moves one step back into the past.
Present Simple → Past Simple:

Direct: "I like beans." → He said he liked beans.

Present Continuous → Past Continuous:

Direct: "I am reading." → He said he was reading.

Present Perfect → Past Perfect:

Direct: "I have finished." → He said he had finished.

Past Simple → Past Perfect:

Direct: "I ate the food." → He said he had eaten the food.

Modal Verbs:

will → would

can → could

may → might

must → had to

Change of Adverbs of Time and Place:

now → then

today → that day
yesterday → the day before (or the previous day)

tomorrow → the next day (or the following day)

here → there

this → that

these → those

Example (Statement):

Direct: The boy said, "I will do my homework tomorrow."

Indirect: The boy said that he would do his homework the next day.

Changing Questions:

Yes/No Questions: We use 'if' or 'whether'. The sentence changes from a question to a statement (verb moves after the subject).
Direct: She asked, "Are you coming?"

Indirect: She asked if I was coming. (NOT "if was I")

Wh- Questions (What, Why, Where, Who, When): We use the same Wh-word.

Direct: He asked, "Where is the bank?"

Indirect: He asked where the bank was. (NOT "where was the bank")

Lesson Content:
A monophthong is a "pure" vowel sound, where the tongue and lips remain in one position (e.g.,
/i:/ in 'seat', /ɒ/ in 'pot', /u:/ in 'food').

A diphthong, also known as a "vowel glide," is a complex vowel sound that starts in the position of one vowel and glides smoothly into the position of another vowel, all within the same syllable. There are eight common diphthongs in standard English.
The Eight Diphthongs:

/eɪ/ (as in "say" or "gate")

Description: Starts like /e/ (as in 'bet') and glides towards /ɪ/ (as in 'sit').

Common Spellings: 'a_e' (make, late), 'ai' (rain, mail), 'ay' (day, play), 'ei' (vein, reign), 'ey' (they, grey).
Examples: date, face, train, pay, eight, obey.

/aɪ/ (as in "my" or "price")

Description: Starts like /a/ (as in 'father') and glides towards /ɪ/ (as in 'sit').

Common Spellings: 'i_e' (time, like), 'i' (find, mild), 'y' (try, sky), 'ie' (tie, die), 'igh' (high, night).
Examples: nine, file, cry, light, buy, height.

/ɔɪ/ (as in "boy" or "noise")

Description: Starts like /ɔ:/ (as in 'saw') and glides towards /ɪ/ (as in 'sit').

Common Spellings: 'oi' (coin, soil), 'oy' (toy, joy).

Examples: voice, choice, oil, annoy, royal, loyal.

/aʊ/ (as in "how" or "mouse")

Description: Starts like /a/ (as in 'father') and glides towards /ʊ/ (as in 'put').

Common Spellings: 'ou' (out, loud), 'ow' (now, brown).

Examples: house, town, cow, shout, couch, our.
/əʊ/ (or /oʊ/) (as in "go" or "show")

Description: Starts with the central vowel /ə/ (schwa) and glides towards /ʊ/ (as in 'put').
Common Spellings: 'o_e' (home, note), 'o' (so, open), 'oa' (boat, road), 'ow' (low, grow), 'ou' (soul, though).
Examples: old, nose, coat, phone, pillow, moment.

/ɪə/ (as in "here" or "near")

Description: Starts like /ɪ/ (as in 'sit') and glides towards /ə/ (schwa).

Common Spellings: 'eer' (deer, cheer), 'ere' (here, severe), 'ear' (near, fear), 'ier' (pier, fierce).
Examples: clear, dear, beer, career, engineer, idea.

/eə/ (as in "air" or "care")

Description: Starts like /e/ (as in 'bet') and glides towards /ə/ (schwa).

Common Spellings: 'air' (fair, chair), 'are' (care, share), 'ear' (bear, wear), 'ere' (there, where).

/ʊə/ (as in "poor" or "tour")

Description: Starts like /ʊ/ (as in 'put') and glides towards /ə/ (schwa).

What is a Report? A report is a formal, factual, and objective account of an event that has already happened, or an investigation into a specific matter.
Purpose: To inform, to provide facts, and to record an event. It is written for someone in authority (e.g., the Principal, a manager, the Police) who needs to know exactly what happened.
Report vs. Narrative Story (e.g., "An Accident I Saw")

FeatureReportNarrative Story (Composition)PurposeTo inform factuallyTo entertain or share feelingsToneFormal, serious, objectiveInformal, emotional, personalLanguageDirect, precise, no slangDescriptive, imaginative, “flowery”TenseConsistently past tenseCan be past tense; may include dialogueOpinionNo personal opinions or feelingsFull of opinions and feelingsExample“The rider was bleeding.”“I was so scared; my heart beat fast.”

Key Features of a Report:

Formal: No slang, no contractions (e.g., use "did not," not "didn't").

Factual: Only write what happened, what you saw, or what was said. Do not add your personal feelings (e.g., "It was a terrible sight" is an opinion; "Two cars were damaged" is a fact).
Objective: Be neutral. Do not take sides.

Chronological: The events must be in the correct time order (what happened first, second, third...).
Clear: It must be easy to understand.

Outline (Format) of a Report: A formal report must have the following parts:

1. Title: This must be clear and specific. It often starts with "A REPORT ON..."

Example: A REPORT ON THE FIRE OUTBREAK AT THE JSS2 BLOCK ON 10TH OCTOBER, 2025.
2. Introduction: This is one paragraph that answers Who, What, Where, and When.

Example: "On Monday, 10th October, 2025, at about 2:00 p.m., a fire started in the JSS2B classroom..."
3. Body: This is the main part of the report. It gives a detailed, step-by-step account of the event in chronological order. Each new point should be in a new paragraph.
Example: (Paragraph 2: How the fire was discovered. Paragraph 3: What students and teachers did. Paragraph 4: The arrival of the fire service. Paragraph 5: The extent of the damage.)
4. Conclusion: This is a final, short paragraph that summarizes the outcome. It can also include a recommendation if asked.
Example: "The fire was put out by 3:00 p.m. No student was injured, but the classroom and all furniture were completely destroyed. It is recommended that..."

What is Intonation? Intonation is the "music" or "melody" of speech. It is the way our voice rises (goes up) and falls (goes down) when we speak. Intonation is very important because it can change the entire meaning of a sentence, even if the words are the same. It shows our mood (happy, sad, angry) or purpose (asking, telling).
The two main intonation patterns (or tunes) are the Falling Tone and the Rising Tone.

The Falling Tone (Tune 1): The pitch of the voice starts high and falls to a low pitch at the end of the sentence. It sounds final and confident.
When to use it:

Statements (Declarative Sentences):

My name is ↘Bisi.

The sky is ↘blue.

We live in ↘Lagos.

"Wh-" Questions (Questions starting with Who, What, Where, When, Why, How):
What is your ↘name?

Where do you ↘live?

How are you ↘today?

Commands (Imperative Sentences):

Go and sit ↘down.

Open your ↘book.

The Rising Tone (Tune 2): The pitch of the voice starts low and rises to a high pitch at the end. It sounds questioning, incomplete, or polite.
When to use it:

"Yes/No" Questions (Questions that can be answered with 'yes' or 'no'):

Are you ↗coming?

Is your name ↗Bisi?

Do you have a ↗pen?

Listing Items (The voice rises on each item except the last one):

What is Speed Reading? Speed reading is the ability to read and understand text quickly. It is not about reading every single word, but about understanding the main ideas in less time.
Bad Habits That Slow You Down (and How to Stop Them):

Vocalization: This is the habit of "mouthing" the words as you read them.

Sub-vocalization: This is the habit of "saying" every word in your head. This is the biggest barrier to fast reading.
Solution: Try to silence your "inner voice." Chew gum (quietly) or hum to distract the part of your brain that wants to "say" the words.
Regression: This is the habit of your eyes "going back" to re-read words you have already passed. It's often unnecessary.
Solution: Use a "pacer"—like a pen, your finger, or a piece of paper—and move it under the line you are reading. Keep the pacer moving forward at a steady pace and force your eyes to keep up.
Word-by-Word Reading: This is reading one word at a time. It is very slow.

Solution: Learn Phrase Reading.

The Technique of Phrase Reading: Instead of reading: "The / cat / sat / on / the / mat." Train your eyes to "chunk" words into phrases: "(The cat) / (sat on) / (the mat)."
You stop your eyes only 3 times, not 6. This doubles your reading speed.

What is Skimming?

Definition: Skimming is reading very quickly to get a general overview or the main idea
of a text.

Purpose: To find out what the text is about without reading every word.

How to Skim:

Read the Title and any Headings.

Read the first paragraph completely.

Read the first sentence of all the other paragraphs.

Read the last paragraph completely.

Let your eyes float over the rest of the text, looking for key "content" words.

What is Scanning?

Definition: Scanning is moving your eyes very quickly over a text to find a specific piece of information.
Purpose: To find a name, a date, a number, or a specific word, without reading the rest of the text. You already know what you are looking for.
How to Scan:

Keep the specific word or number (e.g., "1995") in your mind.

Move your eyes rapidly down the page (in a "Z" or "S" pattern).

Do not read sentences. Just look for the word.

When you find the word, stop and read the sentence around it.

Examples: You scan a phone book for a name, a dictionary for a word, or a text for a date.
Skimming vs. Scanning:

You skim an article to decide if you want to read it.

You scan an article to find the year it was written.

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

This lesson builds on Week 1's "Reported Statements." Commands and dialogues follow different rules.
Reporting Commands (Positive): A direct command is a "do" instruction (e.g., "Go away," "Sit down").
Rule: We do not use 'that'. We use a reporting verb (like told, ordered, commanded) + object + to + the base form of the verb (the infinitive).
Direct: The teacher said, "Be quiet."

Indirect: The teacher told us to be quiet.

Direct: The general shouted, "Attack!"

Indirect: The general ordered the soldiers to attack.

Reporting Commands (Negative): A negative command is a "don't" instruction (e.g., "Don't talk").
Rule: We use the reporting verb + object + not to + the base form of the verb.

Direct: The mother said, "Don't go out."
Indirect: The mother warned her son not to go out.

Direct: "Don't write on the wall," he said to me.

Indirect: He told me not to write on the wall.

Reporting Requests: A request uses "Please" (e.g., "Please help me").

Rule: We use a "softer" reporting verb (like asked, begged, requested) + object + to + the base form. The word "please" is removed.
Direct: She said, "Please wait for me."

Indirect: She asked me to wait for her.

Direct: "Please, please give me some food," the beggar said.

Indirect: The beggar begged the man to give him some food.

Reporting Dialogue: A dialogue is a conversation between two or more people. To report it, you must report each line, one after the other, using the correct rules for statements, questions, or commands. You must use reporting verbs like 'said,' 'replied,' 'asked,' 'answered,' 'added,' etc., to make it flow.
Direct Dialogue:

Ayo: "What are you doing here, Bisi?"

Bisi: "I am waiting for my mother. She will be here soon."

Ayo: "Please give this book to her."

Indirect (Reported) Dialogue:

Ayo asked Bisi what she was doing there. Bisi replied that she was waiting for her mother and added that she would be there soon. Ayo then asked Bisi to give that book to her.

What is ICT?

ICT is an acronym that stands for Information and Communication Technology. It refers to all the technologies we use to create, find, store, handle, and share information. This includes computers, the internet, mobile phones, software, and more.

Internet: A giant, worldwide network that connects millions of computers, allowing them to share information.
World Wide Web (WWW): The collection of websites and pages that you access using the internet.

Browser: The software you use to access and view websites.

Download: To copy a file (like a song, picture, or document) from the internet to your computer.
Upload: To copy a file from your computer to the internet (e.g., posting a photo to Facebook, sending an email attachment).
Virus (or Malware): A type of harmful software designed to damage your computer or steal your information.
Antivirus: Software designed to find and remove viruses.

Firewall: A security system that protects a computer network from unauthorized access (like a digital "fence").
URL (Uniform Resource Locator): The unique address of a page on the internet (e.g., http://www.google.com).
Social Media: Websites and apps that let users create and share content or to network with other people (e.g., Facebook, TikTok, Instagram, Twitter/X).
CPU (Central Processing Unit): The "brain" of the computer that performs all the calculations.

What is Rhythm? Rhythm is the "beat" or "music" of a poem. It is a pattern of sound created by the arrangement of stressed (strong, emphasized) and unstressed (weak, unemphasized) syllables in a line of poetry. Just like in a song, this pattern creates a regular, musical quality when the poem is read aloud.
Syllables and Stress:

A syllable is a single, unbroken sound of a spoken word. For example, "wa-ter" has two syllables. "Cat" has one syllable. "Beau-ti-ful" has three syllables.
Stress is the emphasis we place on a particular syllable when we speak.

Example: In the word "FAther," the first syllable (FA) is stressed, and the second (ther) is unstressed.
Example: In the word "beLIEVE," the first syllable (be) is unstressed, and the second (LIEVE) is stressed.
Finding the Rhythm in a Poem: Rhythm is created by repeating a pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables. Let's mark the stressed syllables ( / ) and unstressed syllables ( - ).
Nursery Rhyme Example: Twink-le, Twink-le, litt-le star ( / - / / - / / - / / ) This line has a clear DA-da DA-da DA-da DA pattern.
Poetry Example: (from "The Rime of the Ancient Mariner") The Bride-groom hath paced in-to the hall ( - / / - / - / - / ) -> This is more complex.
Let's use a simpler, more regular one: (from "I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud") I wan- dered lone-ly as a cloud ( - / - / - / - / ) This has a very regular "da-DA da-DA da-DA da- DA" beat. This specific pattern is called iambic meter.
Class Activity: Read the following lines aloud and tap your desk for the stressed syllables:

Hump-ty Dump-ty sat on a wall.

By identifying the beat, we find the rhythm. The poet chooses a rhythm to make the poem flow, to make it memorable, and to match the mood of the poem (e.g., a fast, bouncy rhythm for a happy poem; a slow, heavy rhythm for a sad poem).

GRAMMAR: REPORTED REQUESTS

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Identify the polite words used in direct requests (e.g., "please," "could you").

Convert direct requests into reported requests using the to-infinitive.

Convert negative direct requests into reported requests using not to-infinitive.

This lesson builds on our work with reported speech. While a command is a direct order (e.g., "Sit down!"), a request is a polite way of asking someone to do something.
Identifying Direct Requests: Direct requests are marked by polite words and phrases:

"Please help me."

"Could you pass the salt, please?"

"Will you be quiet?"

"Would you mind closing the door?"

Reporting Verbs for Requests: When reporting a request, we don't usually use "said" or "ordered." We use "softer" verbs that show politeness:
asked (most common)

requested

begged (for a very strong, desperate request)

implored (a formal way of "begged")

The Rule for Reporting Requests: The rule is very similar to reported commands.

The polite word ("please," "could you") is removed.
The reporting verb (e.g., asked) is followed by the person spoken to (the object).

The main verb is changed to its to-infinitive form (to + verb).

Structure: Reporting Verb + Object + to + Base Verb

Examples (Positive Requests):

Direct: She said, "Please help me."

Indirect: She asked me to help her.

Direct: The boy said, "Could you lend me your pen, please?"

Indirect: The boy requested that I lend him my pen.

OR (simpler): The boy asked me to lend him his pen.

Direct: "I beg you, give me some food," the beggar said.

Indirect: The beggar begged the man to give him some food.

Reporting Negative Requests:

Structure: Reporting Verb + Object + not to + Base Verb

Direct: The teacher said, "Please don't make noise."

Indirect: The teacher asked us not to make noise.

Direct: "Please, please don't go," she said to him.

Indirect: She begged him not to go.

What common word is always removed when we report a request?

Change to reported speech: My friend said, "Please don't forget my book."

What is an Argumentative Essay? An argumentative essay (also called a persuasive essay) is a piece of writing where you take a clear stance (position) on a debatable topic. Your goal is to convince or persuade the reader to agree with your point of view, using logic, facts, and examples.
The Topic: "Herbal medicines are more potent (stronger/more effective) than orthodox (hospital/pharmaceutical) medicines."

Taking a Stance (Your Thesis): You cannot "sit on the fence." You must choose one side.

Stance 1 (FOR): "I agree. Herbal medicines are more potent and better than orthodox medicines."
Stance 2 (AGAINST): "I disagree. Orthodox medicines are safer, more reliable, and more potent than herbal medicines."

Brainstorming Points: A good essay considers both sides.

Points FOR Herbal Medicine (Stance 1):
They are "natural" and come from plants, not chemicals.

They often have fewer side effects.

They are cheaper and more easily available.

They have been used for thousands of years (traditional wisdom).

Points AGAINST Herbal Medicine (for Stance 2):

The dosage is not standardized (you can "overdose").

They are not scientifically tested for safety and effectiveness.

They can be toxic or contaminated (e.g., with heavy metals).

They cannot perform complex tasks like surgery or treat all diseases (e.g., cancer, diabetes).

Structure of an Argumentative Essay:

Paragraph 1: Introduction

Hook: Get the reader's attention (e.g., "For centuries, people have debated the best way to cure sickness...").
Background: Briefly introduce the two types of medicine.

Thesis Statement: State your position clearly and boldly. (e.g., "While herbal medicines have their place, it is clear that orthodox medicines are far more potent, reliable, and essential for modern healthcare.")
Paragraphs 2, 3, 4: Body

Each paragraph should present one reason (one of your brainstormed points) that supports your thesis.
Use the "PEE" structure: Point (your reason), Example (a fact or scenario),
Explanation (how this proves your point).
Paragraph 5: Conclusion

Restate your thesis (in new words).

Summarize your main points.

End with a strong, final, persuasive thought.

What are Figures of Speech? Figures of speech (or "literary devices") are words and phrases used in a non-literal way to make writing more vivid, descriptive, and powerful. They paint a "picture" in the reader's mind.
Key Figures of Speech to Find:

A. Simile:

Definition: A comparison between two different things, using the words 'like' or
'as'.

Generic Example: "He is as fast as a cheetah." "Her smile was like the sun."

Text Example: (Teacher: "Let's open our book, [Prose Text Title], to page [X]. The author writes, 'The man's eyes were like fire.' This is a simile.")
B. Metaphor:

Definition: A direct comparison, saying one thing is another. It does not use 'like' or 'as'.
Generic Example: "He is a cheetah on the field." "Her smile is the sun."
Text Example: (Teacher: "On page [Y], the author describes the angry chief. He says, 'The chief was a roaring lion.' This is a metaphor.")
C. Personification:

Definition: Giving human qualities, actions, or feelings to non-human or inanimate objects (e.g., animals, trees, the wind).
Generic Example: "The angry wind screamed outside." "The trees danced in the breeze."
Text Example: (Teacher: "Look at this description of the storm on page [Z]: 'The thirsty ground drank the rain.' The ground isn't 'thirsty' and it can't 'drink'. This is personification.")
D. Hyperbole:

Definition: A great exaggeration used for emphasis or humour. It is not meant to be taken literally.
Generic Example: "I'm so hungry I could eat a horse." "I've told you a million times."
Text Example: (Teacher: "When the character was describing the rich man, he said, 'He has more money than the whole country.' This is a hyperbole.")

SPEECH WORK: CONSONANT SOUNDS /s/ AND /z/

The consonant sounds /s/ and /z/ are both alveolar fricatives, meaning they are produced with the tongue close to the alveolar ridge (the hard bump just behind the upper front teeth) and the airflow is forced through a narrow passage, creating friction. However, they differ in voicing —
/s/ is voiceless while /z/ is voiced.

HOW TO PRODUCE /s/ AND /z/

Both sounds are produced by directing a steady stream of air through a narrow space between the tongue and the alveolar ridge.
For /s/ (voiceless): the vocal cords do not vibrate. The sound is simply a hissing puff of air.
Example words: sip, sun, stone, bus, loose, peace, rice.

For /z/ (voiced): the vocal cords vibrate, producing a buzzing sound.

Example words: zip, zoo, buzz, rise, peas, lose, these.

To test the difference, place two fingers lightly on your throat as you pronounce the sounds. You will feel a vibration with /z/ but none with /s/.
THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN /s/ AND /z/

The only difference between them is voicing — whether the vocal cords vibrate or not.
/s/ is a voiceless alveolar fricative.

/z/ is a voiced alveolar fricative.

MINIMAL PAIRS PRACTICE
Minimal pairs are words that differ by only one sound. Practice helps learners hear and pronounce the difference.

THE PLURAL “S” ENDING RULE
The sound of ‘s’ at the end of words can be pronounced as /s/ or /z/ depending on the sound before it.
Rule 1: If the final sound before the ‘s’ is voiceless (p, t, k, f), then the ‘s’ is pronounced as /s/.
Examples: cats, books, slips, cliffs.

Rule 2: If the final sound before the ‘s’ is voiced (b, d, g, v, l, m, n, or any vowel), then the ‘s’ is pronounced as /z/.
Examples: dogs, boys, beds, fans, leaves.

LITERATURE IN ENGLISH: FIGURES OF SPEECH (REVISION AND APPLICATION)
Introduction:
Writers and poets use figures of speech to make their language more beautiful, interesting, and expressive. Figures of speech are ways of using words to create images, comparisons, or emphasis in order to make writing more powerful and imaginative.
Simile:
A simile is a comparison between two different things using as or like.

Examples:

She is as gentle as a dove.

The boy runs like the wind.

Effect: It makes descriptions more vivid and imaginative.

Metaphor:
A metaphor is a direct comparison between two different things without using as or like.

Examples:

Time is a thief.

The world is a stage.

Effect: It gives strong imagery and emotional force to writing.

Personification:
This figure of speech gives human qualities or actions to non-human things.

Examples:

The sun smiled down on us.

The wind whispered through the trees.

Effect: It makes nature or objects appear alive and friendly.
Hyperbole:
This is an exaggeration used for emphasis or humor.

Examples:

I’ve told you a thousand times to sit down!

The bag weighed a ton.

Effect: It stresses importance and adds humour or emphasis.

Onomatopoeia:
This is the use of words that imitate natural sounds.

Examples:

The bees buzzed in the garden.

The thunder rumbled across the sky.

Effect: It helps readers to “hear” the sounds being described.

Irony:
Saying the opposite of what is meant, often humorously.

Example: The lazy man calls the hardworking one idle.

Effect: It creates surprise or humor.

Alliteration:
The repetition of the same sound at the beginning of words.

Example: Peter picked a peck of pickled pepper.

Effect: It adds rhythm and musical quality to writing.

Application in Literature:
In literature, authors use figures of speech to create images, express emotions, and make readers enjoy the text more. For instance, in poetry, similes and metaphors are used to compare love, nature, or emotions.

COMPOSITION: REPORT WRITING

Meaning of Report Writing
A report is a formal and factual written account of an event, situation, or investigation that has already taken place. It presents information clearly, truthfully, and in a logical sequence so that the reader can understand exactly what happened without confusion or bias.
A report is not based on opinions or emotions but on facts and observations.

Reports are often written by students, prefects, secretaries, class captains, and journalists to inform people in authority—like the principal, the head teacher, or a committee—about an event or an incident.

PURPOSE OF REPORT WRITING
The main purpose of report writing is to inform. It presents accurate information about a particular event or issue so that the reader can understand what happened and make decisions if necessary.
Other purposes include:

To record important events or findings.

To give feedback to superiors or officials.

To provide evidence of an occurrence.

To make recommendations for action.

For example:

A class captain writes a report to the principal about a broken window.

A club secretary writes a report to the patron about the club’s recent meeting.

A journalist writes a report about a public event for the newspaper.

CHARACTERISTICS (FEATURES) OF A GOOD REPORT
A good report should:
Be factual: It must be based only on what actually happened.

Be clear and concise: Avoid unnecessary details or long sentences.

Be objective: The writer should not include personal opinions or emotions.

Be organized: The information must be arranged in a logical, time-based order.

Be formal: The tone should be serious and respectful. Avoid slang, jokes, or contractions.
Be written in the past tense: Since it is about something that has already happened.

LANGUAGE AND STYLE OF A REPORT The language used in a report is formal and simple. Key features include:
Third person point of view (he, she, they, the students).

Past tense verbs (e.g., The event began at 9 a.m.).

No exaggerations or personal emotions.

Correct grammar, punctuation, and paragraphing.

Avoid phrases like:

“It was so amazing and I was very happy.” Use instead:
“The event was well-organized and successful.”

A formal report usually follows this structure:

The title should be specific, written in capital letters, and underlined.

Who organized or attended it Example:

Paragraph 1: Describe how the event began.

Paragraph 2: State the major activities or incidents.

Conclusion:
The conclusion summarizes the overall outcome of the event and may give recommendations or remarks.
Example:

GRAMMAR: THE USES OF HAVE, HAS, AND HAD

The verbs have, has, and had perform two main grammatical functions in English:

As Main Verbs (showing possession).

As Auxiliary Verbs (helping to form perfect tenses).

As Main Verbs (Showing Possession):

When used as main verbs, they show ownership, possession, or relationships.

Present Tense:

have → Used with I, You, We, They (plural subjects).

“I have a new pen.”

“They have two cars.”

has → Used with He, She, It (singular subjects).

“She has a nice dress.”

“The boy has a blue bag.”

Past Tense:

had → Used with all subjects (I, You, He, She, We, They).

“I had a good meal yesterday.”

“They had two goats last year.”

Present Perfect Tense:
have + past participle → I/You/We/They

“I have finished my homework.”

has + past participle → He/She/It

“She has eaten already.”

Past Perfect Tense:

had + past participle → Used for all subjects.

“She had gone home when I called.”

TOPIC: CONSONANT SOUNDS /W/ AND /J/ (AS IN WONDER / UNIVERSITY AND
WIN / YELLOW)

The consonant sounds /w/ and /j/ are called semi-vowels or approximants. They are special because, although they are consonants, they are produced with the mouth fairly open, just like vowel sounds. These sounds do not block the airflow completely; instead, the air passes smoothly through a narrow space, creating a gliding sound.
The /w/ Sound – The “wuh” Sound:

Type: Voiced labio-velar approximant.

How to Produce:

Round your lips tightly as if to say oo in “food.”

Raise the back of your tongue toward the soft palate (velum).

Vibrate your vocal cords as you release the air.

The sound is made as you glide from the /w/ position to the following vowel, e.g.,
w-w-w-wonder.

Common Spellings:

w: win, wonder, wet, away

u: quick (/kwɪk/), language (/læŋgwɪdʒ/)

o: one (/wʌn/)

The /j/ Sound – The “yuh” Sound:

Type: Voiced palatal approximant.

How to Produce:

Raise the middle or front of your tongue very close to the hard palate (as if about to say ee in “feel”).
Vibrate your vocal cords.

Glide quickly away from this position to the next vowel, e.g., y-y-y-yellow.

Common Spellings:

y: yellow, yes, yard, beyond

u: use (/juːz/), university, union

ew: few (/fjuː/)

eu: Europe

Both /w/ and /j/ are voiced (the vocal cords vibrate) and are used at the beginning of many English words.

GRAMMAR: MORE ON QUESTION TAGS

A question tag is a short question added at the end of a statement to confirm information or seek agreement.
Formation Rules:

A positive statement takes a negative tag. Example: He is tall, isn’t he?
A negative statement takes a positive tag. Example: She is not coming, is she?
The Tag Uses the Auxiliary Verb or ‘Be’ Verb in the Statement:

She will come, won’t she?

They have gone, haven’t they?

If there is no auxiliary verb, use do, does, or did:

He likes rice, doesn’t he?

They went home, didn’t they?

Special Cases:

I am late, aren’t I?

Let’s go home, shall we?

Open the door, will you? (command form)

Intonation in Question Tags:

Falling tone: When expecting agreement. (It’s hot today, isn’t it? ↘)

Rising tone: When truly uncertain. (You didn’t see her, did you? ↗)
