Meaning of Posture

Posture is the way the body is positioned or carried when a person is standing, sitting, lying down, walking, or performing an activity. It refers to the alignment of the body parts in relation to one another and to the environment.
The human body has natural curves in the spine – the cervical curve (neck), thoracic curve (upper back), and lumbar curve (lower back). Good posture ensures that these curves are maintained in balance without unnecessary strain.
In simple terms, posture is the habitual way a person holds or positions the body.

Types of Posture

Good Posture
Good posture occurs when the body is well-aligned in a natural position so that the muscles, bones, and joints are under minimal stress.
Features of good posture include:

Head held upright, not drooping forward.

Shoulders level and slightly back.

Chest slightly forward, not collapsed.

Abdomen tucked in, not protruding.

Back straight with natural spinal curves maintained.

Knees straight, not bent or locked backwards.

Body weight evenly distributed on both feet when standing.

Sitting upright with the back supported.

Good posture enhances efficiency of movement, reduces fatigue, and makes the body look smart, attractive, and confident.
Poor Posture
Poor posture occurs when the body is improperly aligned, putting undue stress on muscles, bones, and ligaments. It is usually the result of bad habits developed over time.
Features of poor posture include:

Slouched or rounded shoulders.

Forward thrust of the head.

Protruding stomach or sagging abdomen.

Curved or bent back.

Uneven hips.
Drooping chest.

Bent knees or awkward standing/sitting positions.

Poor posture leads to deformities, backache, fatigue, and loss of confidence.

Importance of Good Posture

Maintaining good posture is very important for both physical and mental wellbeing.

Physical Health Benefits:

Prevents strain on muscles, bones, and joints.

Prevents body pains such as backache and neck pain.

Promotes proper functioning of internal organs by giving them enough space.

Improves circulation of blood and oxygen in the body.

Ensures proper growth and development, especially during adolescence.

Reduces risk of deformities such as hunchback or spinal curvature.

Psychological and Social Benefits:

Boosts self-confidence and self-esteem.

Makes an individual appear smart, attractive, and disciplined.

Improves body language and communication in social interactions.

Gives a positive impression to others.

Causes of Poor Posture

Several factors can cause poor posture. These include:

Weak muscles and lack of exercise.

Carrying heavy loads, such as school bags on one shoulder.
Sitting in slouched positions for long hours (e.g., in class or when using the computer).

Using chairs, desks, or beds that do not support the body well.

Wearing high-heeled shoes frequently.

Accidents, injuries, or illnesses that affect the bones or muscles.

Overweight or obesity which causes strain on the spine.

Lack of awareness about correct posture habits.

Effects of Poor Posture

Poor posture has several negative consequences:

Fatigue and tiredness because muscles work harder to keep balance.

Backache, neck pain, and body discomfort.

Deformities such as round shoulders, flat chest, or hunchback.

Difficulty in breathing due to compressed lungs.

Reduced efficiency in movement and performance of tasks.

Poor appearance and loss of confidence.

Ways of Maintaining Good Posture

Good posture can be developed and maintained by following correct habits:

Always stand upright with shoulders back and body weight equally distributed.

Sit correctly with the back straight, feet flat on the ground, and buttocks at the back of the chair.
Avoid slouching or bending forward unnecessarily.

Lift objects correctly by bending the knees, not the waist.
Sleep on a good mattress that supports the body.

Engage in physical exercises (such as stretching, swimming, walking, and posture training exercises) to strengthen muscles.
Avoid carrying heavy loads on one shoulder; balance loads evenly.

Wear appropriate footwear; avoid high heels for long durations.

Develop awareness of body alignment in daily activities.

WEEK 2: POSTURE II MEANING OF POSTURE
Posture is the way the body is positioned, arranged, or carried when performing different activities such as sitting, standing, walking, or lifting objects.
It involves the alignment of the bones (skeleton), muscles, and joints in a way that ensures the body is balanced, stable, and efficient in movement.
Good posture does not only refer to “looking straight” but also to the correct positioning that reduces strain on the muscles, ligaments, and joints, thereby preventing tiredness and injury.
Posture can be of two main types:

Good (Correct) Posture – when the body is aligned properly and muscles are not strained.
Bad (Incorrect) Posture – when the body is poorly aligned, leading to unnecessary pressure on muscles and bones.

IMPORTANCE OF GOOD POSTURE

Good posture is very important in daily living and overall health. Its importance includes:

Health Benefits:

Prevents pains in the back, neck, and shoulders.

Reduces the risk of joint problems and deformities.

Promotes healthy growth and development of the bones and muscles in children and adolescents.
Helps internal organs (lungs, heart, stomach, etc.) to function well because they are not squeezed.
Psychological/Appearance Benefits:

Makes a person look attractive, smart, and confident.

Boosts self-esteem and personal presentation in public.

Functional Benefits:

Helps the body to use energy more efficiently, reducing fatigue.

Improves balance and stability, preventing falls and accidents.

Enhances performance in sports, physical activities, and even academic tasks (because good sitting posture helps concentration).

APPROPRIATE POSTURE IN SITTING

When sitting, the following guidelines should be followed:

Sit upright, keeping the back straight and supported by the chair.

Keep the shoulders relaxed and not raised or bent forward.

Both feet should rest flat on the ground or on a small footrest.
Knees should be bent at a right angle (90°) with thighs parallel to the floor.

The head should face forward and not be bent too low for long periods (e.g. while reading or writing).
Avoid slouching, twisting, or sitting on only one side of the buttocks.

Do not sit for excessively long periods without stretching.

Effects of Bad Sitting Posture:

Hunched back (hunchback or kyphosis).

Neck and back pain.

Fatigue and poor concentration.

APPROPRIATE POSTURE IN STANDING

When standing:

Stand erect and upright, keeping the body balanced.

Distribute weight equally on both feet; do not rest weight on one leg for too long.

Feet should be slightly apart, not too close together.

The head should be up and facing forward, with chin slightly tucked in.

Shoulders should be held back, relaxed, and level.

Avoid bending forward or slouching.

Effects of Bad Standing Posture:

Back and leg pains.

Curved spine (lordosis or scoliosis).

Fatigue and imbalance.
APPROPRIATE POSTURE IN WALKING

Good walking posture means walking naturally, gracefully, and with balance:

Walk tall with the head upright, looking forward (not always down).

Keep the back straight and shoulders relaxed.

Swing the arms naturally by the sides in rhythm with steps.

Place each foot forward in a straight line, heel first, followed by the toes.

Maintain a natural rhythm; avoid rushing unnecessarily or dragging the feet.

Effects of Bad Walking Posture:

Awkward appearance.

Uneven wear of shoes.

Faster fatigue.

APPROPRIATE POSTURE IN LIFTING OBJECTS

When lifting objects, especially heavy ones, the following steps should be observed:

Preparation: Stand close to the object with feet apart for balance.

Bending: Bend the knees and squat down while keeping the back straight.

Grip: Hold the object firmly with both hands.

Lifting: Straighten the knees to lift, while keeping the object close to the body.

Carrying: Maintain balance by holding the object in front, not far from the chest.

Avoid: Twisting or bending the waist when lifting, as it strains the back muscles.

Effects of Bad Lifting Posture:

Back injuries and waist pains.

Muscle strain.
Long-term spinal deformities.

CONSEQUENCES OF BAD POSTURE

If correct posture is not maintained, the following negative consequences may occur:

Health problems: Back pain, stiff neck, joint pain, headaches.

Bone deformities: Hunchback (kyphosis), swayback (lordosis), or sideways curve of the spine (scoliosis).
Poor appearance: A person looks dull, lazy, or careless.

Reduced efficiency: Bad posture makes the body easily tired and reduces productivity.

Breathing and digestion problems: Poor posture can press on internal organs.

WEEK 3: POSTURAL DEFECTS
Types of Postural Defects

Posture refers to the way the body is positioned and carried during standing, sitting, walking, or lying down. A good posture is the correct alignment of the body that places minimal stress on bones, joints, and muscles. A postural defect occurs when the body’s alignment deviates from the natural position, resulting in abnormal curves or positions of the spine, shoulders, or limbs. Postural defects can cause discomfort, poor appearance, difficulty in movement, and in severe cases, health problems.

TYPES OF POSTURAL DEFECTS

Kyphosis

An excessive outward curve of the upper spine (thoracic region), leading to a hunchback appearance.
Causes: poor sitting habits, carrying heavy loads, bone diseases like tuberculosis of the spine.
Effects: back pain, reduced lung capacity, poor body image.

Lordosis

An excessive inward curve of the lower spine (lumbar region).

Commonly referred to as “swayback.”

Causes: obesity, weak abdominal muscles, prolonged poor posture, pregnancy.

Effects: backache, fatigue, difficulty standing for long periods.

Scoliosis

A sideways curvature of the spine in an “S” or “C” shape.

Causes: congenital deformities, uneven leg length, carrying heavy loads on one side of the body.
Effects: uneven shoulders or hips, pain, breathing difficulties in severe cases.

Knock Knees (Genu Valgum)

A condition where the knees touch or angle inward while the ankles remain apart.

Causes: weak leg muscles, rickets, obesity, genetic factors.

Effects: awkward walking pattern, knee pain, joint strain.

Bow Legs (Genu Varum)

A condition where the legs curve outward at the knees, leaving a gap between them when standing with feet together.
Causes: rickets, bone malnutrition, improper walking posture in childhood.

Effects: imbalance, pain in legs, difficulty in running or walking long distances.

Flat Foot

A condition where the arch of the foot is absent, causing the entire sole to touch the ground.
Causes: weak foot muscles, prolonged standing, congenital defects.

Effects: pain in the feet and legs, poor balance, inability to stand for long.

Round Shoulders

A condition where the shoulders are bent forward abnormally.

Causes: poor sitting posture, long hours of bending over books, use of improper chairs and desks.
Effects: chest compression, shallow breathing, and poor body shape.

MEANING OF PHYSICAL AND HEALTH EDUCATION

Physical and Health Education (PHE) is a subject that deals with the development of the human body through physical activities, exercises, games, and sports, while also teaching individuals
how to live a healthy lifestyle. It focuses on physical fitness, mental alertness, social interaction, and the prevention of diseases through knowledge of healthy habits.

MEANING OF ISSUES AND CHALLENGES IN PHE

Issues are important matters, topics, or concerns that affect the practice, teaching, and learning of Physical and Health Education in schools and society.
Challenges are difficulties, obstacles, or problems that hinder the effective implementation and growth of Physical and Health Education.

ISSUES IN PHYSICAL AND HEALTH EDUCATION

Low Recognition of the Subject

Many people still see PHE as less important than other academic subjects like Mathematics or English.
Shortage of Facilities and Equipment

Lack of standard sports fields, gymnasiums, balls, nets, and other instructional materials in schools.
Inadequate Funding

Schools and government often allocate little or no funds to promote PHE and sports.
Shortage of Qualified Teachers

Many schools have few or no trained PHE specialists, leading to poor instruction.

Cultural and Religious Beliefs

Some cultures or religions discourage participation in certain games or sports, especially by girls.
Low Student Participation
Many students prefer sedentary lifestyles (e.g., using phones and computers) to engaging in sports and physical activities.

CHALLENGES OF PHYSICAL AND HEALTH EDUCATION

Poor Government Policies

Lack of proper policies and commitment to support sports and health education development.
Health Risks and Injuries

Participants sometimes face accidents, injuries, or illnesses due to poor training or lack of protective equipment.
Inadequate Motivation for Athletes and Teachers

Lack of incentives, rewards, and recognition for both students and teachers reduces interest.
Negative Attitude of Parents and Students

Many parents encourage their children to focus only on “serious” subjects, neglecting PHE.
Poor Infrastructure and Maintenance

Even when facilities are provided, they are poorly maintained, reducing their effectiveness.

POSSIBLE SOLUTIONS

Government should allocate more funds to sports and health education.

Schools should provide adequate facilities, equipment, and qualified PHE teachers.

Parents and communities should support students’ participation in physical activities.
Awareness should be created about the importance of fitness, sports, and health.

Maintenance culture should be developed for sports infrastructure.

Scholarships and incentives should be given to talented students to encourage participation.

WEEK 6: HUMAN TRAFFICKING

INTRODUCTION

Human trafficking is the illegal act of recruiting, transporting, transferring, harbouring, or receiving people through force, deceit, or coercion for the purpose of exploitation. Victims are often promised good jobs, education, or a better life but are deceived and forced into slavery, prostitution, forced labour, or other inhumane conditions. It is a global crime and a violation of human rights, affecting both young and old, especially women and children.

Here's a powerful, symbolic split image addressing human trafficking:

MEANING OF HUMAN TRAFFICKING

Human trafficking means the unlawful movement of people within or across borders for the purpose of exploiting them. It involves acts such as:
Abduction or kidnapping.

False promises of job opportunities or education.

Sale of people into forced marriage, child labour, or prostitution.

It is different from human smuggling because trafficking involves exploitation, not just transportation.

HEALTH IMPLICATIONS OF HUMAN TRAFFICKING ON VICTIMS

Human trafficking has severe effects on the health and well-being of victims. These effects can be:
Physical Health Problems

Victims are often subjected to physical abuse, beatings, rape, and torture.

They suffer injuries, broken bones, and sometimes permanent disabilities.

They may be denied medical care, leading to untreated diseases and infections.

Sexual and Reproductive Health Issues

Victims forced into prostitution are exposed to sexually transmitted infections (STIs) including HIV/AIDS.
Early or forced pregnancy may occur, leading to complications.

Lack of access to proper hygiene and reproductive health care.

Psychological and Emotional Problems

Victims often experience depression, anxiety, trauma, and fear.

Many develop post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).
Loss of self-esteem, hopelessness, and suicidal thoughts are common.

Social and Educational Problems

Victims are usually denied access to education, leading to illiteracy.

They are isolated from family, friends, and community.

Stigma and discrimination make reintegration into society difficult.

Economic Consequences

Victims are exploited for free or cheap labour and earn little or no money.

They remain in poverty and dependency.

WEEK 9: SPORTS LAWS

INTRODUCTION

Sports are organized physical activities or games governed by rules and regulations. These rules, often referred to as sports laws, are designed to ensure fair play, safety, orderliness, and discipline during sporting activities. Without sports laws, games would be chaotic, unfair, and unsafe.
Every sport has its own specific rules, but there are also general laws that apply across all sporting activities. These laws guide the conduct of players, officials, coaches, and even spectators.

MEANING OF SPORTS LAWS

Sports laws are the rules, regulations, and guidelines that govern the playing, officiating, and administration of sports. They are set up by sports governing bodies (such as FIFA for football, FIBA for basketball, IOC for the Olympics, etc.) to ensure fairness, equal opportunity, and safety for participants.

IMPORTANCE OF SPORTS LAWS

To ensure fair play among players.

To protect athletes from injuries and misconduct.

To maintain order and discipline during competitions.

To provide a standard framework for officiating games.

To settle disputes and conflicts in sports.

To enhance sportsmanship and respect among competitors.

GENERAL SPORTS LAWS

While every sport has specific rules, the following are general sports laws applicable in most sports:
Fair Play: Players must compete fairly without cheating or using unfair advantage.

Respect for Officials: Decisions of referees and umpires must be respected.

Use of Standard Equipment: Players must use approved sports equipment (e.g., correct ball size, proper footwear).
Time Regulations: Games must be played within specified time limits.

Scoring System: Each sport has a recognized scoring method that must be followed.
Safety Measures: Players must wear protective gear where necessary (e.g., shin guards in football, helmets in cricket).
Prohibition of Violence: Physical assault, rough play, and misconduct are penalized.

Drug-Free Competition: The use of performance-enhancing drugs (doping) is forbidden.

EXAMPLES OF SPORTS LAWS IN SELECTED GAMES

FOOTBALL (SOCCER)

A match lasts 90 minutes (two halves of 45 minutes each).

Each team consists of 11 players including the goalkeeper.

A goal is scored when the whole ball crosses the goal line.

Offside rule applies to prevent unfair advantage.

Fouls (e.g., tripping, handball) are penalized with free kicks, penalties, or cards.

BASKETBALL

Each team has 5 players on court.

The game is played in four quarters of 10–12 minutes each.

A basket scored inside the arc is 2 points, outside the arc is 3 points, and free throw is 1 point.
Players must dribble while moving with the ball.

Fouls are penalized with free throws or turnovers.

ATHLETICS (TRACK EVENTS)

Athletes must stay within their assigned lanes during races.

Starting before the starter’s gun is a false start (disqualification after repeated offences).

Use of illegal assistance (e.g., pushing another runner) is not allowed.
The first athlete whose torso crosses the finish line wins.

SUMMARY

Sports laws are rules and regulations guiding the conduct of games and players.

They are important for fair play, safety, orderliness, and standardization.

Examples include football rules, basketball rules, and athletics rules.

Violating sports laws leads to penalties, disqualification, or suspension.

WEEK 10: RECREATION AND LEISURE
Meaning and Types of Recreational Activities

Recreation and leisure are important aspects of healthy living. They contribute to the physical, mental, emotional, and social well-being of individuals. Recreation provides opportunities for relaxation, enjoyment, and self-expression, while leisure is the free time that allows people to take part in such activities.

MEANING OF RECREATION AND LEISURE

Recreation is any activity that people engage in during their free time for enjoyment, relaxation, and personal satisfaction. It helps to relieve stress and renew energy after
work or school. Examples include swimming, dancing, playing football, gardening, storytelling, and hiking.
Leisure refers to the free time available after completing one’s duties, responsibilities, or work. It is the time when an individual is free to choose activities of interest, which may include resting, reading, or taking part in recreational activities.
Difference: Leisure is the time available, while recreation is the activity carried out during that time.

TYPES OF RECREATIONAL ACTIVITIES

Recreational activities can be grouped into the following categories:

Indoor Recreational Activities

These are activities carried out inside the home or enclosed spaces.

Examples: board games (chess, scrabble, ludo), table tennis, music, painting, cooking, storytelling, reading books, video games.
Outdoor Recreational Activities

These are activities carried out outside the home or in open spaces.

Examples: football, basketball, volleyball, swimming, horse riding, cycling, gardening, camping, jogging.
Active Recreational Activities

Involve vigorous body movement and physical exercise.

Examples: athletics, dancing, hiking, martial arts, aerobics.

Passive Recreational Activities

Do not require much physical movement. They are mainly for relaxation and entertainment.
Examples: watching movies, listening to music, sightseeing, fishing, picnicking.
Social Recreational Activities

Activities done with others to promote friendship and social interaction.

Examples: cultural festivals, dancing, community games, parties, drama.

Creative Recreational Activities

Activities that encourage self-expression and talent development.

Examples: painting, drawing, sculpture, singing, drama, writing poems or stories.

IMPORTANCE OF RECREATION AND LEISURE

Helps to relieve stress and mental fatigue.

Improves physical fitness and overall health.

Provides social interaction and builds relationships.

Promotes creativity and talent development.

Offers opportunities for rest and relaxation.

Encourages team spirit, cooperation, and discipline.

Prevents idleness and reduces the chances of engaging in negative behaviours.

WEEK 6: TABLE TENNIS

INTRODUCTION

Table tennis, also known as ping pong, is a fast-paced indoor game played between two or four players using small rackets (bats), a lightweight ball, and a rectangular table divided by a net. It is one of the most popular racket sports in the world and is governed internationally by the International Table Tennis Federation (ITTF). In Nigeria, it is regulated by the Nigeria Table Tennis Federation (NTTF).

HISTORY AND DEVELOPMENT OF TABLE TENNIS

The origin of table tennis can be traced back to England in the late 19th century. It began as an after-dinner indoor game among the upper class. Books were first used as makeshift nets, and rounded corks or champagne cork stoppers served as balls. Soon after, proper equipment was developed.
In 1901, the name “ping pong” was trademarked by a British manufacturer, but later, “table tennis” was officially adopted for the sport.
In 1926, the International Table Tennis Federation (ITTF) was formed, and the first official World Championships were held the same year in London.
The sport spread quickly across Europe and Asia, gaining massive popularity.

By the 1950s, Asian countries like Japan and China began to dominate the sport. China, in particular, became the strongest nation in table tennis.
Table tennis became an Olympic sport in 1988 at the Seoul Games.

In Nigeria, table tennis became popular during the mid-20th century and has since produced international champions such as Atanda Musa, Bose Kaffo, Segun Toriola, and Aruna Quadri.

EQUIPMENT AND FACILITIES FOR TABLE TENNIS

The Table

Rectangular in shape, measuring 2.74m long, 1.525m wide, and 0.76m high.

The surface must be uniformly dark in colour, with a white line marking the edges.

A centerline divides the table for doubles play.

The Net and Posts

The net is 15.25cm high and stretches across the middle of the table.

It divides the table into two equal halves.
The Ball

Spherical, lightweight, made of celluloid or plastic.

Diameter: 40mm; Weight: 2.7g.

White or orange in colour for clear visibility.

The Racket (Bat or Paddle)

Made of wood, usually covered with rubber on one or both sides.

Used to strike the ball during play.

Players may choose smooth or pimpled rubber, depending on their playing style.

The Playing Area/Facilities

Minimum playing space recommended is 14m long, 7m wide, and 5m high.

Adequate lighting and smooth flooring are necessary for safe play.

Indoor facilities are preferred to avoid wind interference.

IMPORTANCE OF LEARNING TABLE TENNIS

It develops hand–eye coordination.

It improves reflexes and concentration.

It enhances physical fitness, agility, and stamina.

It promotes social interaction, teamwork, and sportsmanship.

It provides opportunities for recreation, competition, and career development.

WEEK 8: TABLE TENNIS — OFFICIALS AND THEIR DUTIES

In organised table tennis competitions the officials are responsible for fairness, safety, timing and smooth running of matches. Their work ensures that players concentrate on performance while rules, discipline and records are properly maintained. In school and inter-school competitions the core match officials are the REFEREE, UMPIRE and ASSISTANT UMPIRE; other support officials commonly used are the SCORER, TIMEKEEPER and TABLE/FLOOR CONTROLLER. Below is a detailed, practical explanation of each official’s duties before, during and after a match, plus procedures for dealing with disputes and the qualities of a good official.

REFEREE

Overall authority for the competition or event.

Pre-competition duties: inspect venue and playing conditions; ensure tables, nets, balls and scoreboards meet required standards; approve the draw and match schedule; appoint umpires and other match officials; publish competition regulations and ensure officials and teams understand them.
During competition: supervise the conduct of matches, enforce the competition regulations, handle protests and appeals that cannot be settled by the umpire, decide on matters beyond the umpire’s jurisdiction (e.g., eligibility, match abandonment, misconduct), and coordinate with medical or security personnel when necessary.
Post-match duties: review and sign match reports when needed, compile incident reports for disciplinary action, and ensure results are forwarded correctly to tournament records.
Administrative role: organise replacements for absent officials, arrange for anti-doping procedures where applicable, and act as the final arbiter of interpretation of the rules at the event.
UMPIRE (MATCH OFFICIAL)

Match-level official who manages play at the table. The umpire sits (or stands) at the match table and is immediately responsible for all decisions relating to play.
Pre-match duties: check that the table, net, ball and playing area are correct; verify players’ equipment (racket rubbers, shoes, clothing, removal of jewellery where required); conduct the coin-toss (or lot) and explain choice of serve/ends; supervise warm-up time; ensure scoreboards and score sheets are ready.
Duties during play: announce and record the score after each point; make prompt, visible decisions on points, lets and service faults; watch for illegal actions (hidden service, double-hits, obstructing the ball); enforce service order and doubles rotation; give warnings and apply penalties for time-wasting, misconduct or illegal equipment; control
the pace of play (time between points, intervals); ensure safety of players in case of injuries and suspend play if the playing conditions become unsafe.
Interaction with assistant umpire and scorer: consult the assistant umpire for calls the umpire cannot clearly see (edge hits, ball touching net and table rim), consult the scorer to confirm recorded scores and timeouts, and report any incidents to the referee when required.
Post-match duties: complete and sign the match sheet if applicable, report rule infractions or misconduct, and ensure the result is properly submitted.
ASSISTANT UMPIRE

The assistant umpire’s primary role is to support the umpire by handling observations that are difficult to make from the umpire’s position.
Typical duties: watch table edges and corners for ball contact (edge or top-of-net falls), observe service to assist with service faults and toss visibility, help monitor players’ footwork and whether a player touches the playing surface (if required), operate the manual scoreboard when used, and act as a second pair of eyes for close calls.
The assistant also helps maintain order around the table (preventing coaches or spectators from encroaching) and may take over duties if the umpire is indisposed.
SCORER (MATCH RECORDER)

Keeps the official match record: records points, games and match results on the official score sheet or in the competition software; logs timeouts, warnings and penalties; notes the start and finish times of matches where required.
Pre-match: prepare score sheets and ensure any relevant player identification or eligibility information is recorded.
During match: update the scoreboard, confirm with the umpire when a score is disputed, record signatures at the end of the match if required.
Post-match: submit completed match documents to the tournament office and keep accurate records for appeals or statistics.
TIMEKEEPER

Responsible for enforcing time regulations: warm-up periods, intervals between games, time allowed for timeouts, and any time constraints applied by the competition (e.g., to keep matches on schedule).
Ensures compliance with competition rules about deliberate delays or excessive breaks and reports persistent time-wasting to the umpire or referee.
In tournaments that apply an expedite or shot-clock system, the timekeeper runs and enforces that system.
TABLE / FLOOR CONTROLLER (TOURNAMENT SUPPORT)

Ensures matches start at their allocated tables and that playing areas remain free from obstructions.
Coordinates ball retrievers, ensures spare tables and equipment are ready, and directs players to the correct table on time.
Manages crowd control near playing areas and checks that walkways and emergency exits remain clear.
OTHER SUPPORT OFFICIALS (SCHOOL/TOURNAMENT CONTEXT)

Ball retrievers and helpers: speed up play by collecting balls.

Tournament director/organiser: overall event planning, finance, liaising with schools and parents.
Medical attendant/first aider: attend injuries immediately and advise referee on medical decisions.
HANDLING DISPUTES — PRACTICAL PROCEDURE
On-table decisions by the umpire are normally final during play. If a player disagrees, they should briefly raise the issue with the umpire immediately and not delay play.
If disagreement persists, the umpire’s decision may be appealed to the referee; the referee may stop play, inspect the situation, hear both sides and issue a final ruling.
Officials must record time, point of dispute and relevant facts on the match sheet; any protest after the match is dealt with by the referee using the documented report.
For serious misconduct (violence, match-fixing, gross cheating) the referee may suspend play and refer the matter to the competition disciplinary committee.
OFFICIATING ETIQUETTE AND QUALITIES OF A GOOD OFFICIAL

Impartiality: treat all players fairly and without bias.

Knowledge: sound understanding of the rules and tournament regulations.

Alertness and concentration: watch every rally carefully and be ready to make instant decisions.
Clear communication: announce scores clearly and give concise explanations of decisions when required.
Calmness under pressure: manage disputes and manage player emotions without escalating situations.
Professional appearance and conduct: dress appropriately, be punctual and prepared.

Physical fitness: ability to maintain focus throughout long matches and move slightly when necessary to get a better view.
PRACTICAL TEACHING IDEAS FOR THE CLASSROOM/PHYSICAL HALL

Role-play: assign students the roles of umpire, assistant umpire, scorer and timekeeper and conduct a short match while they practice pre-match checks, coin toss, score announcements and writing match sheets.
Demonstration: show correct procedures for checking equipment (racket rubbers, ball condition, net tension) and how to complete a basic match record form.
Discussion: present a few hypothetical disputes and have students explain the steps they would take to resolve them, then compare responses with the referee’s responsibilities.

WEEK 10: TENNIS — SCORING SYSTEM, OFFICIALS AND TERMINOLOGIES

Tennis is one of the most popular racket sports in the world. It is played either as singles (one player on each side) or doubles (two players on each side). For students of Physical and Health Education, it is important to understand how points are scored, the officials who conduct the game, and the basic terminologies used during play.

SCORING SYSTEM IN TENNIS

The scoring system in tennis is unique and different from most other sports. It is organised into
points, games, sets, and matches.

Points

A standard tennis point system runs as:

0 point = “Love”

1 point = “15”

2 points = “30”

3 points = “40”

4 points = “Game” (if a player leads by 2 points).

Example: If Player A wins the first two points, the score is “30–Love.”

Deuce and Advantage

If both players reach 40–40, the score is called Deuce.

From deuce, a player must win two consecutive points to win the game:

First point won after deuce = “Advantage In” (if server wins) or “Advantage Out” (if receiver wins).
If the opponent wins the next point, the score goes back to Deuce.

Play continues until one player wins by two clear points.
Games

A player wins a game by winning enough points to reach “Game” with at least a 2-point lead.
Sets

A set is won by the first player to win 6 games with at least a 2-game lead.

Example: 6–4 is a winning set, but 6–5 means the set continues until a 2-game lead is achieved (or tie-break is used).
Match

A match is usually played as best of 3 sets (first to 2 sets) or best of 5 sets (first to 3 sets in major tournaments).

OFFICIALS IN TENNIS

Tennis requires several officials to ensure fair play and proper conduct of matches.

Referee

Overall authority of the tournament.

Handles disputes, ensures the rules are followed, and has the final decision on all matters.
Chair Umpire

Main on-court official seated on a high chair at the centre of the court.

Calls the score aloud after every point.

Rules on points, lets, foot faults, and player conduct.

Works with line umpires and has authority to overrule them if necessary.

Line Umpires

Positioned around the court.
Watch the lines to judge whether a ball is “IN” or “OUT.”

Signal decisions clearly using hand gestures and voice.

Net Umpire

Observes whether the ball touches the net during service.

This role may also be handled by the chair umpire in some competitions.

Scorer

Records scores, games, and sets officially.

Ensures accurate documentation of the match.

Ball Boys and Ball Girls (Support Officials)

Collect and supply balls to players quickly to maintain smooth play.

Provide towels and assist in keeping the court neat and ready for play.

BASIC TENNIS TERMINOLOGIES

Ace – A legal serve not touched by the receiver, winning the point outright.

Double Fault – When a server fails both first and second service attempts, giving a point to the opponent.
Fault – A failed serve that does not land correctly in the service box.

Let – A serve that touches the net but still lands in the correct service box; the serve is replayed.
Rally – Exchange of strokes between players during a point.

Volley – Hitting the ball before it bounces on the court.

Smash – A powerful overhead shot, usually ending the rally.
Break Point – A situation where the receiver can win the game by winning the next point against the server.
Match Point – A point which, if won by a player, secures the match.

Tie-break – A special game used to decide a set when the score reaches 6–6.

Meaning of Accidents

An accident is an unexpected and unintended event that causes injury, damage, or even death. Accidents usually happen suddenly and without warning. They can occur anywhere – at home, in school, on the road, at the workplace, or during sports and recreational activities.
Key points:

Accidents are unplanned and usually harmful.

They can affect people’s health, cause property loss, or even lead to death.

Most accidents can be prevented if safety rules are observed.

Examples of accidents: falling, burns, cuts, drowning, electric shock, road crashes, sports injuries, poisoning, and suffocation.

Causes of Accidents

Accidents usually happen due to human carelessness or environmental hazards. Common causes include:
Carelessness or negligence.

Ignoring safety instructions.

Faulty equipment or poor maintenance.

Playing rough or engaging in dangerous practices.

Over-speeding or careless driving.

Slippery floors or obstacles left on walkways.

Safety Measures at Home

Homes are prone to accidents such as burns, falls, cuts, poisoning, or electrocution. To prevent them:
Keep sharp objects like knives, blades, and scissors safely away from children.

Do not leave water, oil, or soap on the floor to avoid slips and falls.

Store poisonous substances (detergents, pesticides, medicines) properly.

Avoid faulty electrical appliances and exposed wires.

Do not leave children unsupervised near fire, stoves, or gas cylinders.

Ensure good lighting, especially on staircases and walkways.
Safety Measures at School

Schools must be safe environments for learning and play. Measures include:

WEEK 3: FIRST AID
First Aid Treatment for Common Injuries
First Aid is the immediate and temporary care given to a sick or injured person before professional medical help is available. The main aims of first aid are to preserve life, prevent the condition from worsening, and promote recovery.
In Physical and Health Education, learning first aid is important because accidents and injuries can occur during sports, recreation, or daily life. A prompt and correct response can save lives and prevent complications.

FIRST AID TREATMENT FOR COMMON INJURIES

Cuts and Wounds
Meaning: Break in the skin caused by sharp objects (e.g., knife, glass, blade).

First Aid Treatment:

Wash hands before touching the wound.

Clean the wound gently with clean water to remove dirt.

Apply antiseptic (e.g., iodine, methylated spirit).

Cover with a clean dressing or plaster to prevent infection.

For deep cuts, apply pressure to stop bleeding and seek medical help.

Bruises

Meaning: Injury under the skin that causes discoloration due to ruptured blood vessels.
First Aid Treatment:

Apply a cold compress or ice pack to reduce swelling.

Allow the injured part to rest.

Elevate the area if possible.

If swelling persists, seek medical care.

Burns and Scalds

Meaning: Injury caused by dry heat (burns) or hot liquid/steam (scalds).

First Aid Treatment:

Immediately cool the area with clean running water for at least 10–15 minutes.
Do not burst blisters or apply greasy substances (like oil or butter).
Cover the burn with a clean, non-fluffy cloth or sterile dressing.

Seek medical attention if the burn is severe.

Nosebleeds

Meaning: Bleeding from the nose caused by injury, heat, or high blood pressure.

First Aid Treatment:

Sit the person down with head slightly forward.

Pinch the soft part of the nose for 10–15 minutes.

Apply a cold compress to the nose or forehead.

Do not tilt the head backward (to prevent blood from entering the throat).

If bleeding continues after 20 minutes, seek medical help.

Sprains and Strains

Meaning: A sprain is the overstretching or tearing of ligaments around a joint, while a strain is overstretching of muscles or tendons.
First Aid Treatment (RICE method):

R – Rest the injured part.

I – Apply Ice pack to reduce swelling.

C – Compress with an elastic bandage.

E – Elevate the injured limb above heart level.

Fractures (Broken Bones)

Meaning: A break or crack in a bone.
First Aid Treatment:

Do not move the injured part unnecessarily.

Support and immobilize the bone with a splint or sling.

Control bleeding if it is an open fracture.

Call for medical help immediately.

Fainting

Meaning: Temporary loss of consciousness due to lack of blood flow to the brain.

First Aid Treatment:

Lay the person flat on the ground.

Loosen tight clothing.

Raise the legs slightly to improve blood flow to the brain.

Allow fresh air and reassure the person when they regain consciousness.

INTRODUCTION

Health is the state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being of an individual, not merely the absence of disease or infirmity. A School Health Programme is a set of health services, education, and activities designed to promote and protect the health of students, teachers, and other school staff. It ensures that the school environment is safe, conducive for learning, and supportive of the total development of the child.

WEEK 9: ENVIRONMENTAL POLLUTION
Types of environmental pollution; preventive measures

INTRODUCTION

The environment is the totality of our surroundings – air, water, land, plants, animals, and other natural resources. A clean environment is essential for good health, while a polluted environment leads to diseases, discomfort, and even death.
Pollution is the introduction of harmful substances or conditions into the environment in such a way that it becomes unsafe, unpleasant, or unsuitable for living organisms. Pollution is largely caused by human activities such as industrialization, improper waste disposal, deforestation, and use of harmful chemicals.

TYPES OF ENVIRONMENTAL POLLUTION

Air Pollution

This occurs when harmful gases, smoke, and particles are released into the atmosphere.
Sources: industrial emissions, burning of fossil fuels, car exhausts, bush burning, smoking.
Effects: respiratory diseases (asthma, bronchitis), global warming, acid rain, depletion of the ozone layer.
Water Pollution

Contamination of rivers, lakes, oceans, and groundwater with harmful substances.

Sources: oil spills, sewage disposal, industrial waste discharge, agricultural chemicals (pesticides, fertilizers).
Effects: waterborne diseases (cholera, typhoid), destruction of aquatic life, scarcity of clean drinking water.
Land/Soil Pollution

Occurs when solid waste and chemicals degrade the quality of the soil.

Sources: improper waste disposal, littering, open dumping of refuse, industrial chemicals, excessive use of fertilizers.
Effects: poor agricultural yield, land degradation, breeding of pests, offensive odour.
Noise Pollution

Excessive and disturbing sound that affects human and animal life.

Sources: loud music, traffic, industrial machines, generators, airplanes.

Effects: hearing impairment, stress, lack of concentration, sleep disturbance.
Thermal Pollution

Occurs when industries release hot water or heat into the environment.

Sources: power plants, factories, oil refineries.

Effects: rise in water temperature, death of aquatic organisms, imbalance in ecosystems.
Radioactive Pollution

Caused by the release of radioactive substances from nuclear plants, medical use of radioactive materials, or accidents.
Effects: cancers, genetic disorders, long-term contamination of the environment.

PREVENTIVE MEASURES OF ENVIRONMENTAL POLLUTION

For Air Pollution

Planting more trees (afforestation).

Use of cleaner energy sources (solar, wind, hydropower).

Avoiding bush burning and open incineration of waste.

Proper maintenance of vehicles to reduce exhaust fumes.

For Water Pollution

Proper sewage and waste management systems.

Avoiding dumping of refuse or chemicals into rivers and streams.

Treatment of industrial waste before discharge.

Protection of water bodies from oil spills.

For Land/Soil Pollution

Adoption of proper waste disposal methods (recycling, composting, incineration).
Avoiding excessive use of chemicals on farmlands.

Public enlightenment on hygiene and sanitation.

Government enforcement of environmental laws.

For Noise Pollution

Use of soundproof equipment in industries and generators.

Regulation of noise levels in public areas.

Avoiding unnecessary honking of car horns.

Proper town planning to reduce residential exposure to industrial noise.

For Thermal and Radioactive Pollution

Careful monitoring and regulation of industries.

Use of proper cooling methods before discharging water into rivers.

Safe handling, storage, and disposal of radioactive materials.

Enforcement of strict safety measures in nuclear plants and hospitals.

SUMMARY

Pollution is the introduction of harmful substances into the environment.

Types of pollution include air, water, land, noise, thermal, and radioactive pollution.

Preventive measures include afforestation, proper waste management, recycling, industrial regulation, and public health education.

WEEK 10: NON-COMMUNICABLE DISEASES — MEANING AND NATURE OF COMMON NON-COMMUNICABLE DISEASES

MEANING OF NON-COMMUNICABLE DISEASES (NCDs)

Non-communicable diseases are diseases that are not caused by infectious agents (germs, viruses, fungi, or parasites) and cannot be transmitted from one person to another. They are often long-term (chronic), progress slowly, and may result from genetic, environmental, nutritional, or lifestyle factors.
Unlike communicable diseases such as malaria or tuberculosis, non-communicable diseases are usually linked to risk factors like poor diet, lack of exercise, harmful use of substances, or inherited traits.

NATURE OF COMMON NON-COMMUNICABLE DISEASES

Cardiovascular Diseases (CVDs)

Diseases that affect the heart and blood vessels.

Examples: hypertension (high blood pressure), stroke, and heart attack.

Causes include poor diet (too much salt, sugar, or fat), lack of exercise, smoking, stress, and heredity.
Diabetes Mellitus

A condition where the body cannot properly use or produce enough insulin, leading to high blood sugar levels.
Two main types:

Type 1: caused by lack of insulin production (often hereditary).

Type 2: caused by poor lifestyle such as obesity and inactivity.

Symptoms include frequent urination, excessive thirst, weight loss, and fatigue.

Cancers

A group of diseases caused by the uncontrolled growth of abnormal cells in the body.
Can affect different parts of the body (e.g., breast cancer, cervical cancer, lung cancer, prostate cancer).
Causes include genetic factors, smoking, exposure to harmful chemicals, unhealthy diets, and prolonged infections.
Chronic Respiratory Diseases

Affect the lungs and airways.

Examples: asthma, chronic bronchitis, emphysema.

Triggers include smoking, air pollution, dust, allergens, and weak immunity.
Mental and Neurological Disorders

Disorders that affect thinking, mood, or behavior.

Examples: depression, anxiety, epilepsy, Alzheimer’s disease.

Causes include brain injury, stress, drug abuse, and heredity.

Sickle Cell Anaemia

A genetic blood disorder where red blood cells are abnormally shaped (sickle- shaped) and break down easily.
Leads to anaemia, pain episodes, and organ damage.

Common among Africans due to genetic inheritance.

GENERAL CHARACTERISTICS OF NON-COMMUNICABLE DISEASES

They are not infectious and cannot spread from person to person.

They are often chronic and long-lasting.

They may require long-term treatment and management instead of a cure.

They may arise from lifestyle choices (poor diet, smoking, lack of exercise).

Some are hereditary (passed from parents to children).

They may cause disability, reduce quality of life, or lead to premature death if not properly managed.
