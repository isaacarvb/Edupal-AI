FIRST TERM - JSS 2 NIGERIAN HISTORY LESSON NOTES WEEK 1: INDEPENDENCE OF NIGERIA
Content: Events of October 1, 1960; Prime Minister and Governor-General

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Define "Independence" in the context of Nigeria.
Describe the key events that took place on October 1, 1960.
Identify the first Prime Minister of independent Nigeria.
Identify the first Governor-General of independent Nigeria.

Lesson Content:

What is Independence? Independence is the freedom of a country from the political control of another country. In Nigeria's case, it was the day Nigeria became a free, self-governing nation, no longer under the colonial rule of Great Britain.

The Road to 1960 (Brief Context) For decades, Nigerian nationalists (leaders like Herbert Macaulay, Dr. Nnamdi Azikiwe, Chief Obafemi Awolowo, and Sir Ahmadu Bello) had struggled and negotiated for freedom. Through constitutional conferences held in Nigeria and London, they eventually convinced Great Britain to grant Nigeria its independence.

Events of October 1, 1960 The main independence ceremony was held at the Race Course (now Tafawa Balewa Square) in Lagos, the capital city at the time.

The Midnight Ceremony (12:01 a.m.): The most symbolic event happened at midnight.
The British flag (the "Union Jack") was lowered for the last time.
The new Nigerian flag (Green, White, and Green) was hoisted for the first time.
This moment symbolized the end of British rule and the birth of a new nation.
The Official Ceremony (Daytime):
Dignitaries: Princess Alexandra of Kent represented the Queen of England. Nigeria's new leaders and thousands of citizens were present.
Handing Over Power: Princess Alexandra presented the "Freedom Charter" (the instruments of independence) to Nigeria's new Prime Minister.
Speeches: The new Prime Minister, Sir Abubakar Tafawa Balewa, gave a famous and powerful speech. He said, "...our flag is hoisted... a signal that a new country has come into being. It is a sign that we are now responsible for our own affairs..."
Celebrations: All across Nigeria, there were parades, cultural dances, feasting, and great celebration.

The First Government (1960-1963) When Nigeria became independent, it was a "Dominion," which meant it was still a member of the Commonwealth, and the Queen of England was still the official (but ceremonial) Head of State.
Prime Minister (Head of Government): Alhaji Sir Abubakar Tafawa Balewa. He was the person with the most political power, responsible for running the government.
Governor-General (Head of State): Dr. Nnamdi Azikiwe. He was the representative
of the Queen of England in Nigeria. He had mostly ceremonial duties.

When did Nigeria gain independence?
Where was the main independence ceremony held?
Who was the first Prime Minister of Nigeria?
Who was the first Nigerian Governor-General?

WEEK 2: FIRST REPUBLIC (1963–1966)

Topic: First Republic (1963–1966)

Content: Leaders; Political parties; Achievements and problems

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Define a "Republic" and state when Nigeria became one.
Identify the major leaders and political parties of the First Republic.
List two achievements of the First Republic.
List and explain two major problems of the First Republic.

Lesson Content:

What is a Republic? On October 1, 1963, Nigeria cut its final tie with Britain by becoming a
Republic.

A Republic is a country with no king or queen. The country is led by a President, who is a citizen of that country.
This meant Dr. Nnamdi Azikiwe's title changed from "Governor-General" (representing the Queen) to "President" (representing Nigeria).

Leaders of the First Republic

President (Ceremonial Head of State): Dr. Nnamdi Azikiwe
Prime Minister (Head of Government): Alhaji Sir Abubakar Tafawa Balewa
Regional Premiers: Nigeria was a federation of four regions, and each had a powerful leader called a Premier.
Northern Region: Sir Ahmadu Bello
Western Region: Chief Samuel Akintola
Eastern Region: Dr. Michael Okpara
Mid-Western Region: Chief Dennis Osadebay (This region was newly created in 1963).

Major Political Parties The political parties were the biggest problem of the First Republic, as they were almost all based on the major ethnic groups and regions.

Northern People's Congress (NPC): Dominated the North. (Leader: Ahmadu Bello)
National Council of Nigerian Citizens (NCNC): Dominated the East. (Leader: Nnamdi Azikiwe/Michael Okpara)
Action Group (AG): Dominated the West. (Leader: Chief Obafemi Awolowo)

Achievements of the First Republic

Political: Became a Republic (1963); created the Mid-Western Region; joined international bodies like the OAU (now AU) and the UN.
Economic: There was an agricultural boom. The North was famous for its groundnut pyramids, and the West for its cocoa. The East was known for palm oil.
Infrastructure: The Kainji Dam was built to generate electricity. New universities were established (e.g., Ahmadu Bello University, University of Ife).

Problems of the First Republic This period was filled with chaos, which eventually led to the end of the Republic.

Regionalism/Tribalism: The parties and leaders were more loyal to their regions and tribes than to Nigeria as a whole.
Census Crisis (1962/63): The census (counting the population) was very controversial. Because population numbers decided how many seats each region got in parliament and how much revenue they received, the figures were inflated (falsified). This destroyed trust.
Action Group (AG) Crisis (1962): A serious political fight broke out in the Western Region between Chief Obafemi Awolowo and his deputy, Chief Samuel Akintola. This led to widespread violence and rioting, known as "Operation Wetie," where political opponents and their property were set on fire.
Federal Election Crisis (1964): The federal election was full of massive rigging, violence, and boycotts. It completely undermined democracy.
Corruption: Many politicians were seen by the public as very corrupt, using their positions to get rich.

Who was the first President of Nigeria?
Name the three main political parties of the First Republic.
List one major achievement of this period.
What was "Operation Wetie"?

Assignment:
Draw a simple map of Nigeria showing the four regions of the First Republic.
In your opinion, what was the biggest problem of the First Republic? Explain your answer in one paragraph.
WEEK 3: MILITARY RULE IN NIGERIA I

Topic: Military Rule in Nigeria I

Content: Causes of the January 1966 coup

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Define a "coup d'état."
State the date of Nigeria's first military coup.
Identify the leader of the January 1966 coup.
List and explain three major causes of the coup.

Lesson Content:

What is a "Coup d'État"? A coup d'état (a French term, usually shortened to "coup") is the sudden, illegal, and often violent overthrow of a government by a small group, usually the military. This is not a democratic election; it is a seizure of power.

The January 15, 1966 Coup On this date, a group of young, idealistic army officers (mostly Majors) staged Nigeria's first-ever military coup.

Leader: The coup was led by Major Chukwuma Kaduna Nzeogwu in the North (Kaduna).
Nickname: It was nicknamed the "Majors' Coup."
Goal: The coup plotters claimed they wanted to end corruption, stop the political violence, and create a strong, united Nigeria.

Causes of the January 1966 Coup The coup did not happen in a vacuum. It was a direct result of the major problems of the First Republic (see Week 2).

Widespread Political Corruption: The politicians of the First Republic were seen as very corrupt. They stole public money, lived in luxury, and ignored the poor.
Violence and Breakdown of Law and Order: The "Operation Wetie" crisis in the Western Region had turned into a full-scale disaster. Political opponents were being killed daily, and the government seemed unable to stop it.
Rigged Elections: The 1964 Federal Election and the 1965 Western Region Election were massively rigged. This destroyed any belief in democracy and made many feel that violence was the only way to change the government.
Tribalism and Regionalism: The country was divided along ethnic lines. The coup plotters felt the politicians were tearing the country apart with their tribal feuding.
Ineffective Government: The Prime Minister, Tafawa Balewa, was seen as weak and unable to control the chaos, especially the violence in the West.

The Events and Outcome
The coup was bloody. The Majors assassinated top leaders, including:
The Prime Minister (Alhaji Sir Abubakar Tafawa Balewa).
The Premier of the North (Sir Ahmadu Bello).
The Premier of the West (Chief Samuel Akintola).
Top military officers from the North and West.
The coup failed to take full control. The remaining government ministers were in disarray and, in the confusion, they handed over power to the most senior military officer, Major- General Johnson Aguiyi-Ironsi, who became Nigeria's first Military Head of State.

Evaluation:

What is a coup d'état?
When was Nigeria's first military coup?
Who was the main leader of the coup?
List three major causes of the coup.
Who became the Head of State after the coup?

Assignment:

Find out the names of two other leaders (political or military) who were killed in the January 1966 coup.
Write a short paragraph explaining why the young military majors felt they needed to overthrow the government.
WEEK 4: MILITARY RULE IN NIGERIA II

Topic: Military Rule in Nigeria II

Content: Counter-coup of July 1966; Gowon’s regime

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Define a "counter-coup."
Explain the main causes of the July 1966 counter-coup.
Describe the events of the counter-coup.
Identify Yakubu Gowon as the new Head of State.

Lesson Content:

The Ironsi Regime (January – July 1966) After Major-General Aguiyi-Ironsi took power, his government made a number of mistakes that angered parts of the country.

Perception of the Coup: The January 1966 coup was seen by many in the North as an "Igbo coup" because most of the coup plotters were Igbo, and most of the leaders killed were from the North and West.
Failure to Try Plotters: Ironsi did not publicly court-martial (try and punish) Major Nzeogwu and the other coup plotters, which angered Northern soldiers.
Decree No. 34 (The Unification Decree): This was the biggest mistake. In May 1966, Ironsi issued a decree that abolished the federal system (the regions) and created a single, central "unitary" government. The North, which feared Southern (especially Igbo) domination of the civil service, saw this as a final confirmation that the coup was an Igbo plot to take over the country.

The July 29, 1966 Counter-Coup

Definition: A counter-coup is a "revenge" coup that overthrows the leaders of the first coup.
Leadership: This coup was planned and carried out by officers and soldiers from the
Northern Region, led by figures like Lieutenant-Colonel Murtala Muhammed.
Causes:
Revenge: It was a direct revenge for the killing of Northern political and military leaders in the January coup.
Fear of Domination: The Unification Decree created intense fear in the North.
Failure to Punish: Ironsi's failure to punish the January plotters was seen as favoritism.

Events of the Counter-Coup

This coup was also very bloody. It began in the army barracks in Abeokuta.
The Head of State, Major-General Aguiyi-Ironsi, and the Military Governor of the Western Region, Colonel Adekunle Fajuyi, were arrested in Ibadan and killed.
Across the country, hundreds of Igbo military officers and soldiers were targeted and killed by their Northern colleagues.

Gowon’s Regime

After the coup, there was chaos. For three days, Nigeria had no leader.
The coup plotters finally agreed to appoint the most senior Northern officer who was acceptable to all. This was Lieutenant-Colonel Yakubu Gowon.
Gowon was a Christian from the Middle Belt (not Hausa-Fulani), which made him a good compromise candidate.
He became the new Head of State. His first act was to repeal Decree No. 34 (the Unification Decree) and restore the federal system of regions. This counter-coup, and the massacres that followed, set the stage for the Nigerian Civil War.

Evaluation:

What is a counter-coup?
What was the "Unification Decree" (Decree No. 34)?
List two main causes of the July 1966 counter-coup.
Who was killed alongside Major-General Aguiyi-Ironsi?
Who became the Head of State of Nigeria after the July 1966 coup?

Assignment:

Why was the "Unification Decree" so unpopular, especially in the North?
Yakubu Gowon was not the most senior officer in the army when he was made Head of State. Find out why he was chosen.
WEEK 6: NIGERIAN CIVIL WAR I

Topic: Nigerian Civil War I

Content: Causes of the war (1967–1970)

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

State the dates of the Nigerian Civil War.
Identify the two sides of the war (Nigeria and Biafra).
List and explain the remote (long-term) causes of the war.
List and explain the immediate (short-term) causes of the war.

Lesson Content:

What was the Nigerian Civil War? The Nigerian Civil War (also called the Biafran War) was a bloody conflict fought from July 6, 1967, to January 15, 1970.

The Two Sides:
The Federal Republic of Nigeria: Led by the Federal Military Government under General Yakubu Gowon.
The Republic of Biafra: The secessionist (breakaway) state, which was the former Eastern Region of Nigeria. It was led by Colonel Chukwuemeka Odumegwu Ojukwu.

Remote Causes (Long-term Causes) These are the underlying problems that had existed for years.

Ethnic and Regional Differences: The deep-seated suspicion and rivalry between the major ethnic groups (Hausa-Fulani, Igbo, Yoruba).
Problems of the First Republic: All the crises from Week 2 (corrupt politicians, rigged elections, census crisis, "Operation Wetie").
The January 1966 Coup: Seen as an "Igbo coup."
The July 1966 Counter-Coup: Seen as a "Northern revenge coup."

Immediate Causes (The Final Straws) These are the events that happened just before the war, making it unavoidable.

Pogroms (Massacres) in the North (Sept/Oct 1966): After the July coup, there was widespread, organized violence against Igbo civilians living in the North. Tens of thousands were killed, and this led to a massive refugee crisis.
Refugee Crisis: Over one million Igbos fled from the North back to their homeland in the East. This created a huge humanitarian, social, and economic burden on the Eastern government.
The Aburi Accord (January 1967): The leaders (Gowon and Ojukwu) met in Aburi, Ghana, for a last-ditch peace effort. They agreed on a confederation (a very loose union
of states). However, upon returning to Nigeria, both leaders interpreted the agreement differently, and it failed.
Creation of 12 States (May 27, 1967): Gowon's government suddenly announced it was splitting Nigeria's 4 regions into 12 new states. This was a smart political move to break the power of the regions, but it had one major effect: it split Ojukwu's Eastern Region into three states, cutting off the Igbo heartland from the oil-rich coastal areas (Rivers State).
Secession (The Spark): Three days later, on May 30, 1967, Colonel Ojukwu (seeing the 12-state plan as a final provocation) declared the Eastern Region an independent nation: The Republic of Biafra.
Gowon's Response: Gowon and the Federal Government refused to accept this "secession" and declared it an act of rebellion. On July 6, 1967, the Federal Government launched a "police action" to retake Biafra. This was the start of the war.

Evaluation:

What were the dates of the Civil War?
Who was the leader of Nigeria during the war?
Who was the leader of Biafra?
What were the "pogroms"?
What was the "Aburi Accord"?

Assignment:

What was the "immediate spark" that caused the war to be declared? (Hint: It happened on May 30, 1967).
Why was the creation of 12 states a major cause of the war?
WEEK 8: NIGERIAN CIVIL WAR II

Topic: Nigerian Civil War II

Content: Major events; End of war; "No victor, no vanquished" policy

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Describe at least two major events of the war.
Explain the role of the economic blockade.
State how the war ended and who surrendered.
Define and explain the "No victor, no vanquished" policy.

Lesson Content:

Major Events During the War (1967-1970)

Federal Strategy: The Nigerian side had more soldiers and weapons (supplied by Britain and the USSR). Their main strategy was to encircle and blockade Biafra, cutting it off from the rest of the world.
Biafran Strategy: The Biafran side was outgunned but had high morale and was very inventive (e.g., building "Ogbunigwe" local bombs). They were supplied by France, Portugal, and some other nations.
Biafran Invasion of the Mid-West: In a surprise move in 1967, Biafran soldiers crossed the Niger Bridge, captured the entire Mid-Western Region (including Benin City), and got as close as Ore (in the West). This shocked the Federal side, but they eventually pushed them back.
The Economic Blockade and Starvation: This was the most tragic part. The Federal blockade by land and sea cut off all supplies, including food. This led to mass starvation (especially of children) in Biafra. The disease of severe malnutrition, Kwashiorkor, became famous. This was the first time many people around the world saw such images on TV.
Fall of Major Cities: The Federal troops slowly captured key Biafran cities: Port Harcourt and Calabar were captured, cutting Biafra off from the sea. Enugu (the capital) was also captured.
Uli Airstrip: This was Biafra's only link to the outside world. At night, planes (often flown by foreign charities) would brave anti-aircraft fire to land with food, medicine, and weapons.

The End of the War (January 1970)

By late 1969, Biafra was collapsing from starvation and overwhelming Federal attacks.
On January 11, 1970, the Biafran leader, Colonel Ojukwu, fled the country to Côte d'Ivoire (Ivory Coast) to "seek peace."
He handed over leadership to his deputy, General Philip Effiong.
Realizing the situation was hopeless, General Effiong announced Biafra's surrender.
On January 15, 1970, General Effiong formally surrendered to General Gowon in Lagos. The war was over.

"No Victor, No Vanquished" Policy This was the official policy of General Yakubu Gowon's government after the war.

Meaning: It literally means "Nobody won (no victor), and nobody lost (no vanquished)."
Purpose: It was a policy of reconciliation. Gowon wanted to heal the country quickly. He declared that all Biafrans would be welcomed back as full Nigerian citizens. There were no mass trials for treason or public executions. It was a gesture of peace to try and rebuild Nigeria as one country.

Evaluation:

What was the main economic strategy used by the Nigerian government?
What was the "Uli Airstrip"?
Who took over from Ojukwu and officially surrendered?
On what date did the war officially end?
What did General Gowon's "No victor, no vanquished" policy mean?

Assignment:

The Civil War caused mass starvation. What was the name of the disease associated with this?
Do you think the "No victor, no vanquished" policy was a good idea? Give one reason for your answer.
WEEK 9: POST-CIVIL WAR NIGERIA

Topic: Post-Civil War Nigeria

Content: Reconstruction, Reconciliation, Rehabilitation (The 3 'R's)

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

State the "3R" policy.
Define "Reconstruction" and give an example.
Define "Reconciliation" and give an example.
Define "Rehabilitation" and give an example.

Lesson Content:

The "3R" Policy To heal the wounds of the war, General Gowon's government announced a program based on three 'R's: Reconstruction, Reconciliation, and Rehabilitation. This program was funded by the new oil wealth.

Reconstruction

Definition: This was the physical part of the policy. It meant rebuilding the damaged infrastructure (buildings, roads, bridges) that was destroyed during the 30-month war.
Examples:
Rebuilding the Niger Bridge at Onitsha, which had been blown up.
Repairing roads, hospitals, schools, and markets in the former Eastern Region.
Creating a National Development Plan to fund these projects.

Reconciliation

Definition: This was the political and social part. It meant "making friends again" and bringing the two sides back together as one people.
Examples:
The "No victor, no vanquished" speech was the first and most important step.
Biafran soldiers were re-absorbed (taken back) into the Nigerian Army.
Biafran civil servants were allowed to return to their old jobs in the Federal government.
Gowon made a point of visiting the war-torn areas to show unity.

Rehabilitation

Definition: This was the human and economic part. It meant helping the individuals and families who had lost everything to restart their lives.
Examples:
Providing food, medicine, and shelter for refugees and displaced persons.
Trying to find jobs for demobilized (ex-) soldiers.
Problems with Rehabilitation: This part of the policy was not very successful.
The "20 Pound" Policy: This was a major problem. The government gave every Igbo person with money in a Biafran bank account a flat rate of only 20 Nigerian pounds, regardless of how much money they had before the war. This made it very hard for people to restart their businesses.
Abandoned Property: The issue of property (houses, land) left behind by Igbos in other parts of Nigeria (especially Port Harcourt) was a major problem, as many people found their homes had been taken over.

Evaluation:

What are the "3 R's" of Gowon's post-war policy?
Which "R" involved rebuilding bridges and roads?
Which "R" involved re-absorbing Biafran soldiers into the Nigerian army?
What was the "20 Pound" policy? Was it part of Reconstruction or Rehabilitation?
What was the goal of "Reconciliation"?

Assignment:

Write a short paragraph explaining the difference between Reconstruction and Rehabilitation.
In your opinion, which of the "3 R's" was the most important for Nigeria's future? Why?
WEEK 10: OIL BOOM OF THE 1970S

Topic: Oil Boom of the 1970s

Content: Discovery of crude oil; Effects on economy

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

State where and when crude oil was first discovered in Nigeria.
Explain what the "Oil Boom" of the 1970s was.
List two positive effects of the oil boom.
List two negative effects of the oil boom (the "Oil Curse").

Lesson Content:

Discovery of Crude Oil

Crude oil was first discovered in commercial quantities in Oloibiri (in present-day Bayelsa State) in 1956.
The company that found it was Shell-BP.
Nigeria began exporting oil in 1958.

The "Oil Boom" of the 1970s

For the first few years, oil was not the main source of income.
The "Oil Boom" happened in the 1970s, right after the Civil War.
Cause: In 1973, there was a war in the Middle East (the Yom Kippur War). The Arab countries (OPEC) cut their oil supply to Western countries.
Effect: This caused the world price of crude oil to skyrocket (jump up by 400%).
Result: Nigeria, which was not part of the Arab embargo, suddenly became extremely wealthy, almost overnight. This period of massive, easy wealth was the "Oil Boom."
General Gowon's government famously said Nigeria's problem was "not money, but how to spend it."

Effects on the Economy

Positive Effects:

Massive Revenue: The government had billions of dollars to spend for the first time.
Infrastructure Projects: This money paid for the "3R" policy. It was also used to build:
The new federal capital, Abuja.
New highways, airports (like Murtala Muhammed Intl.), and seaports (like Tin Can Island).
National broadcast (NTA) and the National Stadium.
Udoji Award (1974): The government massively increased the salaries of all public workers (civil servants), making many of them very happy.
Festac '77: Nigeria hosted a massive, expensive international arts festival in Lagos.

Negative Effects (The "Oil Curse") The oil boom had terrible long-term effects that Nigeria still suffers from today.

Neglect of Agriculture: This is the most important negative effect. Nigeria completely abandoned its farms. The groundnut pyramids, cocoa, and palm oil, which were our main exports, disappeared. The country went from exporting food to importing food.
Massive Corruption: The new, easy money led to massive, large-scale corruption. Government contracts became a way to "get rich quick."
Inflation: The "Udoji Award" (salary increase) meant there was too much money in the economy, so the prices of food and goods went up (inflation).
Over-Dependence: The entire Nigerian economy became 100% dependent on one single product: crude oil.
Environmental Pollution: Oil spills in the Niger Delta began to destroy farms and fishing, leading to poverty and anger in that region.

SECOND TERM – JSS 2 NIGERIAN HISTORY LESSON NOTES
WEEK 1: SECOND REPUBLIC (1979–1983)

Topic: Second Republic (1979–1983) Content: Leaders; Achievements; Problems

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Explain how the Second Republic came into being.
Identify the key leaders and political parties of the Second Republic.
List two achievements of the Second Republic.
List and explain three major problems that led to the end of the Second Republic.

Lesson Content:
The Transition to the Second Republic After the assassination of General Murtala Muhammed in 1976, his deputy, General Olusegun Obasanjo, became Head of State. Obasanjo continued the plan to return Nigeria to civilian rule.

He set up a Constitution Drafting Committee.
He established the Federal Electoral Commission (FEDECO).
He lifted the ban on political parties.
In 1979, a series of elections were held, which led to the start of the Second Republic on October 1, 1979. This republic was based on the American-style Presidential System, not the British-style Parliamentary System of the First Republic.

Leaders and Political Parties

President: Alhaji Shehu Shagari (NPN)
Vice President: Dr. Alex Ekwueme (NPN)
Major Political Parties: The five main parties were:
NPN (National Party of Nigeria): The ruling party, with its main support base in the North but with a wide national spread.
UPN (Unity Party of Nigeria): Led by Chief Obafemi Awolowo. Its support was mainly in the West.
NPP (Nigerian People's Party): Led by Dr. Nnamdi Azikiwe. Its support was mainly in the East.
GNPP (Great Nigeria People's Party): Led by Waziri Ibrahim.
PRP (People's Redemption Party): Led by Aminu Kano.

Achievements of the Second Republic

Peaceful Transition: A successful and peaceful handover from military rule to a democratic civilian government.
New Constitution: The 1979 Constitution established the Presidential system, a strong Federal Capital Territory (Abuja), and a 19-state structure.
Economic Development: The Shagari government focused on large-scale projects, including:
Green Revolution: A program to boost agriculture and food production.
Housing: A massive federal program to build low-cost housing estates in every state.
New Capital: Continued the construction of Abuja as the new capital.

Problems of the Second Republic Despite its achievements, the Second Republic was seen as a massive failure by most Nigerians.

Widespread Corruption: This was the single biggest problem. The "Oil Boom" of the 1970s was still ongoing, and many politicians became billionaires overnight through massive theft of public funds and contract inflation.
Economic Collapse: The world oil price crashed in the early 1980s (the "Oil Glut"). Because Nigeria had abandoned agriculture (see First Term, Week 10), the economy collapsed. The government could not pay salaries, and basic goods became scarce.
1983 General Election: The 1983 election was one of the most rigged elections in Nigeria's history. The ruling NPN used violence and fraud to win a "landslide victory."
Political Violence: The rigging of the 1983 election led to widespread political violence, especially in the Western Region (Ondo State), similar to the "Operation Wetie" of the First Republic.

This combination of corruption, economic collapse, and rigged elections made the government very unpopular and led directly to its overthrow.

Assignment:

WEEK 2: MILITARY RULE IN THE 1980S

Topic: Military Rule in the 1980s

Content: Buhari/Idiagbon regime; Babangida regime

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

State the date and reason for the 1983 coup.
Identify the leaders of the Buhari/Idiagbon regime and their key policy.
Explain why the Babangida regime came to power.
Identify and explain the key economic policy of the Babangida regime (SAP).

Lesson Content:

The Buhari/Idiagbon Coup (December 31, 1983)

Event: On the last day of 1983, the military overthrew the government of Shehu Shagari in a coup.
Reason: The coup plotters announced they seized power to save Nigeria from the massive corruption and economic mismanagement of the Second Republic.
Leaders: The new regime was led by:
Major-General Muhammadu Buhari (Head of State).
Major-General Tunde Idiagbon (Chief of Staff, Supreme Headquarters). Idiagbon was seen as the "strongman" or "second-in-command" of the regime.

The Buhari/Idiagbon Regime (1984–1985) This regime was known for its strictness and discipline.

Key Policy: "War Against Indiscipline" (WAI): This was their most famous program. It was a social campaign to force discipline on Nigerians.
WAI forced people to queue orderly at bus stops ("forming a line").
It included monthly environmental sanitation days.
It promoted patriotism (respecting the flag and anthem).
Style: The regime was very tough. It jailed hundreds of politicians from the Second Republic for corruption.
Problems: The regime was also very harsh on human rights.
Decree No. 4: This decree jailed any journalist who published articles that "embarrassed" the government, even if the articles were true.
Public Executions: The regime executed people for crimes like drug trafficking.
Economic Hardship: The economy did not improve, and prices of goods remained high.

The Babangida Coup (August 1985)
On August 27, 1985, General Buhari was overthrown in a "palace coup" (a coup from within the government) by his own Chief of Army Staff, General Ibrahim Badamasi Babangida (IBB).
Reasons: Babangida claimed the Buhari regime was too rigid, had failed to fix the economy, and was committing human rights abuses.

The Babangida Regime (1985–1993)

Title: Babangida took the title of "President" (not "Head of State"). He was known as a very clever and political general, nicknamed "Maradona."
Key Economic Policy: Structural Adjustment Programme (SAP): This is the most important policy of his regime.
To fix the collapsed economy, Nigeria took a loan from the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank.
SAP was a set of new rules: it devalued the Naira, cut government spending, and privatized government companies.
Effect: SAP caused massive hardship, inflation, and unemployment. It is remembered as a very difficult time for most Nigerians.
Other Actions: He moved the Federal Capital from Lagos to Abuja in 1991. He also began a very long and confusing "Transition to Democracy" program, which we will discuss next week.

Actions:
He released many political prisoners.
He set up a new Independent National Electoral Commission (INEC).
He announced a short, 9-month timetable for a return to democracy, which he followed perfectly.

Lesson content:

The 1999 Transition to Civilian Rule

After many years of military rule in Nigeria, the nation once again began preparing for democracy in 1998. When General Sani Abacha died suddenly in June 1998, General Abdulsalami Abubakar became the new Head of State. Unlike many previous military rulers, General Abubakar promised to hand over power to a civilian government within one year.
To achieve this goal, he took several important steps:

Restoration of political activities: He lifted the ban on political parties and allowed Nigerians to form new ones.
Establishment of INEC: His government created the Independent National Electoral Commission (INEC) to plan and conduct free and fair elections.
Creation of the 1999 Constitution: A new constitution was drafted and adopted. It provided the laws and framework for democratic governance in Nigeria. This 1999 Constitution is still in use today.
General Abdulsalami Abubakar’s short but significant leadership paved the way for the return to democracy after years of military dictatorship.

The 1999 Elections

The 1999 elections were held at different levels — local government, state, and national — as part of the transition program.
Three major political parties emerged and were allowed to contest the elections nationwide:

People’s Democratic Party (PDP)

All People’s Party (APP)

Alliance for Democracy (AD) — This party had its strongest support in the Southwestern region of Nigeria.
During the Presidential Election:

The PDP presented Chief Olusegun Obasanjo, a former military Head of State (1976– 1979), as its candidate.
The APP and AD formed an alliance and jointly presented Chief Olu Falae as their candidate.
After the elections, Olusegun Obasanjo of the PDP was declared the winner and became Nigeria’s new democratically elected President.
This election was significant because it restored the people’s power to choose their leaders through voting — the main principle of democracy.

The Handover: Birth of the Fourth Republic

On May 29, 1999, General Abdulsalami Abubakar peacefully handed over power to the newly elected President, Chief Olusegun Obasanjo.
This historic day marked:

The end of 16 years of continuous military rule (from 1983–1999).

The beginning of the Fourth Republic, which is the current democratic era Nigeria is in.

The Fourth Republic refers to Nigeria’s fourth attempt at democratic governance since independence — following previous republics that were interrupted by military coups.
Originally, May 29 was celebrated as Democracy Day to honor this peaceful handover. However, in 2018, President Muhammadu Buhari changed Democracy Day to June 12 in remembrance of M.K.O. Abiola, the presumed winner of the annulled 1993 election, which many consider Nigeria’s freest and fairest election.
This change was made to recognize the struggle and sacrifices made by Nigerians for democracy.

What is Democracy? Democracy is a system of government "by the people, for the people." It means citizens choose their leaders through free and fair elections, and those leaders are accountable to the people.

Definition: This is the abuse of public office for private gain. It includes bribery, stealing public funds (looting), and favoritism.

WEEK 1: WOMEN IN NIGERIAN HISTORY

Topic: Women in Nigerian History

Lesson Content:

Introduction: The Role of Women in Nigerian History Historically, women in Nigeria have held significant positions of power and influence, long before the colonial period. They were queens, warriors, traders, and political activists. In the colonial and post-colonial periods, women were central figures in the fight for independence and social justice. We will look at three outstanding examples.

Queen Amina of Zaria (c. 1533–1610)

Who she was: A Hausa warrior queen of the Zazzau Kingdom (now Zaria) in the 16th century.
Historical Significance:
Military Leader: She was a brilliant and powerful military commander. She led her army in many successful campaigns, expanding the Zazzau kingdom to its largest size ever.
Territorial Expansion: She is credited with conquering many surrounding cities, including Nupe and Kano.
Trade: Her conquests allowed Zaria to control important North African trade routes, bringing great wealth to the kingdom.
Architecture: She is credited with building the famous "Amina's Walls," strong earthen fortifications that surrounded her cities.
Legacy: Queen Amina is remembered as a legendary symbol of female power, military skill, and political leadership in pre-colonial Nigeria.

Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti (1900–1978)

Who she was: A highly influential anti-colonial activist, women's rights leader, and educator from Abeokuta. She was also the mother of the famous musician Fela Kuti.
Achievements:
Women's Rights: She founded the Abeokuta Women's Union (AWU), a powerful organization that fought for the rights of women.
Anti-Colonial Activism: She led massive protests against the colonial government and the traditional ruler, the Alake of Egbaland.
Tax Protests: Her most famous achievement was leading the AWU in a successful protest against unfair taxes imposed on women. This protest was so powerful that it led to the temporary abdication (removal) of the Alake in 1949.
Politics: She was one of the key figures in the nationalist movement, negotiating for independence alongside Azikiwe and Awolowo. She was also the first woman in Nigeria to drive a car.
Legacy: She is known as the "Mother of the Nation" and a pioneer of Nigerian feminism and the independence movement.

Margaret Ekpo (1914–2006)

Who she was: A pioneering female politician, women's rights activist, and nationalist from Calabar, Cross River State.
Achievements:
Political Mobilization: She was a master political organizer. She created the Aba Market Women's Association and used it to mobilize women to fight for their economic and political rights.
Nationalist: She was a leading member of the NCNC party (with Dr. Nnamdi Azikiwe) and fearlessly campaigned for independence.
Political Leader: After independence, she was elected to the Eastern Region House of Assembly, becoming one of the very few women in government.
Social Activism: She famously fought against the colonial practice of having different, better facilities for white people (e.g., in hospitals or at the train station).
Legacy: She is remembered as a fearless activist who brought grassroots women into the mainstream of Nigerian politics. The Calabar International Airport is named after her.

Student Unionism

Definition: Student Unionism is the organized activity of students, usually through an elected "Student Union Government" (SUG) in a university or college.
Purpose: The primary purpose is to represent the interests of students to the school authorities (e.g., protesting high fees or poor hostels).

WEEK 3: WORKERS IN NIGERIAN HISTORY

Topic: Workers in Nigerian History Content: Trade unions; Nigerian Labour Congress

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Define a "trade union."
Explain the primary purpose of a trade union.
Identify the "Nigerian Labour Congress (NLC)" as the central labour union.
Describe the role of trade unions in the fight for independence (e.g., the 1945 strike).

Lesson Content:

What is a Trade Union?
Definition: A trade union (or labour union) is an organization made up of workers (e.g., teachers, doctors, factory workers, drivers) who have joined together.
Purpose: The main purpose is to protect the interests of its members and fight for better working conditions. They use their collective power to demand:
Better pay (higher minimum wage).
Safer working conditions.
Shorter working hours.
Pensions and other benefits.
Main Weapon: Their main weapon is the "strike"—a collective refusal to work until their demands are met.

The Nigerian Labour Congress (NLC)

To be stronger, all the different trade unions in Nigeria (like the teachers' union, doctors' union, etc.) came together to form one giant central organization.
This organization is the Nigerian Labour Congress (NLC), formed in 1978.
When the NLC declares a "general strike," the entire country (ports, banks, schools, hospitals) can shut down. This gives it immense political power.

Role of Workers in the Fight for Independence

Long before the NLC was formed, trade unions were a key part of the nationalist movement.
The 1945 General Strike: This was a major event. Led by Michael Imoudu (Nigeria's most famous labour leader), railway, post, and other government workers went on strike for 45 days.
Reason: They demanded a "Cost of Living Allowance" (COLA) because prices had gone up during World War II, but their salaries had not.
Effect: The strike crippled the colonial economy. It was the first time Nigerian nationalists (like Nnamdi Azikiwe) and workers joined forces. It proved to the British that they could no longer control Nigeria, and it greatly accelerated the journey to independence.
Role of Workers in Modern Nigeria

Protesting Military Rule: The NLC and the oil workers' unions (NUPENG) were very active in the June 12 protests, organizing strikes to try and force the military to step down.
Fighting for Minimum Wage: The NLC is in a constant struggle with the government to increase the minimum wage to match the rising cost of living (inflation).
Fuel Subsidy Protests: The NLC is famous for organizing massive, nationwide protests (like "Occupy Nigeria" in 2012) whenever the government tries to remove the fuel subsidy, which leads to a sharp increase in petrol prices.

WEEK 4: EDUCATION AND DEVELOPMENT

Topic: Education and Development Content: UPE, UBE, tertiary education

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Define "education" and its link to national development.
Explain the "UPE" scheme of 1976.
Explain the "UBE" scheme of 1999.
Define "tertiary education" and give examples.

Lesson Content:

Education and National Development
Education is the process of acquiring knowledge, skills, values, and morals.
National Development is the growth of a country's economy, politics, and society.
The Link: Education is the key to national development.
It produces skilled workers (doctors, engineers, scientists) needed for a modern economy.
It creates an informed citizenry that can make good democratic choices.
It reduces poverty and improves health.
Because education is so important, the Nigerian government has tried to create programs to make it available to everyone.

UPE (Universal Primary Education)

What it was: This was a nationwide program launched by the Gowon military regime
in 1976. (It was also a key policy of Chief Awolowo in the 1950s West).
Goal: To provide free and compulsory primary school education for all children in Nigeria.
Funding: It was funded by the massive wealth from the 1970s "Oil Boom."
Outcome: It was a success at first and led to a massive explosion in primary school enrollment (millions of children went to school for the first time). However, it later faced problems with poor funding (after the oil price crashed), lack of teachers, and not enough classrooms.

UBE (Universal Basic Education)

What it is: This program was launched in 1999 by the Obasanjo civilian government to replace and improve upon the UPE.
Goal: To provide 9 years of free, compulsory basic education.
Difference: "Basic Education" is more than just primary school. The UBE covers:
6 years of Primary School
3 years of Junior Secondary School (JSS 1-3)
Funding: It is funded by the Federal, State, and Local governments. The Federal Government has a special UBE Fund (UBEC) to support the states.
Current Status: This is the official education policy of Nigeria today, though many states still struggle to implement it properly (e.g., "out-of-school" children).

Tertiary Education

Definition: This is "higher education" – any education that comes after secondary school.
Purpose: To produce high-level manpower (experts, leaders, and researchers).
Types of Tertiary Institutions:
Universities: (e.g., University of Ibadan, ABU Zaria, UNN). They award degrees (B.Sc., B.A., M.Sc., Ph.D.).
Polytechnics: (e.g., Yaba College of Technology). They focus on practical, technology-based skills and award National Diplomas (ND, HND).
Colleges of Education: (e.g., Alvan Ikoku). They are set up specifically to train teachers (NCE).

WEEK 6: NIGERIAN ECONOMY

Topic: Nigerian Economy Content: Agriculture, oil, manufacturing

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Define "economy."
Describe the role of agriculture in Nigeria's economy (pre-1970).
Describe the role of oil in Nigeria's economy (post-1970).
Define "manufacturing" and list its challenges in Nigeria.

Lesson Content:

What is an Economy? An economy is the system of how a country produces, distributes, and consumes goods and services. It is how a country creates wealth (its "income").

The Nigerian Economy: Two Main Eras

Era 1: The Agricultural Economy (Pre-1970s)
Before the oil boom, Nigeria's economy was 100% dependent on agriculture.
Agriculture was the main employer of citizens and the main source of government income (exports).
Each region specialized:
North: Groundnut (in pyramids), Cotton, Hides & Skin.
West: Cocoa.
East: Palm Oil and Palm Kernels.
This was a diverse and strong economy, and Nigeria was a major world exporter of these goods.
Era 2: The Oil Economy (Post-1970s)
The "Oil Curse": As we learned (First Term, Week 10), the oil boom of the 1970s caused Nigeria to abandon agriculture.
Monoculture Economy: Nigeria's economy became a "monoculture" – it depends on only one product (crude oil).
Effect: This is very dangerous. When the world price of oil is high, Nigeria is rich. When the price of oil crashes, the entire Nigerian economy (and government budget) crashes with it.
Oil now accounts for over 90% of Nigeria's export earnings but employs very few people.

The Manufacturing Sector

Definition: This is the sector of the economy that makes or produces finished goods. It involves turning raw materials into products.
Examples: Car assembly (e.g., Innoson), food processing (e.g., Dangote sugar, flour), textiles (making clothes), cement (e.g., Dangote, BUA).
Importance: A strong manufacturing sector is the key to development. It creates millions of jobs and allows a country to export finished goods, which are more valuable than raw materials.
Challenges in Nigeria: The manufacturing sector in Nigeria is very weak. It is hard to run a factory due to:

Poor Electricity: This is the biggest problem. Factories have to spend millions on diesel and generators, which is very expensive.
Poor Infrastructure: Bad roads make it hard to transport goods.
Competition: It is often cheaper to import goods from China than to make them in Nigeria.

WEEK 8: SCIENCE AND TECHNOLOGY IN NIGERIA

Topic: Science and Technology in Nigeria Content: Growth and challenges

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Differentiate between "science" and "technology."
Identify areas of technological growth in Nigeria (e.g., Telecoms, FinTech).
List three major challenges to scientific and technological development in Nigeria.
Identify key government agencies responsible for science and technology.

Lesson Content:

Science vs. Technology (Review)

Science: The knowledge we get from studying the natural world (e.g., Physics, Chemistry).
Technology: The application of that knowledge to create tools and solve problems (e.g., building a phone, a car, or an app).

Growth of Science and Technology in Nigeria Nigeria has seen significant growth in some
areas of technology, mostly driven by the private sector.

Telecommunications (Telecoms): This is Nigeria's biggest success story.
Before 2001, there were only about 400,000 phone lines.
After the introduction of GSM (e.g., MTN, Glo) in 2001, Nigeria now has over 200 million mobile lines. This has connected the entire country.
Financial Technology (FinTech):
This refers to technology used for banking and payments.
Nigeria is a world leader in FinTech. Companies like Interswitch, Flutterwave, and Paystack have created apps that allow millions of Nigerians to transfer money, pay bills, and run businesses from their phones.
Space Technology:
Nigeria has a space agency, NASCARDA (National Space Research and Development Agency).
Nigeria has launched several satellites (like NigeriaSat-1, NigeriaSat-2) into orbit for communication, and for monitoring the environment and security.

Challenges to S&T Development Despite these successes, Nigeria faces huge challenges in developing a strong scientific and technological base.

Poor Education System: The foundation is weak. Schools and universities lack modern laboratories, equipment, and well-trained science teachers.
Lack of Funding ("Brain Drain"):
The government does not spend enough money on Research and Development (R&D).
Because of low pay and poor facilities, Nigeria's best scientists, doctors, and engineers often leave the country to work in the USA, UK, or Canada. This is called the "Brain Drain."
Poor Infrastructure: The lack of constant electricity is the biggest challenge. You cannot run advanced labs, data centers, or high-tech factories without reliable power.
Poor Link Between Research and Industry: Universities' research often stays in a book. There is no connection to the factories that could use the research to build new products.

WEEK 9: SPORTS AND NATIONAL UNITY

Topic: Sports and National Unity Content: Notable Nigerian sports achievements

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Define "national unity."
Explain how sports can act as a tool for national unity.
List at least three notable Nigerian sports achievements.
Identify famous Nigerian sports heroes.

Lesson Content:

What is National Unity? National unity is the feeling of togetherness, patriotism, and "oneness" among the citizens of a diverse country like Nigeria. It is the idea that "I am a Nigerian first," before being Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa, or Efik.

Sports as a Tool for National Unity

Nigeria is a country with many ethnic groups, religions, and political differences. These differences often divide the nation.
Sports (especially football) is one of the few things that unites all Nigerians.
When the national football team, the Super Eagles, is playing, nobody cares what tribe or religion the players are. Everyone (Hausa, Igbo, Yoruba) cheers for Nigeria.
Victories in sports give the nation a shared sense of pride, joy, and identity, which helps to bridge divisions.

Notable Nigerian Sports Achievements

1. 1980 African Cup of Nations (AFCON):
Nigeria hosted and won the AFCON for the first time.
Led by captain Christian Chukwu, the "Green Eagles" (as they were then called) defeated Algeria in the final in Lagos. This was a major moment of national celebration, 10 years after the Civil War.
2. 1985 FIFA U-16 World Championship:
Nigeria's "Golden Eaglets" (the youth team) won the first-ever FIFA U-16 world cup in China.
This was Nigeria's first major trophy on the world stage, and it made the country feel like a true football power.
3. 1994 AFCON and World Cup:
This was the "golden generation" of the Super Eagles, with players like Rashidi Yekini, Stephen Keshi, and Jay-Jay Okocha.
They won the AFCON in Tunisia.
They then played in Nigeria's first-ever senior World Cup (USA '94), where they impressed the world with their exciting style of play.
4. 1996 Atlanta Olympics (Men's Football):
This is perhaps Nigeria's greatest sporting moment.
The "Dream Team," led by Nwankwo Kanu, won the Olympic Gold Medal in football.
They achieved this by famously defeating two of the world's greatest teams: Brazil and Argentina. This victory brought intense joy and unity to Nigeria during the difficult Abacha military regime.
5. Other Achievements:
Chioma Ajunwa won an individual Olympic Gold Medal in the Long Jump at the same 1996 Olympics.
Nigeria has won the AFCON a third time (2013).

WEEK 10: CULTURE AND TOURISM

Topic: Culture and Tourism Content: Nigerian cultural heritage; Tourism development

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Define "culture" and "cultural heritage."

Lesson Content:

What is Culture?

Culture: This is the total "way of life" of a group of people. It includes their language, food, clothing, beliefs, music, art, festivals, and traditions.
Cultural Heritage: This refers to the parts of our culture that are passed down from our ancestors, which we must preserve. This can be:
Tangible Heritage: Things you can touch (e.g., Benin Bronzes, Nok Terracottas, ancient buildings, traditional clothes).
Intangible Heritage: Things you cannot touch (e.g., traditional music, folk stories, proverbs, dance).

Nigerian Cultural Heritage Nigeria is one of the most culturally diverse countries in the world, with over 250 ethnic groups.

Art: Nigeria is famous for its ancient art, especially the Nok Terracottas, the Ife Heads (bronze and terracotta), and the Benin Bronzes (which were stolen by the British and are now in foreign museums).
Festivals: Nigeria has hundreds of colourful festivals.
Eyo Festival (Lagos)
Durbar (Kano, Zaria)
New Yam Festival (Igboland)
Argungu Fishing Festival (Kebbi)
Music: Nigeria is globally famous for its music, from traditional music to modern styles like Afrobeats (Wizkid, Burna Boy, Davido).
Film: Nigeria's film industry, Nollywood, is the second-largest in the world (by volume) and is a major cultural export.

What is Tourism?

Definition: Tourism is the act of traveling to a place (for business or pleasure) and the industry (hotels, transport, guides) that supports these travelers.
Tourist Attractions: These are the specific places people travel to see.
Cultural Sites: The Palace of the Oba of Benin, the Sultan's Palace in Sokoto.
Natural Sites:
Obudu Mountain Resort (Cross River): Famous for its high hills, cool climate, and cable car.
Zuma Rock (Niger State): The giant rock "with a human face" near Abuja.
Yankari Game Reserve (Bauchi): A large national park where people can see wildlife like elephants and baboons.
Waterfalls (e.g., Erin-Ijesha).

Challenges of Tourism Development Tourism should be a major source of income for Nigeria (like it is in Kenya or Egypt), but it is not. This is due to:

Insecurity: This is the biggest challenge. Tourists are afraid to travel in areas with kidnapping, banditry, or terrorism.
Poor Infrastructure: Bad roads, unreliable electricity, and poor-quality hotels make travel difficult.
Lack of Preservation: Many historical sites and museums are poorly maintained and falling apart.
