Lesson Content:

Review of Core Programming Concepts (JSS2)

A. What is Programming?
Definition: Programming (or coding) is the process of writing instructions for a computer (or any digital device) to follow to perform a specific task.
These instructions are written in a specific "programming language" that the computer understands.
Algorithm: The plan or step-by-step logic you create before you start coding. It's the "recipe" for solving a problem.
Debugging: The process of finding and fixing errors ("bugs") in your code.
B. Types of Programming Languages (Recap):

Block-Based (Visual): Uses interlocking graphical blocks to build code, like digital LEGOs. It's excellent for beginners.
Example: Scratch. You drag blocks like "Move 10 steps" or "When green flag clicked."
Text-Based: Uses written commands and syntax (grammar rules). This is how all professional software is built.
Example: Python, JavaScript, Java. (e.g., print("Hello World")).
C. The Building Blocks of Programming:
Variables: "Boxes" in the computer's memory used to store and label information that can change (e.g., score = 0, playerName = "Aisha").
Conditional Statements (IF/ELSE): Used for making decisions. They control the flow of the program.
Logic: IF a condition is true, THEN do this. ELSE (otherwise), do that.
Example: IF score > 10, THEN print "You win!".
Loops (Iteration): Used to repeat a block of code multiple times to avoid manual repetition.
Example: Instead of writing "Move 10 steps" 100 times, you use a loop: REPEAT 100 times [Move 10 steps].

Review of Robotics Concepts (JSS2)

A. What is Robotics?
Definition: A field of science and engineering that involves the design, construction, operation, and application of robots.
A robot is a programmable machine built to automatically carry out a series of complex actions.
B. Main Components of a Robot:

Controller (The "Brain"): The computer (microprocessor) that processes information and makes decisions. It runs the program (the software).
Sensors (The "Senses"): Devices that gather information about the robot's environment.
Examples: Light sensors (to see), touch sensors (to feel), ultrasonic sensors (to detect distance), cameras.
Actuators (The "Muscles"): The parts that make the robot move or do
something.
Examples: Motors (to turn wheels), grippers (to pick things up), speakers (to make sound), lights.
C. The Link: Programming and Robotics

A robot's body (sensors, actuators, wires) is the hardware.
The program (the code) is the software.
Robotics is the combination of both. The programming gives the "brain" (controller) the instructions to read the "senses" (sensors) and control the "muscles" (actuators).
Example: IF the Touch Sensor is pressed, THEN tell the Motor to stop.

Page Layout (Formatting Your Document) The "Layout" tab in MS Word controls the look and feel of your entire page before you even type.

A. Margins:
Definition: The empty, white space around the edges (top, bottom, left, right) of your document.
Use: Standard margin is 1 inch (or 2.54 cm). You can make them "Narrow" to fit more text on a page or "Wide" for a more formal look.
How: Layout Tab -> Margins -> Choose a preset (Normal, Narrow, etc.).
B. Orientation:
Definition: The direction your page is facing.

Portrait: Taller than it is wide (like a standard letter). This is the default.
Landscape: Wider than it is tall (like a certificate or a wide chart).

o How: Layout Tab -> Orientation -> Choose Portrait or Landscape.
C. Page Size:
Definition: The physical size of the paper you will print on.
Common Sizes:
A4: The standard size for most of the world (including Nigeria).
Letter: The standard size for the USA.
How: Layout Tab -> Size -> Choose A4.
D. Headers and Footers:
Definition: A "header" is text that appears at the top of every page. A "footer" is text that appears at the bottom of every page.
Use: For page numbers, document titles, or author's name.
How: Insert Tab -> Header or Footer. (To insert page numbers: Insert Tab -> Page Number).

Working with Tables Tables are used to organize data into rows and columns.

A. Creating a Table:
How: Insert Tab -> Table -> Drag your mouse over the grid to select the number of rows and columns.
B. Formatting a Table:
When you click on a table, two new tabs appear: "Table Design" and "Layout".
Table Design Tab:
Borders: Add or remove lines.
Shading: Change the background color of a cell.
Table Styles: Choose a pre-formatted, professional look.
Layout Tab (Table):
Merge Cells: Combine two or more cells into one large cell.
Split Cells: Divide one cell into multiple smaller cells.
Insert/Delete: Add or remove rows and columns.

Mail Merge

Definition: Mail Merge is a powerful tool used to create a large number of personalized
documents from a single template and a data source.
Purpose: To send the same letter to many different people, but with each person's unique details (like their name and address) inserted automatically.
Example Use: A school sending a "report card" letter to 1,000 parents. The letter (template) is the same, but the student's name, grade, and parent's name (data) are different for each one.
The Two Components:
The Template (Main Document): The MS Word file that contains the main text that will be the same for everyone (e.g., "Dear [Name], your result is...").
The Data Source: The file that contains the list of information that will change. This is usually an Excel spreadsheet or an MS Access database (e.g., a list of names, addresses, grades).
Simple Steps for Mail Merge:
Start: Go to the Mailings Tab -> Start Mail Merge -> Letters.
Write: Type your template letter in Word. (e.g., "Dear...").
Select Recipients: Mailings Tab -> Select Recipients -> Use an Existing List (and find your Excel file).
Insert Merge Field: Click where you want the name to go, then Mailings Tab -> Insert Merge Field -> Choose First_Name. Your letter will now look like: "Dear
<<First_Name>>,"
Preview: Mailings Tab -> Preview Results. You will see the <<First_Name>> replaced with the actual names from your list.
Finish: Mailings Tab -> Finish & Merge -> Edit Individual Documents (to create one big file) or Print Documents.

Lesson Content:
Slide Transitions

Definition: A Transition is the visual effect that occurs when you move from one slide to the next during a presentation.
It applies to the entire slide.
Purpose: To make the presentation flow smoothly and professionally, instead of just "cutting" abruptly from one slide to the next.
Examples:
Fade: The old slide fades out and the new one fades in.
Wipe: The new slide "wipes" across the old one.
Push: The new slide "pushes" the old one off the screen.
How to Apply:

Click on the slide you want to transition to.
Go to the Transitions Tab.
Choose an effect (e.g., Morph, Fade, Push).
You can set the Duration (how fast it is) and click Apply to All to make all slides the same.

Animations

Definition: An Animation is a visual effect applied to a single object on a slide.
It applies to individual items (like a piece of text, a picture, or a shape).
Purpose: To control the flow of information. It allows you to reveal points one by one, keeping the audience's attention.
Types of Animations:
Entrance: How the object enters the slide (e.g., Fly In, Appear, Zoom).
Emphasis: An effect to draw attention to an object already on the slide (e.g., Spin, Pulse).
Exit: How the object leaves the slide (e.g., Fly Out).
How to Apply:
Click on the object you want to animate (e.g., a text box).
Go to the Animations Tab.
Choose an effect (e.g., Fly In).
Use the "Animation Pane" to see the list of your animations and change their order.

Lesson Content:

What is Media?

Media: The plural of "medium." It refers to the channels or tools used to store and deliver information or data.
Traditional Media (or "Analog Media"): These are the "old" forms of media that are static and one-way.
o Examples: Printed books, newspapers, magazines, physical photographs, radio, television.

What is Digital Media?

Definition: Digital Media is any form of media content that is created, viewed, distributed, and stored in a digital (electronic) format.
It is "digital" because it is based on code (binary 0s and 1s) that a computer can read.
Key Feature: Digital media is often interactive, dynamic, and can be easily shared and
modified.

Categories of Digital Media Digital media is not just one thing. It includes:

Digital Text: (e.g., A web page, an e-book, a blog post, a WhatsApp message).
Digital Images: (e.g., Photos from your phone, JPEGs, PNGs, GIFs, digital paintings).
Digital Audio: (e.g., MP3 files, podcasts, streaming music, voice notes).
Digital Video: (e.g., Movies on Netflix, YouTube videos, TikTok videos, video calls).
Interactive Media: (e.g., Video games, websites, mobile apps, virtual reality).

Importance of Digital Media Digital media has revolutionized every aspect of modern life:

Communication: Allows for instant, global, and multimedia communication (e.g., email, social media, video conferencing).
Education: Provides access to unlimited information and learning resources (e.g., e- books, online courses, educational videos on YouTube).
Business (Marketing): Allows businesses to reach customers around the world through social media, websites, and digital ads.
Entertainment: Provides "on-demand" entertainment (e.g., Netflix, Spotify).
Information (News): News is now instant and global, delivered through news websites and social media platforms.

Creating and Editing Digital Content

"Creating" is the act of making new digital media (e.g., taking a photo, recording your voice, typing a blog post).
"Editing" is the act of modifying, improving, or combining existing media.
Common Tools:
Text: MS Word, Google Docs (Creating/Editing).
Images: Adobe Photoshop (Professional), Canva (Beginner/Online), phone's photo editor (Editing).
Audio: Audacity (Free software), phone's voice recorder (Creating).
Video: Adobe Premiere Pro (Professional), CapCut (Mobile/Online), iMovie (Apple) (Editing).

Lesson Content:
What is Intellectual Property (IP)?

Definition: "Intellectual Property" refers to creations of the mind. It is "property" (like a car or a house), but it is intangible (you can't touch it).
Examples: A story you write, a song you compose, a painting you make, a logo you design, an invention.

What is Copyright?

Definition: Copyright is a legal right that automatically gives the creator of an original creative work (like a book, song, or photo) the exclusive right to control how it is used, copied, and distributed.
What it protects: Music, books, movies, photos, software, graphic designs.
The "©" Symbol: This is the symbol for copyright. It means "All Rights Reserved." You cannot use this work without permission (a license) from the owner.
Copyright vs. Plagiarism (JSS2 Recap):
Plagiarism: An academic offense. It is claiming someone else's work is your own (cheating).
Copyright Infringement: A legal offense. It is using, copying, or selling someone else's work without permission, even if you give them credit. (e.g., uploading a full Nollywood movie to your YouTube channel).

Digital Rights and Media Ethics

Digital Rights: Your rights as a creator and user of digital media. This includes your right to privacy, freedom of speech, and the right to control your own creations (copyright).
Media Ethics: The moral principles that guide how we use and create media.
Ethical Rule: Just because you can "right-click and save" an image from Google, doesn't mean you should use it in your project without permission or giving credit.

"Fair Use" (or "Fair Dealing" in Nigeria)
Definition: "Fair Use" is a limited exception to the copyright law. It allows you to use a
small part of a copyrighted work without permission for specific, limited purposes.
Purpose: To balance the rights of the creator with the public's right to information and education.
Allowed Uses (Generally):
Criticism/Review: Using a 10-second clip of a movie in your video review of that movie.
News Reporting: A news channel showing a photo of an event.
Education/Teaching: A teacher photocopying one chapter (not the whole book) for students.
Parody: Making a "comedy" version of a song.
What is NOT Fair Use:

Using the whole work.
Using it for commercial purposes (to make money).
Downloading a "cracked" (pirated) version of MS Word.

Creative Commons (CC)

Definition: An alternative to "All Rights Reserved." A Creative Commons (CC) license is a way for creators to give the public permission in advance to share and use their work for free, as long as they follow certain rules.
The "Some Rights Reserved" License:
CC-BY (Attribution): The most common. "You can use my photo for free, even to make money, as long as you give me credit (attribute me)."
Where to find CC content: Wikipedia, Flickr, and many online photo sites.

What is a Digital Portfolio?

Old vs. New: In the past, an artist would carry a large, physical "portfolio" (a big folder) of their drawings. A digital portfolio puts this online, making it accessible to anyone, anywhere in the world.
It is your digital resume. It shows what you can do, rather than just telling.

Purpose of a Digital Portfolio

To get a job.
To apply for admission to a university.
To get freelance clients.
To track your own progress and skills.
To showcase your creativity (e.g., your graphic designs, photos, or writing).

Graphic Designs: Your best posters, flyers, or logos made in Canva (from Week 6).

What are Ethics?

What are Cyber Ethics?

Definition: Cyber Ethics are the moral principles and code of conduct for using the internet and digital devices. It is the study of "right and wrong" in the online world.
Key Question: "Just because I can do this online, should I do it?"

What is Netiquette?

Definition: A combination of the words "Network" and "Etiquette." Netiquette is the set of rules for polite and respectful behavior when communicating online.
Importance: In the real world, we see a person's face and hear their tone of voice. Online, we only see text. This makes it very easy for misunderstandings to happen. Netiquette provides the "polite" rules to prevent conflict.

The Core Rules of Netiquette (Based on the classic rules by Virginia Shea)

Rule 1: Remember the Human.
o Do not say anything to someone online that you would not say to their face. There is a real person with feelings behind that screen.
Rule 2: Adhere to the same standards of behavior online that you follow in real life.
Be ethical. Obey the law.
Rule 3: Know where you are in cyberspace.
Netiquette changes from place to place. Behavior that is acceptable in a WhatsApp group with your close friends is not acceptable in a formal email to your teacher or in a professional LinkedIn message.
Rule 4: Respect other people's time and bandwidth.
Be brief and to the point. Don't send huge, unnecessary files. Don't "spam" a group chat with 50 unrelated messages.
Rule 5: Make yourself look good online.
Write clearly. Use correct spelling and grammar. Know what you're talking about and be logical.
Rule 6: Share expert knowledge.
If you know the answer to a question, share it. This is how the internet grows.
Rule 7: Help keep "flame wars" under control.
Flame War: An angry, insulting, and personal argument that erupts in a public online forum (like a comments section or group chat).
How to stop it: Do not participate. Do not "feed the troll" (reply to the angry person). Ignore them or calmly state the facts.
Rule 8: Respect other people's privacy.
Do not "snoop" in someone's files. Do not share someone's private email or photo without their permission.
Rule 9: Don't abuse your power.
If you are a system administrator or the "admin" of a group chat, do not abuse your power over other users.
Rule 10: Be forgiving of other people's mistakes.
If someone makes a spelling error or a stupid comment, be kind about it. Correct them politely and privately, not by shaming them in public.

Other Netiquette Rules

DON'T TYPE IN ALL CAPS. It is interpreted as SHOUTING and is considered rude.
Be careful with sarcasm and jokes. They are often misunderstood in text. Use emojis
� to show your tone, but use them professionally.
Don't spam. Do not forward every message, chain letter, or "fake news" you receive.

Lesson Content:
What is Online Privacy?

Definition: Online Privacy (or Digital Privacy) is your right to control what information about you is collected and shared online, and who has access to it.
It is the "right to be left alone" in the digital world.

What is Personal Identifiable Information (PII)?

Definition: PII is any data that can be used to identify you specifically. This is the information you must protect.
Examples of PII:
Full Name
Home Address
Phone Number
Date of Birth
Bank Verification Number (BVN)
National Identity Number (NIN)
Your Passwords
Your mother's maiden name
Photos/Videos of you

What is Digital Theft?

Definition: Any crime where a thief steals your digital information to make money.
Identity Theft: The most common form. A criminal steals your PII (like your name, NIN, and bank details) to impersonate you. They can use your identity to take out loans, steal your money, or commit crimes in your name.
Phishing: The method used to steal your PII.
A scammer sends you a fake email or text message that looks like it's from a real company (e.g., your bank, Netflix, or Google).
The message says there is a "problem with your account" and you must "Click this link to log in and fix it."
The link takes you to a fake website (a "phishing site") that looks identical to the real one.
When you type your username and password, you are not giving it to your bank; you are giving it directly to the thief.

How to Protect Your Privacy and Avoid Theft

Use Strong, Unique Passwords.
Strong: At least 12 characters, with uppercase (A), lowercase (a), numbers (1), and symbols (@#$!).
Unique: Use a different password for every important account (Email, Bank, Social Media).
Enable Two-Factor Authentication (2FA).
This is the best protection. Even if a thief steals your password, they also need a second code (sent to your phone) to log in.
Think Before You Click (Avoid Phishing).
Always be suspicious of urgent emails.
Never click a link in an email to log in to your bank. Instead, type the bank's real website address (www.gtbank.com) into your browser manually.
Manage Your Privacy Settings.
Go into the settings of Instagram, Facebook, and WhatsApp. Set your profile to "Private."
Control who can see your posts and tag you.
Be Wary of Public Wi-Fi.
"Free" Wi-Fi (at airports, hotels, cafes) is not secure.
Avoid logging into your bank or shopping online when using public Wi-Fi. A hacker on the same network can steal your data.

WEEK 1: MANAGEMENT OF E-WASTE

Content: (I) Meaning and sources of e-waste (II) Safe disposal methods (III) Environmental effects.

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Describe at least three (3) safe methods for managing e-waste.
Differentiate e-waste from general (biodegradable) waste.
Lesson Content:

What is e-Waste?

Definition: e-Waste (Electronic Waste) is a term for all discarded, obsolete, or broken electrical or electronic devices.
It is any household or office appliance with a battery or plug that has reached the end of its "useful life."
e-Waste is the fastest-growing type of waste in the world due to the high demand for new technology.

Common Sources of e-Waste e-Waste is generated from a wide range of products:

Communication Devices: Smartphones (e.g., old Tecno, iPhone), feature phones, landlines, modems, routers.
Computing Devices: Laptops, desktop computers (CPUs, monitors), tablets, printers, scanners, keyboards, mice, hard drives.
Entertainment Electronics: Televisions (especially old CRT "box" TVs), DVD players, CD players, game consoles (e.g., PlayStation, Xbox), radios.
Household Appliances: Microwaves, blenders, refrigerators, air conditioners, electric fans, washing machines.
Power & Accessories: Batteries (phone, laptop, inverter), power banks, chargers, USB cables, stabilizers (UPS).
Lighting: Fluorescent tubes, LED bulbs.

Environmental and Health Effects of Improper Disposal e-Waste is extremely hazardous because it contains toxic heavy metals and chemicals. When dumped in regular landfills or burned, these toxins poison the environment and people.

Location: Places like Alaba Market or Computer Village in Lagos, which have large informal repair/disposal markets, are at high risk.
Major Toxins and Their Effects:
Lead (Pb): Found in old TV/computer monitors (CRT) and solder. It is a potent neurotoxin that damages the brain, nervous system, and kidneys, especially in children.
Mercury (Hg): Found in fluorescent bulbs and flat-screen (LCD) monitors. It damages the brain and kidneys. When burned, it enters the air and falls into rivers, poisoning fish and the people who eat them.
Cadmium (Cd): Found in old rechargeable batteries and semiconductors. It is a carcinogen (causes cancer) and severely damages the lungs and kidneys.
Brominated Flame Retardants (BFRs): Chemicals added to plastic casings to prevent fire. When burned, they release dioxins and furans, which are highly toxic, cause cancer, and disrupt human hormones.
Environmental Impact:

Water Pollution: When rain falls on an e-waste dump, these heavy metals leach (dissolve and seep) into the soil, contaminating groundwater and nearby rivers. This kills aquatic life and makes water unsafe for drinking.
Air Pollution: The common practice of burning e-waste (e.g., burning wires to get the copper inside) releases a black, toxic smoke. This smoke contains carcinogens and heavy metals, causing severe respiratory problems (like asthma) for people nearby.

Safe Disposal and Management Methods e-Waste must never be thrown in the regular bin or burned.

The 3 Rs (Reduce, Reuse, Recycle):
Reduce: The best method. Use your devices for as long as possible. Don't upgrade your phone every year if it still works.
Reuse / Refurbish: (See Week 2). Repair broken devices. Donate your old, working laptop to a school or a student who needs it.
Recycle (Formal): This is the most important disposal method.
Formal e-Waste Recycling:
This involves sending the e-waste to a specialized factory (not an open-air market).
In the factory, the devices are safely dismantled.
Toxic parts (like batteries) are separated and treated.
Valuable materials (gold, copper, silver, aluminum, plastic) are recovered and can be used to make new products.
Take-Back Programs:
Many large manufacturers (like HP, Dell, Apple) and telecommunication companies (like MTN) have programs where you can return your old, "end-of- life" product to them. They are then responsible for recycling it safely.
Government Regulation:
Governments (like through NESREA in Nigeria) must create laws that ban the dumping of e-waste and support the creation of formal, safe recycling companies.

WEEK 2: RECYCLING DIGITAL DEVICES

Content: (I) Refurbishing and reusing old gadgets.

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Lesson Content:

The 3 Rs of e-Waste Management (This is the guiding principle for managing all waste, especially e-waste)
1. REDUCE: The best solution. Buy fewer gadgets. Use your phone for 4 years, not 1.
2. REUSE: The second-best solution. If a gadget still works, let someone else use it.
3. RECYCLE: The last and final solution. If a gadget is truly broken and cannot be reused, break it down to recover its raw materials.

Understanding "Reuse"

Definition: "Reuse" is the act of passing a product on to a second, third, or fourth user with minimal or no changes.
It extends the life of a product.
Examples:
Giving your old (but working) smartphone to a younger sibling or a parent.
Donating old (but working) computers from an office to a school.
Selling your old phone on Jiji or to a repair shop.
Benefit: This is highly sustainable. It keeps a functional product out of the landfill and provides a low-cost device for someone who may not afford a new one.

Understanding "Refurbishing"

Definition: "Refurbishing" is a formal process of rebuilding a used, returned, or slightly faulty product to a "like-new" condition.
Refurbishing vs. Repairing:
Repairing: Fixes one specific problem (e.g., "My screen is cracked, replace it").
Refurbishing: A complete overhaul. The device is:
Tested: A full diagnostic is run.
Cleaned: All dust is removed, and the casing is cleaned.
Repaired: Any faulty parts (like an old battery or a slow hard drive) are
replaced.
Wiped: All personal data from the previous owner is securely erased (a "factory reset").
Certified: The device is tested again and often sold with a new, short warranty (e.g., "6-Month Certified Refurbished").
Examples: "Refurbished iPhones," "Refurbished Laptops." These are very common in markets like Computer Village.

Benefits of Reusing and Refurbishing

Environmental Benefits:
Reduces e-Waste: Every refurbished laptop sold is one less laptop in a landfill.
Saves Energy: It takes far more energy (electricity, oil, gas) to manufacture a new laptop from raw materials than to refurbish an old one.
Conserves Resources: It reduces the need to mine new gold, copper, and coltan.
Economic Benefits:
Affordability: Provides high-quality, reliable devices to low-income students, families, and businesses.
Job Creation: Creates a large and skilled industry of technicians, repair specialists, and salespersons.

The Dangers of Informal Recycling

The Problem: True, formal recycling (as discussed in Week 1) is expensive and requires a factory. In many parts of Nigeria, "recycling" is done informally.
Informal Recycling: This involves untrained people (often children) in open-air scrap yards (like Agbogbloshie in Ghana or parts of Alaba in Lagos) breaking e-waste apart with hammers and burning it to recover valuable metals.
The Dangers:
Extreme Health Hazard: As seen in Week 1, these workers are exposed directly to lead, mercury, and cadmium. They breathe toxic smoke daily, leading to severe lung damage, brain damage, and cancer.
Environmental Devastation: The ground and water in these areas become "dead zones," so poisoned with heavy metals that nothing can grow, and the water is undrinkable.
Solution: We must move from this dangerous informal practice to formal refurbishing and recycling industries.

What are Emerging Technologies?

Definition: "Emerging Technologies" are new technologies whose development and application are still in their early stages, but they are expected to have a significant impact on society, business, and our daily lives in the near future.
These are the "next big things" in the digital world.

Key Emerging Technologies

A. The Internet of Things (IoT)
Definition: A giant network of physical objects (devices, vehicles, home appliances) that are embedded with sensors and software that allow them to connect to the internet to send and receive data.
Simple Meaning: Giving everyday "dumb" objects the power of the internet, making them "smart."
Examples:
Smartwatch: An IoT device. It uses sensors to track your heart rate and sends the data to your phone via the internet.
Smart Home: A "smart bulb" you can turn on/off with your phone from anywhere, or a "smart fridge" that tells you when you are out of milk.
Smart City: Using IoT sensors to manage traffic lights, monitor pollution, or detect water leaks.
B. Cloud Computing
Definition: (Recap from JSS3 Term 1) The delivery of "on-demand" computing services—including servers, storage, databases, and software—over the Internet (the "cloud").
Simple Meaning: Using someone else's powerful computer (a "server" in a data center) through the internet, instead of using your own.
You use it every day:
Cloud Storage: Google Drive, iCloud, Dropbox.
Cloud Apps: Canva, Google Docs, GMail, Netflix. (The software runs on their server, not your device).
Importance: It allows small businesses (digital entrepreneurs) to access powerful tools (like databases and servers) without having to buy expensive hardware.
C. Blockchain
Definition: A decentralized, distributed, and immutable digital ledger.
Breakdown:
Ledger: A book for recording transactions (like a bank's record book).
Digital: It's on a computer.
Distributed: It is not owned by one person or bank. Everyone on the network has an identical copy of the ledger.
Decentralized: There is no central boss (like a bank manager). The network is run by all its members.
Immutable: "Immutable" means unchangeable. Once a transaction ("block") is added to the "chain," it cannot be altered or deleted, ever. This makes it highly secure and transparent.
Use: Its most famous use is for Cryptocurrency (like Bitcoin), but it can also be used for secure voting, tracking land ownership, or verifying the source of a product (e.g., "Is this drug fake?").
D. Augmented Reality (AR) vs. Virtual Reality (VR)
Augmented Reality (AR):
Definition: Overlays digital information (images, text) onto the real world through your device (usually a phone camera). It adds to (augments) reality.
Example:
Snapchat / Instagram Filters: They "augment" your real face with digital dog ears.
Pokémon Go: You see the real street through your camera, but a digital Pokémon is overlaid on top.
Google Maps Live View: You hold up your phone, and it overlays a digital arrow onto the real street to show you where to turn.
Virtual Reality (VR):
Definition: Replaces your reality with a completely immersive, 3D, computer-generated environment. You are "transported" to a new world.
Requires: A VR headset (like the Meta Quest or PlayStation VR) that blocks your view of the real world.
Example: A VR game where you are inside the game, able to look around 360 degrees. A VR simulation where a medical student can "practice" surgery.

How Does AI "Learn"? (Machine Learning)

Definition: Machine Learning (ML) is a subset of AI. It is the main technique used to create AI.
Process: Instead of a programmer writing exact rules for every situation, they "train" a program (an "model") by feeding it massive amounts of data.
Example (How AI learns to recognize a cat):
You don't write rules like "IF it has whiskers AND pointy ears...".
Instead, you show the ML model 1 million pictures, labeled "Cat" or "Not Cat."
The model learns for itself to identify the patterns that make a picture a "cat."
After training, it can accurately identify a cat in a new photo it has never seen before.

Artificial Intelligence in YOUR Everyday Life AI is not just in science fiction movies; you use it every single day, mostly without noticing.

Voice Assistants (Siri, Google Assistant, Alexa):
When you say, "Hey Google, what is the weather today?", AI is used for:
Natural Language Processing (NLP): To understand your spoken words.
Search: To find the answer.
Speech Synthesis: To speak the answer back to you in a human-sounding voice.
Recommendation Engines (The "For You" Page):
Netflix / YouTube: The AI learns your viewing habits. It "knows" you watch a lot of action movies and comedy. It then recommends new, similar content, (e.g., "Because you watched...").
TikTok / Instagram Reels: The "For You" page is 100% AI. It learns very quickly (from your likes, shares, and even how long you watch a video) what kind of content will keep you scrolling.
Spam Filters (in Email):
How does GMail know that an email is "spam"?
It is an AI model trained on billions of spam emails. It has learned to recognize the patterns (e.g., words like "winner," "congratulations," "free money," bad spelling) and automatically moves them from your inbox.
Navigation Apps (Google Maps / Waze):
How does Google Maps know there is traffic on Third Mainland Bridge right now?
It uses AI to analyze live, anonymous data from thousands of other phones (also using Google Maps) that are moving slowly on that road. It then predicts your arrival time and "re-routes" you.
Chatbots (Customer Service):
When you visit a bank's website and a little window pops up saying, "Hi, I'm [Bank]'s digital assistant! How can I help you?"... that is an AI chatbot.
Phone Camera:
When you take a photo in "Portrait Mode," AI is what identifies the person and
blurs the background.

Types of AI

1. Narrow AI (or "Weak AI"):
This is all the AI we have today.
It is an AI that is specialized and trained to do one single task (or a very narrow set of tasks).
Example: The Netflix AI is a genius at recommending movies, but it cannot drive a car, write a poem, or diagnose a disease.
2. General AI (AGI) (or "Strong AI"):
This is the "Hollywood" version of AI (like The Terminator, Ultron, or Data from Star Trek).
It is a hypothetical future AI that would have the full range of human intelligence—the ability to reason, plan, learn, and be "conscious" or "self- aware."
This does not exist.

1. Global Awareness & Perspective:
Responsibility: To understand that your local problems and views are not the only ones.
Action: Use the internet to learn about other cultures, religions, and political situations. Follow news from other countries. Understand that a joke that is "funny" in Nigeria might be deeply offensive to someone in India or Brazil.
2. Cross-Cultural Respect (Global Netiquette):
Responsibility: To communicate with people from different backgrounds with
respect and politeness, even when you disagree.
Action: Avoid using stereotypes, racist, or nationalist insults. Do not assume your way is the "only" right way. Be patient with people who are not fluent in your language.
3. Digital Empathy:
Definition: "Empathy" is the ability to understand and share the feelings of another person. "Digital Empathy" is applying this skill online.
Responsibility: To pause before posting or commenting and ask: "How would this make the other person feel?"
Action: This is the #1 tool to fight cyberbullying (Week 3). Before you post a mean comment, empathize with the person in the photo. This prevents online conflict and promotes a healthier global community.
4. Global Ethics and Responsibility:
Responsibility: To understand that your actions contribute to global problems or solutions.
Action (Negative): Participating in international scams ("Yahoo-Yahoo") damages the reputation of all Nigerians globally. Spreading global misinformation (e.g., about health crises) can cause real-world harm.
Action (Positive): Using the internet to collaborate on global projects (e.g., contributing to Wikipedia, working on an open-source code project, or participating in a global climate change awareness campaign).

The "Global Village"

This is a term used to describe the world, which has been "shrunk" by technology (internet, airplanes).
A message that once took 6 months to travel by ship now takes 0.6 seconds. We are all "neighbors" in a single "Global Village."
As a Global Digital Citizen, your responsibility is to be a good neighbor.

WEEK 9: TECHNOLOGY AND SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT

Content: (I) Role of ICT in achieving SDGs.

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Recall the "SDGs" (Sustainable Development Goals).
Explain how ICT can help achieve SDG 3 (Good Health).
Explain how ICT can help achieve SDG 4 (Quality Education).
Explain how ICT can help achieve SDG 7 (Clean Energy) and SDG 8 (Decent Work).

Lesson Content:
Definitions (Recap)

Sustainable Development: "Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs." (Balancing Economy, Society, and Environment).
SDGs (Sustainable Development Goals): The 17 "Global Goals" set by the UN to achieve a better world by 2030 (e.g., No Poverty, Zero Hunger, Quality Education, Climate Action).
ICT (Information & Comm. Technology): The umbrella term for all digital technologies (computers, internet, mobile phones, software).

The Role of ICT in Achieving the SDGs Technology is one of the most powerful tools humanity has to solve the world's biggest problems. ICT can accelerate (speed up) our progress toward the SDGs.

ICT for SDG 3: Good Health and Well-being
Telemedicine: Using video calls (like Zoom or WhatsApp) for "virtual appointments" with a doctor. This allows people in remote villages (who live far from a hospital) to get a diagnosis and medical advice.
Health Information: Mobile apps (like "mHealth") can track disease outbreaks (like cholera) in real-time, send vaccination reminders to mothers, and give people life-saving health information.
Research: AI is used to analyze medical data to discover new drugs and treatments faster than ever before.
ICT for SDG 4: Quality Education
Access to Information: The internet (Google, Wikipedia, YouTube) gives any student with a smartphone access to more information than is in any physical library.
E-Learning Platforms: Websites and apps (like ULesson, Khan Academy, Coursera) provide high-quality video lessons in Math, Science, and Coding, which can be accessed from anywhere.
Collaboration Tools: Google Classroom and Google Docs allow teachers and students to collaborate on assignments digitally.
ICT for SDG 7: Affordable and Clean Energy
Smart Grids (IoT): Using IoT sensors to build a "smart" electrical grid that monitors energy usage in real-time. This reduces waste, detects faults (like a "NEPA" fault) instantly, and makes it easier to add renewable energy (like solar) to the system.
ICT for SDG 8: Decent Work and Economic Growth
Digital Entrepreneurship (Week 1): The internet and mobile money (Fintech) create a new digital economy, allowing millions of young people to start businesses and create new jobs.
Remote Work: Collaboration tools (like Zoom and Google Docs) allow people to "work from home," providing decent jobs without needing to move to an overcrowded city.
ICT for SDG 16: Peace, Justice, and Strong Institutions
Fighting Corruption: Using technology to promote transparency. (e.g., "BudgIT," a Nigerian org, uses ICT to track government spending and publishes it online for all citizens to see).
Digital Identity: Using secure digital ID (like NIN) can help ensure all citizens have access to legal services and can vote.

WEEK 10: FINAL PROJECT: STUDENT DIGITAL INNOVATION

Content: (I) Individual or group project presentation.

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Identify a local, national, or global problem (e.g., an SDG).
Brainstorm a "digital innovation" (a tech-based idea) to help solve it.
Use digital tools (Canva, Google Sites, etc.) to create a prototype or presentation of their idea.
Present their idea, problem, and solution to the class.
Practice skills in teamwork, critical thinking, and public speaking.
Lesson Content:

Project Objective (The "BECE" Project) This is the final, capstone project for all of JSS Digital Technologies. The goal is to act like a Social Digital Entrepreneur.

Identify a Problem: Find a real problem in your school, community, Nigeria, or the world (e.g., any of the SDGs).
Propose a Solution: Brainstorm a digital solution. This could be an app, a website, a social media campaign, or a new tech idea.
Create a Prototype: Build a small "mock-up" of your idea to show the class.
Present (Pitch) Your Idea: Explain the problem and your solution, like an entrepreneur pitching to an investor.

Project Ideas (Group or Individual)

Problem: e-Waste (Week 1)
Solution: "Green-Tech Initiative."
Prototype: A 1-page Google Site that educates people on e-waste dangers and provides a (mock) map of "drop-off" locations.
Problem: Fake News & Cyberbullying (Term 2)
Solution: "JSS3 Digital Eagles."
Prototype: A series of 3 powerful Canva infographics (posters) designed for Instagram, each one teaching a "Rule of Netiquette" or "How to Spot Fake News."
Problem: Poor SDG Awareness (Week 9)
Solution: "SDG Naija Kids."
Prototype: A simple Scratch Animation (from JSS2) or a PowerPoint presentation (from Term 1) that explains one of the SDGs (like Clean Water) to JSS1 students.
Problem: Local Business Needs Help (Term 2)
Solution: "Lagos Student Marketers."
Prototype: A 3-slide PowerPoint pitching your "Social Media Management" service to a local business, showing a sample post you designed for them in Canva.

Project Steps (To be done over the week):

Form Groups (Optional).
Choose a Problem.
Brainstorm a Solution.
Assign Tasks: (e.g., one person on research, one on Canva design, one on Google Site building).
Build the Prototype.
Practice the Presentation (3-5 minutes per group).

Presentation Structure Each group must present:

The Problem: "The problem we chose is..."
The Solution: "Our digital innovation is called... It is a [website/app/campaign]..."
The Prototype: (Show the class your Google Site, your Canva designs, etc.)
The Impact: "This will help by..."

Evaluation (Project-Based):

(Problem) Was the problem clearly defined and relevant?
(Solution) Was the digital solution creative and practical?
(Prototype) Was the digital prototype (Canva, Google Site) well-executed and professional?
(Presentation) Was the presentation clear, confident, and persuasive?

Assignment:

(In class) Form your group and decide on your project topic, solution, and prototype.
(At home) Begin work on your prototype.
(At home) Write a 1-paragraph "mission statement" for your project.
(At home) Prepare your 3-minute presentation script.
(Next Week) Be ready to present your final project.
