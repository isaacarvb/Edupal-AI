SPEECH WORK

Topic: Revision of Speech Sounds (Vowels and Consonants)

Introduction

Speech work (Phonology and Phonetics) is the study of how sounds are produced and used in a language. A clear understanding of speech sounds is the foundation for correct pronunciation and effective communication. This week, we will revise the key sounds from JSS2.

Vowel Sounds (Revision)
Vowel sounds are produced with no obstruction in the vocal tract. They are the nucleus of every syllable. English has 20 vowel sounds, divided into monophthongs (pure vowels) and diphthongs (glide vowels).

Monophthongs (Pure Vowels - 12) These sounds have a single, consistent quality.

Front Vowels:
/i:/ as in seat, feet, me
/ɪ/ as in sit, hit, bin
/e/ as in set, bed, men
/æ/ as in sat, cat, man
Central Vowels:
/ɜ:/ as in bird, shirt, fur
/ə/ (schwa) as in about, teacher, doctor (the most common, weakest sound)
/ʌ/ as in cut, bus, sun
Back Vowels:
/u:/ as in shoe, blue, food
/ʊ/ as in book, put, could
/ɔ:/ as in caught, door, law
/ɒ/ as in cot, hot, pot
/ɑ:/ as in father, car, hard

Diphthongs (Glide Vowels - 8) These sounds are a combination of two pure vowels, involving a glide from one to the other within the same syllable.

/eɪ/ as in say, pain, fate
/aɪ/ as in my, buy, high
/ɔɪ/ as in boy, coin, toy
/əʊ/ (UK) or /oʊ/ (US) as in go, no, road
/aʊ/ as in now, house, cow
/ɪə/ as in here, near, fear
/eə/ as in hair, air, there
/ʊə/ as in poor, tour, sure

Consonant Sounds (Revision)

Consonant sounds are produced with some obstruction or blockage of airflow in the vocal tract. We will focus on the sounds that often cause confusion.

The 'th' sounds (Dental Fricatives):
/θ/ (Voiceless): Air passes between the tongue and top teeth. No vocal cord vibration.
Examples: thin, think, math, path, thunder
/ð/ (Voiced): Same mouth position, but the vocal cords vibrate.
Examples: this, that, mother, father, breathe
The 'sh' and 'j' sounds (Palato-Alveolar):
/ʃ/ (Voiceless): The "sh" sound.
Examples: shoe, ship, wash, nation, machine
/ʒ/ (Voiced): The "zh" sound, often found in the middle of words.
Examples: pleasure, treasure, measure, television, beige
/tʃ/ (Voiceless): The "ch" sound.
Examples: church, chair, watch, catch
/dʒ/ (Voiced): The "j" or "soft g" sound.
Examples: judge, jam, giant, age, bridge

GRAMMAR

Topic: Revision of the Eight Parts of Speech

Introduction

The parts of speech are categories that words are grouped into based on their function in a sentence. Understanding them is the key to building and analyzing sentences.

Noun

Definition: A word that names a person (Sade), animal (goat), place (Lagos), thing (book), or idea (love).
Types:
Proper: Specific names (capitalized). E.g., JSS3, Mr. Ade, Nigeria.
Common: General names. E.g., school, teacher, country.
Abstract: Ideas, feelings, qualities (cannot be touched). E.g., honesty, fear, courage.
Concrete: Things that can be touched. E.g., desk, water, smoke.
Collective: A name for a group. E.g., flock, crowd, committee, government.
Countable: Can be counted. E.g., three chairs, many boys.
Uncountable: Cannot be counted (no plural). E.g., rice, information, advice.
Functions: Subject, Object, Complement.

Pronoun

Definition: A word used in place of a noun to avoid repetition.
Types:
Personal: I, you, he, she, it, we, they, me, him, her, us, them.
Possessive: My, your, his, her, its, our, their, mine, yours, hers.
Reflexive: Myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves.
Demonstrative: This, that, these, those.
Interrogative: Who, what, which, where, when, whose.
Relative: Who, which, that (introduces a clause).

Verb

Definition: A word that shows an action (run, jump) or a state of being (is, are, feel). It is the most important word in a sentence.
Types:
Transitive: Requires a direct object. E.g., She wrote <u>a letter</u>.
Intransitive: Does not require an object. E.g., He slept.
Linking: Connects the. subject to a noun or adjective. E.g., He is <u>a doctor</u>. She seems <u>happy</u>.
Auxiliary (Helping): Helps the main verb.
Primary: be, have, do. E.g., I am running. He has eaten.
Modal: can, could, may, might, will, would, shall, should, must. E.g., You
must study.

Adjective

Definition: A word that modifies (describes or limits) a noun or pronoun. It answers:
What kind? How many? Which one?
Examples: a tall building, three goats, the blue sky, a beautiful girl.
Degrees: Positive (tall), Comparative (taller), Superlative (tallest).

Adverb

Definition: A word that modifies a verb, an adjective, or another adverb. It answers:
How? When? Where? How often? To what extent?
Types & Examples:
Manner: He ran quickly.
Time: We will leave tomorrow.
Place: She looked around.
Frequency: I never see him.
Degree: It is very hot.

Preposition

Definition: A word that shows the relationship between a noun/pronoun (its object) and another word in the sentence.
Examples: The book is on the table. He went to the store. She is in the room. He sat
under the tree.

Conjunction

Definition: A word that joins words, phrases, or clauses.
Types:
Coordinating (FANBOYS): For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So. (Joins equal parts).
Subordinating: because, while, although, if, when, since. (Joins a dependent clause to an independent one).
Correlative: either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also.

Interjection

Definition: An exclamatory word that shows strong emotion and is grammatically separate from the sentence.
Examples: Ouch!, Wow!, Alas!, Oh no!, Hurray!

READING & COMPREHENSION

Topic: Scanning for Main Points (and its difference from Skimming)
Introduction

Good readers use different strategies for different tasks. You do not read a novel the same way you read a phone directory. Scanning and Skimming are two essential "speed reading" strategies.

Clarification: The topic mentions "Scanning for main points." This is a common confusion.
The technique for finding main points or the general idea (gist) is SKIMMING.
The technique for finding specific details (like a name, date, or number) is
SCANNING.
This lesson will focus on SCANNING, as it is a distinct skill.

What is Scanning?

Scanning is the process of moving your eyes quickly over a text to find a specific piece of information. You are not reading every word.

Analogy: Imagine you are looking for your friend's name in a list of 100 students. You don't read every name; you let your eyes "scan" for the specific name.

How to Scan Effectively

Know Your Target: Be very clear on what you are looking for before you start. Is it a name? A date? A number? A keyword?
Use Keywords: Focus only on the keywords related to your target.
Move Your Eyes Quickly: Do not read in a straight line. Move your eyes in a zig-zag or "S" pattern down the page.
Ignore Everything Else: Your goal is to find one thing. You must ignore all other information, even if it seems interesting.
Stop and Read: Once your eye catches the keyword or information you want, stop scanning and read the sentence around it to confirm it's what you need.

COMPOSITION

Topic: Informal Letter – Letter to My Best Friend on My Plan for the Academic Session

Introduction

An informal letter, or personal letter, is a letter you write to someone you know well, such as a friend, sibling, or parent. The tone is casual and conversational, like you are talking to them.

Features and Format of an Informal Letter

An informal letter has a specific, though relaxed, format.

Writer's Address:
Written at the top-right corner.
Do not write your name here.
Example:

Government Secondary School, P.M.B. 102, Abuja, F.C.T.

Date:
Written just below your address.
Example: 6th September, 2025.
Salutation (Greeting):
Written on the left side, after skipping a line.
It is casual and ends with a comma.
Example: Dear Kunle, or Dearest Titi,
Body of the Letter:
This is the main message, organized into paragraphs.
Paragraph 1 (Introduction): Start with greetings, ask about your friend's well- being, and state the purpose of your letter.
Paragraph 2-4 (Main Points): Discuss the main topic. In this case, your plans for the new JSS3 session.
Paragraph 5 (Conclusion): Conclude your letter, send greetings to their family, and say you hope to hear from them.
Complimentary Close:
Written at the bottom, on the right side. It is followed by a comma.
Example: Yours sincerely, or Your friend, or Best wishes,
First Name:
Written just below the complimentary close.
Example: David (or your first name only).

LITERATURE IN ENGLISH

Topic: Introduction to Fiction & Non-Fiction

Introduction

Literature is the broad term for written works. All literature can be divided into two main categories: Fiction and Non-Fiction.

Fiction

Definition: Fiction is any creative work, chiefly in a narrative form, that describes events and people that are not real. It is based on the imagination of the author.
Purpose: To entertain, to explore ideas, to comment on the human condition through imaginary stories.
Keywords: Imaginary, "made-up," created.
Examples:
Novels: Things Fall Apart by Chinua Achebe
Short Stories: "The Lottery" by Shirley Jackson
Plays (Drama): The Lion and the Jewel by Wole Soyinka
Myths, Legends, Fables, Fairy Tales.

Non-Fiction

Definition: Non-fiction is any text or creative work that is based on facts, real events, and real people. The author claims to be writing the truth.
Purpose: To inform, to educate, to persuade, to explain, to describe.
Keywords: Factual, "real," informational, verifiable.

Key Differences (Fiction vs. Non-Fiction)

FeatureFICTIONNON-FICTIONBasisImaginationFact and RealityAuthor's IntentTo entertain, explore ideasTo inform, educate, persuadeCharactersImaginary (even if based on real people)Real PeopleEventsMade-upVerifiable, historicalTruthExplores emotional truth ("what if")Claims to be factually true
Examples and Types of Non-Fiction

This is a very broad category. Here are some of the most common types:

Biography:
A factual story of a real person's life written by someone else.
Example: A book about the life of Nelson Mandela, written by a historian.
Autobiography:
A factual story of a real person's life written by that person themselves. (Auto = self).
Example: Long Walk to Freedom by Nelson Mandela; Ake: The Years of Childhood by Wole Soyinka.
Memoir:
Similar to an autobiography, but it focuses on one specific period, event, or theme
in the author's life, not their entire life story.
Example: Becoming by Michelle Obama (focuses heavily on her time in the White House).
Textbooks:
Your New General Mathematics or Integrated Science books are non-fiction. Their purpose is to educate.
Essays:
A short written work on a specific subject (e.g., an argumentative essay on "The Importance of Education").
Journalism / News Reports:
Articles in newspapers or on websites that report on real, current events.
Diaries and Journals:
A personal, day-to-day account of a person's life.
Example: The Diary of a Young Girl by Anne Frank.
Speeches:
Written transcripts of famous public addresses (e.g., Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream" speech).

SPEECH WORK Topic: The Schwa Sound /ə/
Introduction

The schwa /ə/ is the most common vowel sound in the English language. It is a very short, "lazy," unstressed vowel sound. It is the sound you hear in:

about
computer
doctor
pencil

The schwa sound is never in a stressed syllable. This is why it's often called a "weak" vowel. Its symbol /ə/ looks like an upside-down 'e'.
Identifying the Schwa

The most confusing part of the schwa is that it can be represented by any vowel letter (a, e, i, o, u). You must listen for the sound, not look at the spelling.

'a' can be /ə/:
about, ago, arise, banana, salad
'e' can be /ə/:
telephone, problem, synthesis, mother
'i' can be /ə/:
pencil, easily, cousin
'o' can be /ə/:
computer, polite, lemon, condition
'u' can be /ə/:
support, success, album, focus

Topic: Expressing/Describing Emotions (Verb/Adjective + Preposition)

Introduction

In English, we often use specific prepositions to connect verbs or adjectives to the object of our feelings. Using the correct preposition is essential for sounding natural. These combinations are called collocations.

Common Emotion Collocations

Here is a list of common adjectives and verbs used to express emotions, followed by the prepositions they require.

about (used for a situation, topic, or event)
worried about: I am worried about the exam.

angry about: She is angry about the broken window.

of (used for the source of the fear or pride)

scared of: Are you scared of snakes?

READING & COMPREHENSION
Topic: Skimming for Specific Information

Clarification: Skimming vs. Scanning

In Week 1, we learned Scanning, which is searching for specific details (like a name or date). The scheme for this week says "Skimming for specific information." This is slightly confusing. The true definition of Skimming is reading quickly to get the general idea or gist of a passage.

We will focus on the true definition of SKIMMING.

What is Skimming?

Skimming is a high-speed reading technique used to get a general overview of a text. You are not reading every word, but looking for the main ideas.

Analogy: Skimming is like watching a 2-minute movie trailer. You don't see the whole movie, but you get a very good idea of what the story is about.

How to Skim Effectively

Read the Title and Subheadings: These tell you the main topic.
Read the Introduction: The first paragraph usually introduces the main idea.
Read the First Sentence of Other Paragraphs: This is the topic sentence, and it often summarizes the paragraph's main point.
Read the Conclusion: The last paragraph often summarizes the entire passage.
Look for Key Words: Let your eyes drift over the text, looking for bold words, names, dates, and keywords.

COMPOSITION
Topic: Writing to a Pen-pal

Introduction

A pen-pal (or pen-friend) is a person you get to know by sending letters or emails. Often, a pen- pal lives in another city or country, and you exchange information about your lives, cultures, and interests.

Writing to a pen-pal is a type of informal letter. Therefore, it must follow all the rules of informal letter writing we revised in Week 1.

Format (Revision)

Writer's Address (Top right)
Date (Below the address)
Salutation (Left side, e.g., Dear Kofi,)
Body (The main letter, in paragraphs)
Complimentary Close (Bottom right, e.g., Your friend,)
First Name (Below the close)

Content of a First Letter to a Pen-pal

Your first letter is very important. Its goal is to introduce yourself and ask questions to encourage a reply.

Paragraph 1: Introduction
Start with a friendly greeting.
Explain who you are and how you got their name (e.g., "My teacher, Mr. Bello, gave me your name as part of a school pen-pal program.")
Paragraph 2: About You
Give details about yourself: your age, your class (JSS3), and your family (e.g., "I am 14 years old and I am in the final year of Junior Secondary School. I have two brothers and one sister.")
Paragraph 3: Your Home and Hobbies
Describe your town or city and your school.
Talk about your hobbies and interests (e.g., "I live in Lagos, which is a very big and busy city in Nigeria. My school is large... My favourite hobbies are playing football and reading adventure novels.")
Paragraph 4: Ask Questions
This is very important! Ask your pen-pal questions about their life. This shows you are interested in them.
(e.g., "What is your country, Ghana, like? What is your school like? What are your hobbies? What is your favourite food?")
Paragraph 5: Conclusion
End the letter politely.
(e.g., "I have to stop here. I am looking forward to hearing from you. Please write soon.")

LITERATURE IN ENGLISH Topic: Poetry Analysis
Introduction
Poetry is a form of literature that uses the sound, rhythm, and meaning of language to create vivid images and express deep emotions. Unlike prose, poetry is often written in a compact form, using lines and stanzas.

Basic Elements of a Poem

To analyze a poem, we look at its building blocks:

Stanza: A stanza is a group of lines that form a unit in a poem. It is like a "paragraph" in prose.
Couplet: A stanza with 2 lines.
Tercet: A stanza with 3 lines.
Quatrain: A stanza with 4 lines (the most common).
Rhyme: The repetition of similar sounds at the end of lines.
Rhyme Scheme: The pattern of rhyme in a stanza. We use letters (A, B, C, D) to mark the rhyme scheme.

Poetic Devices (Figures of Speech)

Poets use figures of speech to make their writing more powerful and descriptive.

Simile: A comparison between two different things using the words "like" or "as".
Example: He is as tall as a giant. The moon shines like a diamond.
Metaphor: A direct comparison that states one thing is another thing (without using "like" or "as").
Example: He is a giant. The moon is a diamond.
Personification: Giving human qualities, feelings, or actions to an inanimate (non-living) object.
Example: The sun smiled at us. The angry wind shouted through the trees.

SPEECH WORK Topic: Stress and Intonation
Introduction

Stress and Intonation are the "music" of the English language. They convey meaning just as much as the words themselves.

Stress: The emphasis or "punch" given to a specific syllable in a word.
Intonation: The rise and fall of the voice when speaking (the "melody").

Word Stress

In English, not all syllables are pronounced with the same force. One syllable is always stressed (louder, longer, higher pitch).

One-Syllable Words: The whole word is stressed (e.g., CAT, RUN, FAST).
Two-Syllable Words:
Many nouns and adjectives stress the first syllable.
TEA-cher, TA-ble, PRE-sent, HAP-py
Many verbs stress the second syllable.
a-BOUT, be-GIN, ex-PORT, pre-SENT
Changing Stress: Sometimes, the stress changes the word's meaning and function.
RE-cord (Noun - a music disc) vs. re-CORD (Verb - to write down)
OB-ject (Noun - a thing) vs. ob-JECT (Verb - to disagree)

Intonation

Intonation is the "tune" of your voice. It changes the meaning of a sentence.

Falling Tone ( $\searrow$ )
The pitch of the voice falls at the end.
Use: For statements and commands.
It is raining. $\searrow$
My name is John. $\searrow$

Use: For "Wh-" questions (What, Where, When, Why, Who).
What is your name? $\searrow$
Where are you going? $\searrow$
Rising Tone ( $\nearrow$ )
The pitch of the voice rises at the end.
Use: For Yes/No questions (questions that can be answered with "yes" or "no").
Are you coming? $\nearrow$
Is that your book? $\nearrow$

GRAMMAR

Topic: Adverbs of Frequency

Introduction

An adverb modifies a verb, adjective, or another adverb. An Adverb of Frequency is a specific type of adverb that answers the question: "How often?"

The Scale of Frequency

We can place adverbs of frequency on a scale from 100% (all the time) to 0% (never).

100%: Always (e.g., I always brush my teeth at night.)
90%: Usually / Generally (e.g., I usually walk to school.)
70%: Often / Frequently (e.g., We often play football.)
50%: Sometimes (e.g., I sometimes eat rice for breakfast.)
30%: Occasionally (e.g., We occasionally visit our grandmother.)
10%: Seldom / Rarely (e.g., He rarely speaks in class.)
0%: Never (e.g., I have never been to London.)

Placement in a Sentence (The Rules)

The position of the adverb is very important.

Rule 1: Adverbs of frequency go BEFORE the main verb.

Subject + Adverb + Main Verb
Example:
She never <u>eats</u> meat. (NOT: She eats never meat.)
They often <u>watch</u> television.
I sometimes <u>forget</u> my pen.

Rule 2: Adverbs of frequency go AFTER the verb "to be" (am, is, are, was, were).

Subject + Verb 'to be' + Adverb
Example:
He is <u>always</u> late. (NOT: He always is late.)
The students are <u>usually</u> quiet.
I was <u>never</u> good at sports.

Topic: The Consonants /ʒ/ and /dʒ/

Introduction

This lesson focuses on two similar, voiced consonant sounds that often confuse learners:

/ʒ/ (Voiced Palato-alveolar Fricative): This is the "zh" sound. It is a continuous, buzzing sound, like the 's' in pleasure.
/dʒ/ (Voiced Palato-alveolar Affricate): This is the "j" or "soft g" sound. It is a stop sound that is released with a "zh" sound. It is not continuous.

Key Difference: /dʒ/ starts with a complete stop (like the /d/ sound), while /ʒ/ is a continuous, hissing sound.

Articulatory Description & Spelling Patterns
The Sound /ʒ/ (as in leisure)

How to make it: The tongue is held near the roof of the mouth (palate), and voiced air is pushed through the narrow gap, creating friction (a "zh" hiss).
Common Spellings:
's' + 'ion': vision, television, confusion, decision
's' + 'ure': pleasure, measure, treasure, leisure
'ge' (from French): garage, beige, prestige, massage

The Sound /dʒ/ (as in ledger)

How to make it: The tongue tip first touches the ridge behind the teeth (like 'd'), stopping the air. It is then released with friction (like 'ʒ'). It's a "d + zh" sound combined: /dʒ/.
Common Spellings:
'j': June, jam, joy, judge, major
'g' (before e, i, y): giant, Germany, gym, gentle, age
'dge': ledger, bridge, edge, badge

GRAMMAR

Topic: Changing Positive Statements to Negative (using "not")

Introduction

A positive statement (or affirmative statement) says that something is true.

Example: The boy is tall. She likes rice.

A negative statement says that something is not true. In English, we usually form this by adding the word "not".

Example: The boy is not tall. She does not like rice.
The Rules for Using "not"

The placement of "not" depends on the verb in the sentence.

Rule 1: Sentences with an Auxiliary (Helping) Verb This is the easiest rule. Auxiliary verbs include:

'to be': am, is, are, was, were
'to have': has, have, had
Modals: can, could, will, would, shall, should, must, may, might

Rule: Place "not" AFTER the auxiliary verb.

Positive: He is sleeping.
Negative: He is not sleeping. (or isn't)
Positive: They have finished.
Negative: They have not finished. (or haven't)
Positive: You must touch it.
Negative: You must not touch it. (or mustn't)

Rule 2: Sentences with NO Auxiliary Verb (Simple Present & Simple Past) This is the most important rule. If the sentence only has a main verb (like play, eats, went, saw), we must add an auxiliary verb to help. We add: do, does, or did.

Simple Present (with I, You, We, They): Add do not (don't)
Positive: They like school.
Negative: They do not (don't) like school.
Simple Present (with He, She, It): Add does not (doesn't)
Positive: He likes school.
Negative: He does not (doesn't) like school. (Note: the 's' on likes is removed).
Simple Past (All subjects): Add did not (didn't)
Positive: She went to the market.
Negative: She did not (didn't) go to the market. (Note: the past tense went
changes back to the present tense go).

Practice Exercise (Changing to Negative)

Negative: The students are not (aren't) in the hall.

Negative: Mr. Ade does not (doesn't) teach French.
Positive: I understand the question.

Positive: The dog stole the meat.
Negative: The dog did not (didn't) steal the meat.
Positive: We will be there.
Negative: We will not (won't) be there.

Topic: Expository Essay – How to Care for a Motor Vehicle

An Expository Essay is a piece of writing that "exposes" (explains), describes, or informs about a topic. It is factual, logical, and organized. It is not a story (narrative) and not an argument (argumentative).

The topic "How to..." requires you to write a process essay. You must explain the steps for doing something in a clear, logical order.

Features of an Expository "How-to" Essay

Clear Topic: The purpose is obvious from the start.
Logical Order: Steps are presented chronologically (first, next, finally).
Formal Tone: Use clear and direct language. Avoid slang ("...and stuff").
Transitional Words: Use words to guide the reader: First, Second, Next, Then, After that, Furthermore, Finally.
Factual Content: The information should be accurate and helpful.

Planning the Essay: "How to Care for a Motor Vehicle"

Hook: Start with a general statement (e.g., "A car is one of the most valuable possessions a person can have.")
Background: Explain that it needs care to last long and be safe.
Thesis Statement (Main Idea): State the main points you will discuss. (e.g., "Properly caring for a motor vehicle involves three essential areas: checking the fluids, maintaining the tires, and keeping it clean.")

Body Paragraph 2: Maintaining Tires
Topic Sentence: "Second, a driver must never ignore the tires."

LITERATURE IN ENGLISH

Topic: Prose – In-depth Analysis of Characterization, Theme, and Plot

Introduction

In Week 3, we introduced Plot, Character, and Theme. This week, we go deeper. These elements are the keys to unlocking the meaning of your recommended prose text.

Deeper Look 1: PLOT & CONFLICT

Plot: The sequence of events.
Conflict: The engine of the plot. Conflict is the problem that the characters must face. Without conflict, there is no story.
Types of Conflict:
Internal Conflict: Character vs. Self (a character struggling with a difficult decision, fear, or a weakness in their own mind).
External Conflict:
Character vs. Character (The hero vs. the villain).
Character vs. Nature (A character lost in a storm, or facing a wild animal).
Character vs. Society (A character fighting against the rules, laws, or traditions of their town).

Deeper Look 2: CHARACTERIZATION

Characterization: The way an author creates and develops a character.
Review:
Protagonist: Main character (hero).
Antagonist: Character who opposes the protagonist (villain).
New Concepts:
Direct Characterization: The author tells you the character's personality.
Example: "Tunde was a very lazy and selfish boy."
Indirect Characterization: The author shows you the character's personality through their...
Speech (What they say)
Thoughts (What they think)
Effect on others (How others react to them)
Actions (What they do)
Looks (How they dress or look)
Example: "Tunde lay on the sofa watching TV, ignoring his mother as she struggled to carry the heavy bag of water." (This shows us he is lazy and selfish, without using those words).
Types of Characters:
Round Character: Complex, multi-sided, feels real (like the protagonist).
Flat Character: Simple, one-sided (e.g., a "bad guy" who is just evil).
Dynamic Character: A character who changes their personality or attitude by the end of the story.
Static Character: A character who stays the same from beginning to end.

Deeper Look 3: THEME

Theme: The central message, idea, or truth about life that the story reveals.
Theme vs. Topic:
A Topic is one word (e.g., "Love," "War," "Greed").
A Theme is a full sentence about the topic (e.g., "True love can overcome any obstacle," "War destroys everyone," "Greed leads to a person's downfall").

GRAMMAR

Topic: Adjectives & Adverbs Expressing Willingness/Unwillingness

Introduction

Willingness is a state of being prepared, happy, or ready to do something. Unwillingness is a state of being reluctant, hesitant, or refusing to do something.

We can express these ideas using:

Modals: will, would, won't, wouldn't
Adjectives: eager, willing, reluctant, unwilling
Adverbs: readily, gladly, grudgingly, reluctantly

Expressing Willingness

Using Modals: The modal verb will (or would in the past/polite) shows readiness.

Example: "Don't worry, I will help you with the bags."
Example: "Would you help me, please?" (Asking about willingness)

Using Adjectives (Structure: to be + adjective + to-infinitive)

willing to: He is willing to work late.
eager to: The students are eager to learn.
happy to: I am happy to help.
prepared to: The school is prepared to fund the project.
ready to: We are ready to start.

Using Adverbs (Structure: modal + adverb + verb or verb + adverb)

gladly / cheerfully: I will gladly drive you. She cheerfully accepted the task.
readily: He readily shared his notes. (meaning: willingly and without hesitation)

Expressing Unwillingness

Using Modals: The negative modals won't (will not) and wouldn't (would not) are the strongest ways to show refusal.

Example: "He is stubborn. He won't listen to advice."
Example: "I asked him for help, but he wouldn't even look at me."

Using Adjectives (Structure: to be + adjective + to-infinitive)

unwilling to: The man is unwilling to pay for the damage.
reluctant to: She was reluctant to tell the teacher.
hesitant to: They are hesitant to join the club.

Using Adverbs (Structure: verb + adverb)

reluctantly: He reluctantly cleaned his room.
grudgingly: She grudgingly admitted she was wrong. (meaning: angrily and reluctantly)

READING & COMPREHENSION

Topic: Reading to Make Deductions from a Selected Passage

Introduction: What is a Deduction?
A deduction (or inference) is a conclusion you reach by using evidence and reasoning. In reading, it means "reading between the lines."

It is the answer to the question: "What is the author showing me, without telling me directly?"

Deduction Formula: What the Text SAYS (Clue) + What I already KNOW (Background Knowledge) = What I can DEDUCE (Inference)

Deduction vs. Literal Comprehension

Literal Question: What colour was the girl's dress?
Text: "The girl wore a red dress."
Answer: Red. (The answer is stated directly.)
Deductive Question: What was the weather like?
Text: "The girl's umbrella was useless against the howling wind, and her clothes were soaked to the skin before she even reached the bus stop."
Answer: It was raining heavily and very windy. (The text never says "rain" or "windy," but you can deduce it from "soaked" and "howling wind.")

COMPOSITION Topic: Guided Writing
Introduction

Guided Writing is a type of composition, common in examinations, where you are given a specific prompt that "guides" your writing. You must follow these instructions carefully to get full marks.

The prompt will tell you:

WHO you are (your role).
WHAT format to use (letter, speech, article, essay).
WHOM you are writing to (your audience).
WHICH specific points you must include.

Deconstructing a Guided Writing Prompt

Let's look at an example prompt:

"Your school's taps are broken, and the toilets are in a poor state. As the Senior Prefect, write a letter to the Principal. In your letter, you must: (a) state the problems clearly; (b) explain the effect these problems are having on the students (e.g., health, lateness); (c) suggest two practical solutions."

How to Plan Your Guided Writing

Identify Key Elements:

Role: Senior Prefect.
Format: A Letter (so it must be a Formal Letter).
Audience: The Principal.
Content Points:
(a) Describe broken taps/toilets.
(b) Explain the negative effects.
(c) Suggest two solutions.

Create an Outline:

Format: Start with the Formal Letter Format (Your address, The Principal's address, Date, Salutation "Dear Sir/Ma," Title).
Introduction: State your purpose for writing. "I am writing on behalf of the students to draw your attention to the poor state of the water and sanitation facilities in the school."
Body Paragraph 1 (Point a): Detail the problems. "The main tap at the JSS block has been broken for three weeks... The toilets in the senior block have no doors and no running water..."
Body Paragraph 2 (Point b): Explain the effects. "This situation is affecting students badly. Many students are late for class as they must walk to the staff quarters to fetch water. Furthermore, the unhygienic state of the toilets poses a serious health risk..."
Body Paragraph 3 (Point c): Suggest solutions. "We suggest that a special 'Sanitation Fee' be collected to hire a plumber immediately. We also suggest that two student volunteers be assigned to monitor the facilities each week."
Conclusion: End politely. "We hope you will give this urgent matter your attention. Thank you. Yours faithfully, [Signature], [Full Name], Senior Prefect."

Key Takeaway: The outline proves you have included every point from the guide.

Topic: Drama (Analysis of Recommended Text)

Introduction

Drama is a form of literature designed to be performed by actors on a stage. It is different from Prose (novels) because the story is told almost entirely through dialogue (what characters say) and action (what characters do).

The written text of a play is called a script.

Key Elements of Drama

Plot:
The sequence of events. In drama, the plot is structured into Acts (major divisions, like chapters) and Scenes (smaller divisions within an Act).
The plot is still driven by Conflict (Internal or External).
Characterization:
In a novel, the author can tell you what a character is thinking (direct characterization).
In a play, you learn about characters indirectly through:
Their Dialogue (what they say and how they say it).
Their Actions (what they do).
What other characters say about them.
Setting:
The time and place of the story.
In a script, this is described in the Stage Directions (the notes written in italics, often in parentheses).
Example: (The stage is dark. A single light on an old wooden chair. It is raining.)
Diction:
The author's choice of words. Diction is very important in drama.
Diction reveals a character's:
Education: (e.g., using big words vs. simple words).
Social Status: (e.g., speaking in formal language vs. slang).
Origin: (e.g., using a specific dialect or accent).
Emotional State: (e.g., speaking in short, sharp, angry sentences).
Rhyme Scheme (Note):
"Rhyme Scheme" (like AABB, ABAB) is a key element of Poetry, not drama.
Most modern plays (like your recommended text) are written in Prose (normal sentences).
However, some very old plays (like those by Shakespeare) are written in Verse (poetic lines). Even then, they don't always rhyme. This term is more important for poetry analysis.

Topic: Adverbs of Place and Manner
Introduction

An Adverb is a word that modifies (describes) a verb, an adjective, or another adverb. This week, we focus on two important types: Adverbs of Manner and Adverbs of Place.

Adverbs of Manner

Function: Answers the question "How?" or "In what way?"
Description: They describe how an action is performed.
Formation: Many are formed by adding -ly to an adjective (e.g., slow
$\rightarrow$ slowly).
Examples:
slowly, quickly, happily, sadly, loudly, quietly, angrily, carefully
Irregular: Some adverbs don't end in -ly.
well (The adjective is "good." He did a good job. He did the job well.)
fast (The adjective is also "fast." He is a fast runner. He runs fast.)
hard (The adjective is also "hard." He works hard.)
Sentence Placement: Usually placed after the verb or after the object.
Example 1: He spoke loudly.
Example 2: She closed the door gently.

Adverbs of Place

Function: Answers the question "Where?"
Description: They tell us the location of an action.
Examples:
here, there, everywhere, nowhere, somewhere
inside, outside, upstairs, downstairs, abroad, underground
Adverbial Phrases of Place: Sometimes a prepositional phrase functions as an adverb of place.
Example: He is sitting in the room. She went to the market.
Sentence Placement: Almost always placed after the verb or after the object.
Example 1: Please come inside.
Example 2: I left the keys on the table.

When Adverbs of Manner and Place are in the same sentence, there is a preferred order: Manner, Place, Time (MPT). The Adverb of Manner (How?) comes before the Adverb of Place (Where?).

Incorrect: He sat in the corner quietly. (This is awkward).
Correct: She sang beautifully (Manner) on the stage (Place).
Incorrect: She sang on the stage beautifully.

COMPOSITION

Topic: Debate – "Corruption is worse than Armed Robbery"

Introduction

A Debate is a formal, structured argument between two opposing sides on a specific topic, known as a motion or proposition. It is not a quarrel or a shouting match; it is a contest of ideas, logic, and persuasion.

The Motion: "Corruption is worse than Armed Robbery."
The Proposition (Affirmative): This side agrees with the motion. Their job is to prove that corruption is worse.
The Opposition (Negative): This side disagrees with the motion. Their job is to prove that armed robbery is worse (or that corruption is not as bad).

The Structure of a Debate Point (A.E.E.C.)

A strong debate point is not just a statement. It must be built logically. A good model is A.E.E.C.

Assertion: State your point (your "headline").
e.g., "My first point is that corruption is a silent killer, while armed robbery is a localized crime."
Explanation: Explain why this is true.
e.g., "This means that while an armed robber can only attack one family at a time, a corrupt official who steals money meant for a hospital can cause the deaths of thousands of people who can't get medicine."
Example: Give a clear, powerful example.
e.g., "When funds for a major highway are stolen, the result is a bad road. The accidents on that road kill far more people in one year than a single robber ever could."
Conclusion: Link your point back to the motion.
e.g., "Therefore, it is clear that the massive, hidden, and widespread damage of corruption is far worse than the immediate, visible crime of armed robbery."

In drama, the story is not just told with words (dialogue) but also with visual elements. The Stage Directions in a script tell the director and actors what the scene looks like and what to use. Two of the most important visual elements are costumes and props.

Costumes

Definition: Costumes are the clothes, accessories, and even makeup worn by an actor to portray a specific character.
Purpose: Costumes tell the audience essential information about the character before
they even speak.
Costumes reveal:
Time Period: A character wearing a 1960s "Afro" and "bell-bottom" trousers looks different from one in a modern suit.
Social Status: A king wears a crown and velvet; a servant wears simple, rough- spun clothes.
Occupation: A character in a white coat and stethoscope is a doctor. A character with a uniform and helmet is a police officer.
Personality: A character who is sad and mourning might wear all black. A vibrant, happy character might wear bright yellow.
Situation: A character in torn, dirty clothes is clearly in trouble.

Props (Properties)

Definition: A prop (short for "property") is any movable object that an actor uses or interacts with on stage. (Note: The fixed background, like the walls or a large sofa, is the Set.)
Examples: A teacup, a book, a letter, a knife, a walking stick, a telephone, a briefcase.
Purpose:
To advance the plot: A letter might contain crucial news. A hidden knife might be used in a fight. A ring might prove someone's identity.
To reveal character: A character who is always carrying a "holy book" is shown to be religious. A character who is always polishing a gun is shown to be violent.

Rhyme Schemes (Revisiting the Topic)

Clarification: As we have noted before, "Rhyme Scheme" (the pattern of rhymes at the end of lines, like AABB or ABAB) is a primary element of POETRY, not drama.
When is it used in Drama? Rhyme schemes only appear in a specific type of drama called Verse Drama or Poetic Drama.
Example: Many plays by William Shakespeare are written in poetic verse.
e.g., "My only love sprung from my only hate! (A)
Too early seen unknown, and known too late!" (A)
This is a rhyming couplet (AA). It is used to add emphasis.
Your Recommended Text: Most modern recommended texts (like The Successors or Trials of Brother Jero) are written in Prose (normal, everyday language). They do not have a rhyme scheme.

To make /tʃ/: This is an affricate.
First, the tongue tip touches the alveolar ridge (just behind the teeth), completely
stopping the air (like the /t/ sound).

It is a single, sharp sound: /t/ + /ʃ/ = /tʃ/ (like a train's "choo-choo").

Subject: Mathematics $\rightarrow$ The silence of deep concentration, the tap- tap-tap of the calculator, the teacher's voice explaining a theorem.

Subject: Mathematics $\rightarrow$ The smooth, cool surface of a protractor, the anxiety of a difficult test, the relief of finding the answer.
Subject: English $\rightarrow$ The rough pages of an old book, the sadness for a character, the excitement of a plot twist.

SPEECH WORK

Topic: The Consonants /w/ and /j/ (Semi-vowels)

Introduction

This lesson focuses on two unique voiced consonants known as semi-vowels or glides. They are called "semi-vowels" because they are produced with very little obstruction, almost like vowels, but they function as consonants in words.

/w/ (Voiced Labio-velar Glide): The "w" sound, as in wet, away, queen.
/j/ (Voiced Palatal Glide): The "y" sound, as in yes, use, onion.

NOTE: The symbol /j/ does not represent the "j" sound in "jam." That sound is /dʒ/. The symbol
/j/ always represents the "y" sound.

Articulatory Description
To make /w/:
Start by rounding your lips tightly (like you are about to say /u:/ as in "who").
Your tongue is high at the back (near the velum).
Quickly glide away from this position to the vowel sound that follows.

Examples: wet (glide from /w/ to /e/), win, wall.
To make /j/:

Start by raising the front/middle of your tongue high against the hard palate (like you are about to say /i:/ as in "see").
Your lips are spread (not rounded).
Quickly glide away from this position to the vowel sound that follows.

Examples: yes (glide from /j/ to /e/), yard, yell.

GRAMMAR

Topic: Adverbs of Cause/Reason (Adverbial Clauses)

Introduction

The scheme refers to these as "Adverbs of Cause/Reason." While traditional adverbs can show this (e.g., "He consequently left"), the examples so that and in order to function as subordinating conjunctions that introduce adverbial clauses of purpose or reason.

These phrases and clauses answer the question: "Why?"
Question: Why did you study hard?
Answer (Reason/Purpose):
because I wanted to pass.
in order to pass.
so that I could pass.

Common Phrases and Conjunctions

because / since / as (Show the Cause/Reason) These give the direct reason something happened.

Example: He failed because he did not read.
Example: Since you are here, you can help.
Example: As it was raining, we stayed indoors.

in order to / so as to (Show Purpose - Infinitive) These introduce the purpose of an action. They are followed by a base verb (e.g., pass, eat, see).

Structure: Action + in order to + Base Verb
Example: He studied hard in order to pass the exam.
Example: She woke up early in order to catch the bus.
Example: We must eat so as to live.

so that / in order that (Show Purpose - Clause) These also introduce purpose, but they are followed by a full clause (a subject + a modal verb like can, could, will, would).

Structure: Action + so that + Subject + Modal + Verb
Example: He studied hard so that he could pass the exam.
Example: She woke up early so that she would catch the bus.
Example: Give me the money so that I can buy the book.

Combining Sentences

Sentences: 1. I am saving money. 2. I want to buy a bicycle.
Combined (Reason): I am saving money because I want to buy a bicycle.
Combined (Purpose): I am saving money in order to buy a bicycle.
Combined (Purpose): I am saving money so that I can buy a bicycle.

READING & COMPREHENSION Topic: Identifying Topic Sentences in Passages
Introduction

A topic sentence is the single most important sentence in a paragraph. It states the main idea or central point of that paragraph. All other sentences in the paragraph (the "supporting sentences") must explain, describe, or give examples of the topic sentence.

Finding the topic sentence is the key to understanding a text quickly.
Where to Find the Topic Sentence

The topic sentence can be in one of three places:

At the Beginning (Most Common) The author states the main idea first and then gives examples.

(Topic Sentence) $\rightarrow$ The problem of waste in our major cities is becoming a chronic emergency. As urban populations grow, the volume of refuse is staggering. In many cities, plastic bags and bottles clog the drainage systems. This leads to severe flooding.

At the End (Less Common) The author gives several examples first, building up to a final conclusion (the main idea).

As populations grow, the volume of refuse is staggering. Plastic bags and bottles clog drainage systems, leading to severe flooding. Uncollected piles of refuse attract rats and spread diseases like Lassa fever. (Topic Sentence) $\rightarrow$ Clearly, the problem of waste in our major cities is a chronic emergency.

In the Middle (Least Common) The author starts with an introductory sentence, states the main idea, and then gives examples.

Many people are wondering what our biggest urban problem is. (Topic Sentence)
$\rightarrow$ The problem of waste in our major cities is becoming a chronic emergency.
For example, plastic bags and bottles clog drainage systems, leading to severe flooding.

Topic: The Consonants /t/ and /θ/

Introduction

This lesson focuses on two voiceless consonants that are often confused, leading to pronunciation errors. The difference is based on where you place your tongue.

/t/ (Voiceless Alveolar Stop): This is the sound for "t" in tin, tick, and pot.
/θ/ (Voiceless Dental Fricative): This is the "th" sound in thin, thick, and path.

Articulatory Description (How to Make the Sounds)

To make the /t/ sound:
Tongue: The tip of your tongue touches the alveolar ridge (the hard ridge just behind your top teeth).
Air: You stop the air completely for a moment, then release it in a small puff.
Vocal Cords: Do not vibrate (voiceless).
Practice: Say "t-t-t-t-t." Feel your tongue tapping behind your teeth.

To make the /θ/ sound:

Tongue: The tip of your tongue touches the bottom of your top front teeth, or is placed just between your top and bottom teeth.
Air: You push air continuously through the narrow gap between your tongue and teeth. This creates a hissing, fricative sound.
Vocal Cords: Do not vibrate (voiceless).
Practice: Say "th-th-th-th" (as in think). Feel the air hissing over your tongue and against your teeth.

Key Difference: /t/ is a stop sound (it stops). /θ/ is a fricative (it's continuous, like /s/ or /f/).

Topic: Revision of Parts of Speech

Introduction

The Parts of Speech are the categories we give to words based on their function in a sentence. A word's part of speech depends on how it is used. There are eight parts of speech.

The Eight Parts of Speech

Noun:

Definition: Names a person (Bello), place (Lagos), thing (book), or idea (love).
Functions: Can be the subject, object, or complement of a sentence.
Types: Proper (Nigeria), Common (country), Abstract (honesty), Concrete (table), Collective (flock).
Example: Ade kicked the ball in the park.

Pronoun:

Definition: Replaces a noun to avoid repetition.
Types: Personal (I, he, she, it, us, them), Possessive (my, his, our, theirs), Relative (who, which, that).
Example: Ade kicked it. He is a good player.

Verb:

Definition: Shows action (run, jump) or a state of being (is, are, feel, seem). It is the heart of the sentence.
Types: Transitive (needs an object - He kicked the ball), Intransitive (no object - He slept).
Example: The students wrote the test and felt happy.

Adjective:
Definition: Modifies (describes or limits) a noun or pronoun. It answers: What kind? How many? Which one?
Example: The tall boy bought three new books. She is smart.

Adverb:

Definition: Modifies a verb, an adjective, or another adverb. It answers: How? When? Where? How often? To what extent?
Example: He ran quickly (modifies ran). It is very hot (modifies hot).

Preposition:

Definition: Shows the relationship between a noun/pronoun and another word in the sentence (often showing location or time).
Example: The book is on the table, under the chair, near the window. He arrived at 5 p.m.

Conjunction:

Definition: Joins words, phrases, or clauses.
Types:
Coordinating (FANBOYS): For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So.
Subordinating: because, while, although, if, when.
Example: I like rice and beans, but I won't eat until the class ends.

Interjection:

Definition: An exclamatory word that shows strong, sudden emotion. It is grammatically separate from the sentence.
Example: Wow! That is a great score. Ouch! You stepped on my foot.

READING & LISTENING COMPREHENSION Topic: Reading for Critical Evaluation
Introduction

In JSS1 and JSS2, you learned to read for comprehension (understanding what the text says). In JSS3, you must learn to read for critical evaluation (judging what the text means and does).

Critical Evaluation is the process of thinking deeply about a text. You do not just accept the information; you question it, analyze it, and form your own judgments.

What is Critical Evaluation?

Critical evaluation involves:

Distinguishing Fact from Opinion:
Fact: A statement that can be proven true or false. Example: "Nigeria is a country in West Africa."
Opinion: A statement of belief, feeling, or judgment. It cannot be proven true or false. Example: "Nigeria is the best country in the world."
Identifying the Author's Purpose:
Why did the author write this?
To Inform: (e.g., a textbook, a news report).
To Persuade: (e.g., a debate, an advertisement, an editorial).
To Entertain: (e.g., a novel, a comic).
Detecting Bias:
Is the author presenting a one-sided view? Are they using emotionally loaded words to make you feel a certain way?
Example (Biased): "That foolish company is destroying our pure, beautiful forests."
Example (Unbiased): "The company is cutting down trees in the forest."
Judging Reliability:
Can you trust this information? Is the author an expert? Where was it published?

Topic: Review of Features of Folktales; Discussion of Selected Folktales

Introduction

A folktale is a story that has been passed down from one generation to another through oral tradition (word of mouth) before being written down. Folktales are a key part of orature (oral literature) and are found in every culture.

Key Features of Folktales

Oral Tradition: They were originally told, not read. This is why they are often simple, repetitive, and use memorable language.
Formulaic Opening/Closing: They often start and end with a set formula.
Opening: "Once upon a time..." or "A long time ago..."
Closing: "...and they lived happily ever after." or "This is my story..."
Stock Characters: The characters are often simple and represent one main quality. They are rarely complex.
The Trickster: A clever, cunning animal or person who uses their wits to trick others (e.g., the Tortoise, Anansi the Spider, the Hare).
The Hero: A brave (often young) person who goes on a quest.
The Villain: A simple "bad" character (e.g., the wicked stepmother, the greedy king, the hyena).
Magic and Supernatural Elements: Events happen that are not possible in real life (e.g., animals talk, magic pots, enchanted forests, ghosts, transformations).
A Clear Moral or Lesson: Folktales are meant to teach a lesson about good and bad behaviour, or to explain why something is the way it is (e.g., "Why the Tortoise's Shell is Cracked"). The moral is the most important part.
Simple Plot: The story is usually straightforward (Beginning, Middle, End) and easy to follow and remember.

Topic: Contrast Consonants /ʃ/ and /ʒ/

Introduction

This lesson focuses on two consonants that share the same place of articulation (palato-alveolar) but differ in their voicing.

/ʃ/ (Voiceless Palato-alveolar Fricative): This is the "sh" sound. It is voiceless (your vocal cords do not vibrate). It is the sound in ship, she, and mission.
/ʒ/ (Voiced Palato-alveolar Fricative): This is the "zh" sound. It is voiced (your vocal cords vibrate). It is the sound in pleasure, vision, and garage.

How to check for voicing: Place your fingers lightly on your throat (your voice box).

Say "shhhhhh" (/ʃ/). You should feel no vibration.
Say "zhhhhhh" (/ʒ/). You should feel a buzzing vibration.

Articulatory Description (How to Make the Sounds)

To make the /ʃ/ sound:

Tongue: The blade (middle-front) of your tongue is raised towards the hard palate (the roof of your mouth).
Lips: Your lips are often slightly rounded or pushed forward.
Air: You push air continuously through the narrow gap.
Vocal Cords: Do not vibrate.
Example words: shoe, wash, Chicago, nation, mission, pressure, sure.

To make the /ʒ/ sound:

Tongue: The tongue position is identical to /ʃ/.
Lips: The lip position is identical to /ʃ/.
Air: You push air continuously through the narrow gap.
Vocal Cords: You must vibrate your vocal cords.
This sound is less common in English and never starts a word (except for foreign words).
Example words: pleasure, measure, treasure, vision, decision, television, beige, garage, usual.

GRAMMAR

Topic: The Simple Sentence

Introduction

A sentence is a group of words that makes complete sense. It must contain a subject and a finite verb. A clause is a group of words containing a subject and a verb. There are three main types of sentences: Simple, Compound, and Complex. This week, we focus on the Simple Sentence.

Defining the Simple Sentence

A simple sentence consists of only one independent clause. An independent clause is a group of words that can stand on its own and make complete sense. The core of a simple sentence is one Subject (S) and one Finite Verb (V).

Example: The boy (S) cried (V).
Example: She (S) is (V) a doctor.
Example: Birds (S) fly (V).

Simple Sentences with Compound Parts

This is the most important concept to understand: A simple sentence can have more than one
subject or more than one verb, but it is still a simple sentence as long as it is one single clause.

Simple Sentence (SS) with Compound Subject (S + S):
Formula: S + S + V
Example: Bisi and Tunde (S+S) are reading (V).
(This is one clause. The subject is "Bisi and Tunde".)
Simple Sentence (SS) with Compound Verb (V + V):
Formula: S + V + V
Example: The man (S) laughed and jumped (V+V).
(This is one clause. The verb is "laughed and jumped".)
Simple Sentence (SS) with Compound Subject and Verb:
Formula: S + S + V + V
Example: The boys and girls (S+S) sang and danced (V+V).
(This is still a simple sentence because it is one independent clause.)

Simple Sentence Patterns

All simple sentences follow a few basic patterns:

S + V (Subject + Verb):
Example: The baby cried.
S + V + O (Subject + Verb + Object):
Example: The girl kicked the ball.
S + V + C (Subject + Verb + Complement): (The complement renames or describes the subject)
Example: He is a teacher. (teacher = He)
Example: She seems happy. (happy = She)
S + V + A (Subject + Verb + Adverbial): (The adverbial tells where or when)
Example: They went to the market.

What a Simple Sentence is NOT:

Compound Sentence: Two independent clauses joined by FANBOYS (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so).
Example: [The boy cried], but [the girl laughed]. (Two clauses)
Complex Sentence: One independent clause and one or more dependent clauses (joined by because, when, if, although, etc.).
Example: [The boy cried] because [he was hungry]. (One independent, one dependent)

Jaw: Your jaw is open wide.
Sound: It is a long, drawn-out sound: "aaaaah."
Common Spellings: ar (car, park, march), a (father, pass, bath), al (palm, calm), ear (heart).

Topic: The Compound Sentence

Introduction

In Week 3, we studied the Simple Sentence, which has only one independent clause (one subject-verb unit).

Example (Simple): The boy ran.
Example (Simple, compound subject): The boy and the girl ran.

This week, we study the Compound Sentence. A compound sentence is made of two or more independent clauses joined together. It has at least two separate subject-verb units.

Independent Clause 1: The boy sang. (This is a full sentence).
Independent Clause 2: The girl danced. (This is a full sentence).
Compound Sentence: The boy sang, **and** the girl danced.

How to Join Independent Clauses

You must use the correct "glue" to join two independent clauses.

Method 1: Coordinating Conjunctions (FANBOYS) This is the most common method. The "FANBOYS" are seven conjunctions.

For (shows reason)
And (adds information)
Nor (adds a negative)
But (shows contrast)
Or (shows a choice)
Yet (shows contrast, like 'but')
So (shows a result)
Punctuation Rule: You must use a comma (,) before the FANBOYS conjunction. Formula:
[Independent Clause 1] , [FANBOYS] [Independent Clause 2]

And: He is a good student, and he is also a talented artist.
But: I studied hard, but I failed the test.
Or: You can go to the market, or you can stay home.
So: She was tired, so she went to bed.
For: I went to sleep, for I was very tired. (Note: 'for' is formal, like 'because').

Method 2: The Semicolon ( ; ) You can use a semicolon to join two independent clauses that are very closely related.

Example: The man is a doctor; his wife is a lawyer.

Method 3: The Semicolon + Conjunctive Adverb

Example: He studied hard; **however,** he failed the test.

Simple vs. Compound (A common confusion)

Simple Sentence (with compound verb): Bisi sang **and** danced.
(One subject, "Bisi," does two things. This is ONE clause).
Compound Sentence: Bisi sang, **and** Tunde danced.
(Two subjects, "Bisi" and "Tunde," do two different things. This is TWO clauses).

READING & LISTENING COMPREHENSION Topic: Listening to Questions in Order to Answer Them
Introduction

This lesson focuses on a critical listening skill: understanding a question before you answer it. The way a question is asked (its structure and intonation) tells you exactly what kind of answer is expected.

If you misunderstand the question, you will give the wrong answer, even if you know the information.

Types of Questions (and How to Answer Them) Type 1: Yes/No Questions
How to recognize: They begin with an auxiliary verb (e.g., Is, Are, Was, Do, Did, Have, Can, Will).
Intonation: They have a rising tone (↗) at the end.
Expected Answer: "Yes" or "No," followed by a short explanation.
Example Question: "Did you eat breakfast this morning? (↗)"
Wrong Answer: "I ate rice."
Correct Answer: "Yes, I did. I ate rice." or "No, I did not."

Type 2: "Wh-" Questions (Information Questions)

How to recognize: They begin with an interrogative word (e.g., What, Where, When, Who, Why, How).
Intonation: They have a falling tone (↘) at the end.
Expected Answer: A specific piece of information.
Question: "What did you eat for breakfast? (↘)"
Wrong Answer: "Yes, I did."
Correct Answer: "I ate rice."
Breakdown of "Wh-" Words:

What? (Asks for a thing or idea): "What is that?"
Who? (Asks for a person): "Who is the principal?"
Where? (Asks for a place): "Where is your book?"
When? (Asks for a time): "When are you leaving?"
Why? (Asks for a reason): "Why were you late?"
How? (Asks for a manner/process): "How does this work?"

Type 3: Alternative Questions (Choice Questions)

How to recognize: They contain the word "or" and give a choice.
Intonation: The tone rises on the first choice and falls on the second.
Expected Answer: One of the choices.
Example Question: "Do you want tea (↗) or coffee (↘)?"
Wrong Answer: "Yes."
Correct Answer: "I would like coffee, please."

Introduction

This lesson is a practical application and review of the key elements of prose. Prose is literature written in ordinary, non-metrical language (unlike poetry). Your recommended novel is a work of prose. We will review the "Big Four" features and apply them to your text.

The Four Key Features of Prose Analysis

PLOT (What Happens?)
Definition: The sequence of events that make up the story.

Definition: The central idea, moral, or lesson about life that the author wants to share.
Key Element: Theme is a sentence, not one word.

Topic: The Nasal Sounds /m/, /n/, and /ŋ/

Introduction

This lesson focuses on the three nasal consonants in English. They are called "nasal" because when we produce them, the airflow is blocked from leaving the mouth and is released through the nose instead. All three are voiced, meaning your vocal cords vibrate.

/m/ (Voiced Bilabial Nasal): The sound in mentor, hammer, sum.
/n/ (Voiced Alveolar Nasal): The sound in notty, know, sun.
/ŋ/ (Voiced Velar Nasal): The "ng" sound, as in fling, sing, bank.

Articulatory Description (How to Make the Sounds)

The only difference between these three sounds is the place where the air is blocked in the mouth.
To make the /m/ sound:
Place: Bilabial (using both lips).
Action: Press your lips firmly together, blocking the air from leaving the mouth.
Air: Release the voiced air through your nose.
Practice: Say "m-m-m-m" and feel the vibration on your lips.
Examples: man, more, lamb, swim, hammer.
To make the /n/ sound:
Place: Alveolar (using the tongue-tip).
Action: Press the tip of your tongue against the alveolar ridge (the hard ridge just behind your top teeth).
Air: Release the voiced air through your nose.
Practice: Say "n-n-n-n" and feel the vibration on your tongue tip.
Examples: now, never, manner, know, knotty, sun.
To make the /ŋ/ sound (the 'ng' sound):
Place: Velar (using the back of the tongue).
Action: Raise the back of your tongue to press against the velum (the soft palate, at the back roof of your mouth).
Air: Release the voiced air through your nose.
Practice: Say "sing... sing... sing" and hold the final "ng" sound: "si-ŋ-ŋ-ŋ". Feel the sound vibrating at the back of your mouth, not the front.
Spelling Rule 1: Always found with the letters ng.
Examples: sing, long, fling, wrong, hanging.
Spelling Rule 2: Often found when n comes before k or g.
Examples: bank (/bæŋk/), think (/θɪŋk/), uncle (/ˈʌŋkl/), anger (/ˈæŋgə(r)/).

Topic: Punctuation Marks – Uses of Capital Letters

Introduction

Capital letters (also called "majuscules" or "upper case") are an essential punctuation mark. Their main purpose is to show the beginning of a sentence or to identify a proper, specific name. Using them incorrectly can cause confusion.

The Major Rules for Capitalization

The First Word of a Sentence:
This is the most important rule. Every new sentence must begin with a capital letter.
Example: The boy is here. Where is he going?
Proper Nouns (Specific Names):
People: Capitalize specific names of people.
Example: Ade, Musa, Mr. Okonjo, Bello.
Places: Specific cities, states, countries, continents, rivers, and mountains.
Example: Ibadan, Lagos State, Nigeria, Africa, River Niger.
Organizations: Specific schools, companies, teams.
Example: Government Secondary School, United Nations, Google.
Proper Adjectives:
Adjectives that are formed from proper nouns.
Example: I am a Nigerian. I speak English. I like African music.
Days, Months, and Holidays (But not Seasons):
Correct: Monday, January, December, Christmas, Eid el-Kabir.
Incorrect: Do not capitalize seasons: summer, winter, harmattan, rainy season.
The Pronoun "I":
The single-letter pronoun "I" is always capitalized, no matter where it is in a sentence.
Example: He said that I must go.
Titles of People (When used with their name):
Capitalize titles like Dr., Mrs., Mr., King, President, Principal when they come before a name.
Example: Principal Bello arrived. We spoke to Dr. Smith.
Note: Do not capitalize them when used generally: "He is a principal."
Titles of Works (Books, Movies, Poems):
Capitalize the first word, the last word, and all "important" words (nouns, verbs, adjectives).
Example: Things Fall Apart
Example: The Lion and the Jewel (Note the small 'a' and 't').
Acronyms and Initialisms:
Words formed from the first letters of other words.
Example: JSS (Junior Secondary School), ECOWAS, WAEC, CNN.
The First Word of a Direct Quotation:
If the quote is a full sentence.
Example: The teacher said, "Open your books to page 10."

READING & LISTENING COMPREHENSION Topic: Listening to Interpret Ideas in a Passage
Introduction

This skill goes beyond just understanding what was said (comprehension). Interpretation means figuring out the speaker's purpose, attitude, feelings, or opinion.

Comprehension: "What did the speaker say?" (The main gist/facts).

To interpret, you must listen for facts, opinions, tone of voice, and word choice (diction).

Purpose: Why is the person speaking?

Attitude/Tone: How does the speaker feel about the topic?

Sarcasm: When the tone makes the words mean the opposite of what they say.
Words: "Oh, another test. That's wonderful."
Sarcastic Tone: Low, slow, unenthusiastic.
Interpretation: The speaker is not happy about the test.
Word Choice (Diction): What words does the speaker use?
Neutral Words: "The government's new policy..."
Biased Words (Negative): "The government's disastrous, foolish, job-killing
policy..."
Biased Words (Positive): "The government's brilliant, life-saving, heroic
policy..."

Topic: Drama – Theme, Setting, Characterization in Recommended Drama Text

Introduction

This lesson is a focused review of the three most important elements of your recommended drama text: Theme, Setting, and Characterization. Unlike a novel (prose), a drama (play) reveals these elements visually and through dialogue.

Element 1: Theme

Definition: The theme is the central message, lesson, or idea about life that the playwright wants to communicate. It is the "Why?" of the play.
How to find it: A theme is not one word (like "love"). It is a full sentence (e.g., "Love can overcome family rivalry").
Application (Teacher-led):
What is the main topic of our play? (e.g., Power, Tradition, Greed, Love).
What is the playwright's message about this topic? (This is the theme).
Example: If the topic is "Power," is the theme "Power corrupts" or "Power is necessary for order"?

Element 2: Setting

Definition: The setting is the time (e.g., 1960s, 21st century) and place (e.g., a small village, a king's palace, a city) of the play.
How we know: The playwright tells us in the stage directions at the beginning of an act or scene. (e.g., [A dusty, poor room in a village. A single chair sits center stage. It is night.])
Importance:
Atmosphere (Mood): The setting creates the feeling (e.g., the poor room feels sad or tense).
Conflict: The setting can cause the problem. A play set in a royal palace will have problems of power and succession. A play set in a slum will have problems of poverty and survival.

Element 3: Characterization

Definition: How the playwright creates and reveals the characters.
How we know: In drama, we rely on indirect characterization:
Dialogue: What the character says (their Diction). Are they polite? Rude? Educated?
Action: What the character does (e.g., helps someone, steals, lies).
Reaction: What other characters say about them or how they react to them.
Review:

Protagonist: The main character, who the audience follows.
Antagonist: The character or force that opposes the protagonist.
Dynamic Character: A character who changes or learns a lesson by the end.
Static Character: A character who stays the same.

Topic: Reflexive and Relative Pronouns

PART 1: REFLEXIVE PRONOUNS

Definition: A Reflexive Pronoun is a pronoun that ends in -self (singular) or -selves (plural). It is used when the subject and the object of a verb are the same person or thing.

List of Reflexive Pronouns:

Singular: myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself
Plural: ourselves, yourselves (for more than one 'you'), themselves

Functions:

Function 1: As an Object (Reflexive Use)
This is used when the subject performs an action to itself.
Example: I saw myself in the mirror. (Subject = I, Object = myself)
Example: He accidentally cut himself.
Example: The cat is washing itself.
Example: They fooled themselves.
Common Error: Incorrect: "Please call my wife and myself." Correct: "Please call my wife and me." (Use "myself" only if "I" is the subject).
Function 2: As an Emphatic Pronoun (Intensive Use)
This is used to add emphasis to a noun or pronoun. It means "that person/thing and nobody else."
Example: The principal himself came to our class. (This emphasizes "the principal.")
Example: I will do it myself. (This means "I will do it personally, without help.")

PART 2: RELATIVE PRONOUNS
Definition: A Relative Pronoun is a pronoun that introduces a dependent clause (called a relative clause) and connects it to a noun that came before it. It gives us more information about that noun.

List and Usage:

who: Refers to people (as the subject).
Example: That is the man who stole the car. (The relative clause "who stole the car" describes "the man.")
whom: Refers to people (as the object). It is formal and often replaced by "who."
Example: That is the man whom the police arrested. (The police arrested him.)
whose: Refers to possession (for people, animals, or things).
Example: He is the boy whose book was stolen.
which: Refers to animals or things.
Example: This is the book which I bought yesterday.
Example: The car, which is red, belongs to my father.
that: Refers to people, animals, or things. (It is less formal than who or which).
Example: He is the man that lives next door.
Example: This is the book that I bought.

GRAMMAR

Topic: Personal Pronouns – Rules of Usage (Case)

Introduction

Personal Pronouns are words that replace nouns referring to people or things (e.g., I, me, he, him, they, them). A common error is using the wrong form (or case) of a pronoun. The case of a pronoun changes based on its grammatical job in the sentence.

There are three main cases: Subjective, Objective, and Possessive.

Rule 1: The Subjective Case (Nominative)

Pronouns: I, you, he, she, it, we, they
Rule: Use the subjective case when the pronoun is the SUBJECT of the verb (the person or thing doing the action).
Examples:
He is a teacher. (Who is a teacher? He is.)
They went to the market. (Who went? They did.)
Common Error (Compound Subjects):
Incorrect: "My friend and me are in the same class."
Correction: To check, remove the other person: "Me am in the same class." (Wrong). "I am in the same class." (Right).
Correct: "My friend and I are in the same class."

Rule 2: The Objective Case (Accusative)

Pronouns: me, you, him, her, it, us, them
Rule (a): Use the objective case when the pronoun is the OBJECT of the verb (the person or thing receiving the action).
Example: The principal saw him. (The principal saw who? Him.)
Example: The dog followed us.
Rule (b): Use the objective case after a PREPOSITION (e.g., to, for, with, between, at, on).
Example: The gift is for her.
Example: Please give the book to me.
Common Error (Compound Objects):
Incorrect: "Between you and I, this is a secret."
Correct: "Between you and me, this is a secret." (Because "between" is a preposition).
Incorrect: "The teacher punished Tunde and I."
Correct: "The teacher punished Tunde and me." (Tunde and me are the objects).

Rule 3: The Possessive Case

This case shows ownership. It has two forms:

Possessive Adjectives:
Words: my, your, his, her, its, our, their
Rule: These words MUST be followed by a noun.
Example: That is my book. This is our class.
Possessive Pronouns:
Words: mine, yours, his, hers, its, ours, theirs
Rule: These words stand ALONE. They replace the noun.
Example: That book is mine. The class is ours.

Common Error (Apostrophes):
Incorrect: The book is our's. The dog wagged it's tail.
Correct: The book is ours. The dog wagged its tail. (Note: It's = "It is").

Introduction

An Expository Essay is a formal piece of writing that explains, informs, and describes a topic. It is not a story (narrative) or an argument (debate), but a factual analysis. The topic "Drug Addiction" is a serious social issue. Your essay should be structured, formal, and objective.

Clear Thesis Statement: A sentence in the introduction that states the main idea of the entire essay.
Logical Structure: Organized into paragraphs, each with a clear topic sentence.
Factual Evidence: Uses facts, examples, and logical reasoning (not personal, emotional stories).
Formal Tone: No slang. The language is serious and academic.
Clear Transitions: Uses words like First, Furthermore, However, In conclusion.

Planning the Essay: "Drug Addiction"

Title: Drug Addiction: A Growing Threat to Our Youth

Hook: Start with a powerful general statement. (e.g., "Every day, the futures of thousands of young people are destroyed by a powerful and dangerous enemy.")

Thesis Statement: State the main points your essay will cover. (e.g., "To tackle this problem, it is essential to understand its primary causes, its devastating effects, and its potential solutions.")
Body Paragraph 1: The Causes of Drug Addiction
Topic Sentence: "There are several reasons why young people fall into the trap of drug addiction."
Examples:
Peer Pressure: The desire to fit in with a particular group.

Topic: Poetry – Language in Poetry; Construct Sentences with Irony and Hyperbole

Introduction

Poetry uses language in a much more compressed and powerful way than prose. Poets use figurative language (or poetic devices) to create vivid pictures and deep meanings in the mind of the reader. This lesson focuses on two powerful devices: Hyperbole (exaggeration) and Irony (contradiction).

Hyperbole (Hi-PER-bo-lee)

Definition: Hyperbole is a deliberate, extreme exaggeration used for emphasis or to make a point. It is not meant to be taken literally.
Purpose: To be funny, to show strong emotion, or to emphasize a quality.
Examples in Everyday Speech:
"I'm so hungry I could eat a horse." (Meaning: I am extremely hungry.)
"My school bag weighs a ton." (Meaning: It is very heavy.)
"I've told you a million times not to do that." (Meaning: I have told you many times.)
Constructing Sentences with Hyperbole:
To describe being tired: "I'm so tired, I could sleep for a year."
To describe being fast: "She ran faster than the speed of light."
To describe sadness: "He cried a river of tears."

Irony

Definition: Irony is a figure of speech where there is a strong contrast between what is said and what is actually meant, or between what is expected to happen and what does happen.
There are three main types:

Verbal Irony (Sarcasm):
Saying the opposite of what you mean. The tone of voice is often the biggest clue.
Example: Walking out into a heavy rainstorm and saying, "What lovely weather we're having."
Example: Looking at a very messy room and saying, "Wow, you are so clean."
Situational Irony:
When the opposite of what is expected to happen, happens. The outcome is a surprise that is often a cruel twist of fate.
Example: "A fire station burns down." (You expect it to be the safest place from fire.)
Example: "A police officer gets arrested for theft." (You expect them to prevent, not commit, crime.)
Example: "The man who invented the 'Stop' sign was killed in a car accident."
Dramatic Irony: (Mostly in plays/stories)
When the audience knows a secret or a truth that one or more of the characters on stage do not know.

Topic: Demonstrative Pronouns

Introduction

Demonstrative Pronouns are words that point to specific people or things. They "demonstrate" which noun you are talking about. They answer the question: Which one(s)?

The four demonstrative pronouns are: this, that, these, and those.

The Rules of Usage (Number & Proximity)

Demonstratives are used based on two simple rules:

Number: Is the noun singular (one) or plural (more than one)?
Proximity (Distance): Is the noun near (here) or far (there)?

We can see this in a simple chart:

NEAR (Here)FAR (There)Singular (one)thisthatPlural (many)thesethose

Demonstratives can be used in two different ways: as pronouns or as adjectives (determiners).

"This pen is mine."
"That movie was great."
"These answers are correct."
"I will buy those shoes."

READING & LISTENING COMPREHENSION Topic: Reading to Summarize Paragraphs
Introduction

A summary is a brief statement that covers the main points of a text. It is a "short version" of the original. Summarizing is a skill that combines:

Skimming (to find the main idea).
Identifying the Topic Sentence (to know the paragraph's focus).
Rewriting in your own words.

A good summary is short, clear, accurate, and in your own words.

How to Summarize a Paragraph (Step-by-Step)

Read: Read the entire paragraph carefully to understand its full meaning.
Identify: Find the topic sentence (the main idea, usually the first or last sentence).

Omit: Leave out (omit) minor details, examples, repetitions, and descriptive language.
Rewrite: Combine the main idea and key supporting details into one or two new sentences, using your own words.

Near: this, these
Far: that, those
Number (Quantity):
Singular: this, that
Plural: these, those

The Core Difference: Function

The word's function depends entirely on its job in the sentence. Function 1: Demonstrative Pronoun
Job: It replaces a noun. It stands alone as the subject or object.
Formula: Demonstrative + Verb (e.g., This is...) OR Verb + Demonstrative (e.g., I want that.)

Formula: Demonstrative + Noun
Examples:
"This book is mine." (Here, "This" modifies the noun "book.")
"That smell is wonderful." (Here, "That" modifies the noun "smell.")
"I will buy these shoes." (Here, "these" modifies the noun "shoes.")
"Those friends are yours." (Here, "Those" modifies the noun "friends.")

Formal Tone: No slang. The language is serious and academic.
Clear Transitions: Uses words like First, Furthermore, However, In conclusion.

Topic Sentence: "There are two main causes of this rapid population growth."
Point 1 (Decreased Death Rate): Explain that advances in modern medicine, better sanitation, and increased food production mean people are living longer (lower infant mortality, higher life expectancy).
Point 2 (High Birth Rate): Explain that in many cultures, there is a preference for large families, or a lack of access to education and family planning.

Topic Sentence: "The most immediate consequence of this explosion is the immense strain on our environment and social services."

Point 2 (Services): There is too much pressure on hospitals, schools, and housing, leading to overcrowded classrooms and slums.

A Monophthong (from Greek mono, meaning "one," and phthong, meaning "sound") is a pure vowel sound. The sound has a single, unchanging quality. The 12 monophthongs are divided into short vowels and long vowels.

GRAMMAR (ENGLISH STRUCTURE) Topic: Review of Nouns and Pronouns
Introduction

This lesson revises the first two parts of speech, which are the fundamental "naming words" that anchor a sentence: Nouns and Pronouns.

In-Depth Review of Nouns

A Noun is a word that names a person, animal, place, thing, or idea.

Types of Nouns:

Proper Nouns: Specific names of people, places, or organizations. They are always capitalized.
Examples: Bello, Lagos, Nigeria, Monday, Google.
Common Nouns: General names for a class of people, places, or things.
Examples: boy, city, country, day, company.
Abstract Nouns: Names of ideas, qualities, feelings, or concepts. You cannot see or touch them.
Examples: love, honesty, fear, bravery, time, wisdom.
Concrete Nouns: Names of things you can experience with your five senses (see, touch, smell, hear, taste).
Examples: table, smoke, music, perfume, book.
Collective Nouns: A singular name for a group of individuals.
Examples: flock (of sheep), army (of soldiers), committee, family, team.
Countable Nouns: Nouns that can be counted and have a plural form.
Examples: one book, two books; one man, two men.
Uncountable (Mass) Nouns: Nouns that cannot be counted and usually have no plural form.
Examples: water, rice, information, advice, luggage, furniture.
Functions of Nouns in a Sentence:

Subject of a Verb: The person/thing doing the action.
Example: The teacher arrived.
Object of a Verb: The person/thing receiving the action.
Example: I saw the teacher.
Subject Complement: Renames the subject after a linking verb (is, are, was, seem, become).
Example: Mr. Ade is a teacher. (Mr. Ade = a teacher)
Object of a Preposition: Follows a preposition (in, on, at, for, with).
Example: I gave the book to the teacher.

In-Depth Review of Pronouns

A Pronoun is a word used in place of a noun to avoid repetition.

Personal Pronouns (Subjective & Objective Case):

Subjective Case: Used as the subject of a verb. (I, you, he, she, it, we, they)
Example: She went to the market.
Objective Case: Used as the object of a verb or preposition. (me, you, him, her, it, us, them)
Example: The teacher saw her. He gave the book to me.
Rule: When you have a compound subject, always use the subjective case.
Incorrect: "My friend and me are in JSS3."
Correct: "My friend and I are in JSS3." (Test: "I am in JSS3." not "Me am in JSS3.")

Possessive Pronouns: (Show ownership)

Possessive Adjectives: Come before a noun. (my, your, his, her, its, our, their)
Example: That is my book.
Possessive Pronouns: Stand alone and replace the noun. (mine, yours, his, hers, its, ours, theirs)
Example: That book is mine.
Rule: it's (with an apostrophe) means "it is." its (no apostrophe) is the possessive pronoun.

Reflexive & Emphatic Pronouns: (myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves)

Reflexive: The subject performs an action on itself. The pronoun is the object.
Example: He cut himself.
Emphatic: Used for emphasis only. The sentence still makes sense without it.
Example: The principal himself came to our class.

Relative Pronouns: (Introduce a relative clause to describe a noun)

who/whom: For people. ("The man who came...")
which: For things/animals. ("The book which I read...")
whose: For possession. ("The boy whose bag was stolen...")
that: For people or things.

Topic: Review of Narrative and Descriptive Essays

Introduction

This lesson revises two important essay types. While they are often mixed, they have different primary goals.

A Narrative Essay TELLS A STORY. Its main goal is to show a sequence of events (Plot).
A Descriptive Essay PAINTS A PICTURE. Its main goal is to make the reader

In-Depth Review of Narrative Essays

Purpose: To tell a story (real or imagined) that has a point or teaches a lesson.
Key Elements (The "Story"):
Plot: The most important element. It must have:
Beginning (Exposition): Introduce characters and setting.
Middle (Rising Action & Climax): The main problem or event happens.

In-Depth Review of Descriptive Essays

Purpose: To describe a person, place, object, or feeling so vividly that the reader can see, hear, smell, feel, and taste it.
Key Element (The "Picture"):
Sensory Details (The 5 Senses): This is the most important part.
Sight: What did it look like? (e.g., "the bright red, peeling paint")
Sound: What did it sound like? (e.g., "the high-pitched, endless scraping")
Smell: What did it smell like? (e.g., "the sharp, tangy smell of salt and fish")
Touch/Feel: What did it feel like? (e.g., "the rough, cracked surface")
Taste: (If applicable) (e.g., "the bitter, metallic taste of the medicine")

This is the key to both essay types, especially descriptive.

Telling: "The man was angry."

Topic: Review of Major/Minor Characters, Theme, Plot, Tragic/Comic Elements, Diction

Introduction

This is a comprehensive review of the advanced terms used to analyze literature (prose, drama, and poetry). As JSS3 students, you must be able to use these terms to explain how a story works.

In-Depth Review of Literary Terms

Plot (Revision):

The sequence of events.
Exposition (Start), Rising Action (Tension builds), Climax (Peak of tension), Falling Action, Resolution (End).

Characterization (Major vs. Minor):

Major Characters: The story revolves around them.
Protagonist: The main character, who the audience roots for.
Antagonist: The character or force that opposes the protagonist.
Minor Characters: They support the story but are not the main focus. They are there to help or hinder the protagonist, or to reveal more about the major characters.
Review:
Dynamic: A character who changes significantly (in personality or belief) by the end.
Static: A character who does not change.
Round: A complex, believable character with many traits.
Flat: A simple, one-dimensional character (e.g., the "bully").

Theme (Revision):

The central message, moral, or idea about life that the author wants to convey.
Theme is a full sentence, not one word.
Topic: "Greed"
Theme: "The author shows that greed ultimately leads to self-destruction."

Tragic and Comic Elements (Genre):

Tragedy: A serious, dignified story where the protagonist (who is often a good person) faces a great struggle. The story ends in disaster (often death) for the protagonist, usually because of their own Tragic Flaw (a weakness like pride, ambition, or jealousy).
o Example: A king who is so proud he refuses to listen to advice, and as a result, loses his kingdom and his life.
Comedy: A story with a happy, or at least a positive, ending. It is not always "funny," but it is not tragic. The protagonist overcomes the obstacles. In classic comedy, the story often ends with a celebration, like a wedding.
Example: A story about two people who misunderstand each other, get into arguments, but finally resolve their differences and become friends or get married.

Diction:

Definition: The author's or playwright's specific choice of words.
Purpose: Diction is the tool used to build everything else.
Formal Diction: Using complex, serious, and professional language. (e.g., "We must endeavour to ascertain the truth.")
Informal Diction (Colloquial): Using casual, everyday language or slang. (e.g., "We have to find out what's going on.")
Function in Literature:
Reveals Character: An educated professor speaks with formal diction, while a market seller might use colloquial diction.
Sets Tone/Mood: Using dark, heavy, and sad words (e.g., "gloomy," "despair," "shattered") creates a tragic or sad mood.

What is a Diphthong? A diphthong is a single vowel sound that is formed by the combination of two different vowel sounds. It starts with one vowel sound and "glides" into another within the same syllable. There are eight common diphthongs in English.

/eɪ/ (as in "say")

/ɪə/ (as in "near")

/eə/ (as in "hair")

/ʊə/ (as in "pure")

GRAMMAR: REVIEW OF VERBS AND ADVERBS

Verbs

Definition: A verb is a word or group of words that expresses an action (e.g., run, eat, think) or a state of being (e.g., is, are, was, feel, seem). It is the most important part of a sentence.

Types of Verbs:

Main Verbs (Lexical Verbs): These carry the main meaning of the sentence.
Transitive Verbs: Require a direct object to complete their meaning. (e.g., "She
wrote a letter." The object is "a letter".)
Intransitive Verbs: Do not require an object. (e.g., "He slept." "The bird sings.")
Auxiliary Verbs (Helping Verbs): These help the main verb to form tenses, moods, or voices.
Primary Auxiliaries: be, have, do (e.g., "He is running." "She has eaten." "Do you understand?")
Modal Auxiliaries: Express ability, permission, possibility, necessity, etc. (e.g.,
can, could, will, would, shall, should, may, might, must)

Verb Forms:

Finite Verbs: Show tense (past/present) and agree with a subject. (e.g., "He walks." "They walked.")
Non-Finite Verbs: Do not show tense and do not agree with a subject.
Infinitives: The base form, often with 'to'. (e.g., "I want to eat.")
Gerunds: The '-ing' form of a verb used as a noun. (e.g., "Swimming is good exercise.")
Participles: Used as adjectives or to form tenses.
Present Participle: (-ing) "The running boy fell."
Past Participle: (-ed, -en) "The broken chair is mine."
Adverbs

Definition: An adverb is a word that modifies (describes or gives more information about) a verb, an adjective, or another adverb. It answers questions like How? When? Where? How often? To what extent?

Types of Adverbs:

Adverbs of Manner: (How?) e.g., slowly, quickly, carefully, well, badly
"He drove carefully."
Adverbs of Place: (Where?) e.g., here, there, everywhere, outside, in
"Please wait here."
Adverbs of Time: (When?) e.g., now, then, today, yesterday, soon
"She will arrive soon."
Adverbs of Frequency: (How often?) e.g., always, often, sometimes, never, rarely
"They often visit us."
Adverbs of Degree: (To what extent?) e.g., very, extremely, quite, too, almost
"The food is very hot." (Modifies adjective 'hot')
"He ran too quickly." (Modifies adverb 'quickly')

Position of Adverbs: The position of an adverb in a sentence can vary, but common patterns include:

Manner: Usually after the verb or object. ("He speaks English well.")
Frequency: Usually before the main verb but after 'be'. ("He rarely complains." "He is
rarely late.")
Time: Usually at the beginning or end of a clause. ("Today, we are going out." "We are going out today.")

COMPOSITION: REVIEW OF ARGUMENTATIVE & EXPOSITORY ESSAYS

Argumentative Essay

Definition: An argumentative essay is a type of writing that aims to persuade the reader to accept a particular point of view (your "argument" or "thesis"). It uses logic, evidence, and reasoning to support this viewpoint and may also address opposing viewpoints.

Key Features:

A Clear Thesis Statement: A single sentence in the introduction that states your main argument. (e.g., "Mobile phones should be banned in secondary schools because they are a distraction, promote cheating, and hinder social interaction.")
Logical Arguments: Each body paragraph presents a single, clear reason supporting your thesis.
Evidence: You must support your arguments with facts, examples, statistics, or expert opinions.
Counter-Argument (Optional but strong): Acknowledging and refuting the opposing view shows you have considered all sides.
Formal Tone: Use formal language. Avoid slang or casual expressions.
Strong Conclusion: Summarise your points and restate your thesis in a compelling way.

Structure:

Introduction: Grab the reader's attention, provide brief background, and state your thesis.
Body Paragraphs (2-3):
Start with a topic sentence (your reason).
Explain your reason.
Provide evidence/examples.
(Optional) Counter-Argument Paragraph: State the opposing view and then show why it is weak or wrong.
Conclusion: Restate your thesis, summarise your main points, and leave the reader with a strong final thought.
Expository Essay

Definition: An expository essay is a type of writing that aims to explain, describe, define, or inform (to "expose") a topic. It is not about arguing a point but about presenting information clearly and objectively.

Key Features:

Clear Topic: The essay focuses on one central topic.
Factual Information: The content is based on facts, not personal opinions or emotions.
Logical Organisation: Information is presented in a clear, easy-to-follow order.
Objective Tone: The writer's feelings are kept out of the essay.
Types: Expository essays can:
Define: Explain the meaning of a concept (e.g., "What is Democracy?").
Compare & Contrast: Show similarities and differences (e.g., "City Life vs. Village Life").
Cause & Effect: Explain the reasons for and results of an event (e.g., "The Causes of Deforestation").
Process ("How-to"): Explain how to do something step-by-step (e.g., "How to Prepare Gari").

Structure:

Introduction: Introduce the topic and state the main idea (what you will be explaining).
Body Paragraphs (2-3): Each paragraph explains one aspect of the topic, using facts, details, and examples.
Conclusion: Summarise the main points of the explanation.

LITERATURE: REVIEW OF LITERARY TERMS IN POETRY

Introduction: Poets use special tools called literary devices (or figurative language) to make their poems more vivid, meaningful, and memorable.

Key Literary Terms:

Simile: A direct comparison between two unlike things, using the words "like" or "as".
Example: "He runs as fast as the wind." "Her smile was like sunshine."
Metaphor: An indirect or implied comparison between two unlike things, stating that one thing is the other. It does not use "like" or "as".
Example: "He is a lion in battle." "Her smile is sunshine."
Personification: Giving human qualities, feelings, or actions to inanimate objects, animals, or ideas.
Example: "The wind whispered through the trees." "My alarm clock shouted at me."

Example: "She sells seashells by the seashore." (Note: 'c' in 'cells' would not be alliteration here as it makes an /s/ sound).
Assonance: The repetition of vowel sounds within words that are close together.
Example: "The rain in Spain stays mainly on the plain." (Repetition of the /eɪ/ sound).
Irony: A contrast between what is said and what is meant, or between what is expected and what actually happens.
Verbal Irony: Saying the opposite of what you mean (sarcasm is a form of this). (e.g., Looking at a messy room and saying, "You are so tidy!")
Situational Irony: When the opposite of what is expected happens. (e.g., A fire station burns down.)
Dramatic Irony: When the audience knows something that a character in the story does not.
Onomatopoeia: The use of words that imitate the sound they describe.
Example: buzz, hiss, clang, moo, splash, boom
Rhyme: The repetition of similar sounds at the end of lines in a poem.
Example: "The cat in the hat / Sat on the mat."
Rhyme Scheme: The pattern of rhymes, labelled with letters (e.g., AABB, ABAB).
Rhythm: The pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables in a line of poetry, creating a "beat".

COMPOSITION: REVISIT INFORMAL AND FORMAL LETTERS

Introduction: Letter writing is a way of communicating with someone in writing. The two main types are informal (friendly) and formal (official).

Informal Letter

Purpose: Written to people you know well, such as family members, friends, and close relatives.
Tone: Casual, friendly, personal, and conversational. You can use contractions (e.g., I'm, don't), slang, and jokes.
Features/Format:
Your Address: Top right corner.
Date: Directly below your address.
Salutation: (e.g., Dear Dad, Dearest Funke, My dear friend,)
Body: The main message, usually in paragraphs.
Closing: (e.g., Yours, Your friend, Your loving son,)
Your First Name: (e.g., Tunde)

Formal Letter

Purpose: Written for official or business reasons. (e.g., a letter to a bank, a job application, a letter of complaint, a letter to a government official).
Tone: Formal, polite, respectful, and straight to the point (concise). You must not use contractions or slang.
Features/Format:
Your Address: Top right corner.
Date: Directly below your address.
Recipient's Address: On the left side, below your date. (Includes their title, name, and full address).
Salutation:
If you know the name: Dear Mr. Okoro, Dear Mrs. Bello, Dear Dr. Smith,
If you don't know the name: Dear Sir, Dear Madam, Dear Sir/Madam,
Title/Subject Line (Optional but recommended): A brief heading that states the letter's purpose (e.g., Application for the Post of Library Prefect).
Body: The main message. Be clear and direct.
Closing: (e.g., Yours faithfully, (if you used "Dear Sir/Madam") or Yours sincerely, (if you used their name)).
Your Signature: Your handwritten signature.
Your Full Name: Printed clearly below your signature.

Review of Diphthongs (Gliding Vowels)

A diphthong is a single vowel sound that involves a glide from one vowel position to another within the same syllable.

/eɪ/ as in: pain, date, say, great

/ɔɪ/ as in: boy, noise, coin

Review of Consonant Sounds

Consonants are sounds made with a partial or complete obstruction of airflow. They are classified by voicing (voiced or voiceless) and manner of articulation.

Plosives (Stops):
/p/ (voiceless) & /b/ (voiced) - e.g., pin/bin
/t/ (voiceless) & /d/ (voiced) - e.g., tin/din
/k/ (voiceless) & /g/ (voiced) - e.g., cot/got

/f/ (voiceless) & /v/ (voiced) - e.g., fan/van
/θ/ (voiceless) & /ð/ (voiced) - e.g., thigh/thy
/s/ (voiceless) & /z/ (voiced) - e.g., sip/zip

/tʃ/ (voiceless) & /dʒ/ (voiced) - e.g., chin/gin

GRAMMAR (ENGLISH STRUCTURE): GENERAL REVISION

This section revises the main parts of speech and their functions.

Verbs: Action words or state-of-being words.
Main Verb: Carries the meaning (e.g., run, eat, is).
Auxiliary Verb: Helps the main verb (e.g., is running, has eaten, do you know?).
Transitive: Takes a direct object (e.g., "She kicked the ball.").
Intransitive: Does not take an object (e.g., "He slept.").
Adverbs: Modify verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs.
Manner: how? (e.g., slowly, well)
Place: where? (e.g., here, outside)
Time: when? (e.g., yesterday, now)
Frequency: how often? (e.g., always, sometimes)
Degree: to what extent? (e.g., very, too)
Adjectives: Modify nouns or pronouns (e.g., tall, beautiful).
Comparison: Positive (tall), Comparative (taller), Superlative (tallest).
Order (NOSASCOMP): Number, Opinion, Size, Age, Shape, Colour, Origin, Material, Purpose.
Conjunctions: Joining words.
Coordinating (FANBOYS): For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So (joins equal clauses).
Subordinating: because, if, although, when... (joins a dependent clause to a main clause).
Correlative: either...or, both...and...
Prepositions: Show relationships (time, location, direction). E.g., in, on, at, under, towards, since.
Interjections: Express strong, sudden emotion. E.g., Wow! Ouch! Alas!

COMPOSITION: GENERAL REVISION

This section revises the structure and features of key writing types.

Argumentative vs. Expository Essays

Argumentative:
Purpose: To persuade the reader to agree with your point of view.
Tone: Confident, formal, persuasive.
Key Feature: A strong thesis statement (your main argument) in the introduction.
Content: Uses evidence, logic, and examples to support arguments. May include a counter-argument.
Expository:
Purpose: To explain or inform the reader about a topic (e.g., "How to...", "Causes of...", "Types of...").
Tone: Objective, formal, informative.
Key Feature: A clear topic statement in the introduction.
Content: Uses facts, definitions, and step-by-step processes. Does not include the writer's opinion.

Formal vs. Informal Letters

Formal Letter:
Purpose: Official or business communication (e.g., to a Principal, a bank, a Commissioner).
Features:
Writer's address (top right)
Date (below writer's address)
Recipient's address (left, below date)
Salutation: Dear Sir, Dear Madam, Dear Mr. Ade,
Title/Subject Line (e.g., Application for Leave)
Closing: Yours faithfully, (if you started with Dear Sir/Madam) or Yours sincerely, (if you used their name).
Signature and full name.
Tone: Polite, respectful, concise. No slang or contractions.
Informal Letter:
Purpose: Communication with friends or family.
Features:
Writer's address (top right)
Date (below address)
Salutation: Dear Tunde, My dearest Mother,
Closing: Yours, Your friend, Your son,
First name only.
Tone: Casual, friendly, conversational. Slang and contractions are allowed.

LITERATURE IN ENGLISH: GENERAL REVISION

This section revises the key literary devices used in poetry.

Simile: A direct comparison using "like" or "as". (e.g., "He is as brave as a lion.")
Metaphor: An indirect comparison stating one thing is another. (e.g., "He is a lion in battle.")
Personification: Giving human qualities to non-human things. (e.g., "The wind whispered.")
Alliteration: Repetition of initial consonant sounds. (e.g., "Peter piper picked...")
Assonance: Repetition of vowel sounds within words. (e.g., "Try to light the fire.")
Onomatopoeia: A word that imitates a sound. (e.g., buzz, hiss, clang, plop).
Irony: A contrast between expectation and reality. (e.g., A fire station burning down).
Rhyme: Repetition of end-sounds in lines. (e.g., hat/cat).
Rhythm: The "beat" or pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables.
