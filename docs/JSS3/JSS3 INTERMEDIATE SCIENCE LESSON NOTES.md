Lesson Content:

Review of Key JSS2 Concepts

A. Matter:
Definition: Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space (volume).
States: Solid (definite shape/volume), Liquid (indefinite shape, definite volume), Gas (indefinite shape/volume).
Classification:
Elements: Pure substances that cannot be broken down (e.g., Iron, Oxygen, Carbon).
Compounds: Two or more elements chemically combined (e.g., Water - H₂O, Carbon Dioxide - CO₂).
Mixtures: Two or more substances physically combined, not chemically (e.g., sand and water, salt solution).
Separation Techniques: We learned about filtration, evaporation, sieving, and decantation.
B. Energy:
Definition: Energy is the ability or capacity to do work.
Forms (Recap): Potential (stored), Kinetic (movement), Heat, Light, Sound, Electrical, Chemical.
Principle of Conservation: Energy cannot be created or destroyed, but can be transformed from one form to another (e.g., a battery (chemical) powers a bulb (electrical) which gives off light and heat).
C. Classification of Living Things (The Five Kingdoms):

Monera: Unicellular, no true nucleus (prokaryotic). (e.g., Bacteria, Blue-green algae).
Protista: Unicellular, have a nucleus (eukaryotic). (e.g., Amoeba, Paramecium).
Fungi: Multicellular, "plant-like" but cannot make their own food (heterotrophic). (e.g., Mushroom, Mould, Yeast).
Plantae: Multicellular, make their own food (autotrophic) through photosynthesis. (e.g., Mango tree, maize, fern).
Animalia: Multicellular, cannot make their own food (heterotrophic), and can move. (e.g., Insects, fish, lions, humans).

Laboratory Conduct and Safety (Recap) The laboratory is a place for scientific discovery, but it can be dangerous. Safety is the most important rule.

Rules:
NEVER enter the lab without a teacher or lab attendant.
NO eating, drinking, or playing in the lab.
Wear protective clothing (lab coat, safety goggles) when required.
Read all instructions before starting an experiment.
NEVER taste, smell, or touch chemicals unless instructed.
When smelling a chemical, waft the vapour towards your nose; do not sniff directly from the container.
Report all accidents, breakages, or spills to the teacher immediately.
Always point test tubes away from yourself and others when heating.
Clean your workspace and wash your hands before leaving the lab.
Know the location of safety equipment (fire extinguisher, first aid box, eye-wash station).

Environmental Hazards: Problems like flooding, erosion, and drought, and how we can prevent them.

Lesson Content:

Meaning and Components of an Ecosystem

Definition: An Ecosystem is a biological community of living organisms (biotic) interacting with their non-living physical environment (abiotic) in a particular area. An ecosystem can be as large as a desert or an ocean, or as small as a pond or a rotting log.
Components:
A. Biotic (Living) Components: All the living or once-living parts of the ecosystem. They are grouped by how they get energy:
Producers (Autotrophs): Make their own food, usually from sunlight. (e.g., Plants, algae).
Consumers (Heterotrophs): Get energy by eating other organisms.
Primary Consumers (Herbivores): Eat producers. (e.g., Grasshopper, cow, rabbit).
Secondary Consumers (Carnivores/Omnivores): Eat primary consumers. (e.g., Frog, fox).
Tertiary Consumers: Eat secondary consumers. (e.g., Snake, hawk).
Decomposers (Saprotrophs): Break down dead organisms and waste products, returning nutrients to the soil. (e.g., Bacteria, Fungi).
B. Abiotic (Non-Living) Components: All the non-living chemical and physical parts of the environment. (e.g., Sunlight, Water, Air, Soil, Temperature, Salinity).

Interdependence and Balance of Nature

Interdependence: All these components depend on each other.
Plants (biotic) need soil, water, and sunlight (abiotic) to grow.
Animals (biotic) need plants (biotic) for food and water (abiotic) to drink.
Decomposers (biotic) need dead animals and plants (biotic) to feed on.
Plants (biotic) need decomposers (biotic) to create nutrients in the soil (abiotic).
Balance of Nature: In a healthy ecosystem, the population of each organism is kept in check. There is a balance between producers, consumers, and decomposers. If one part is removed, the whole system can be affected. (e.g., If all the frogs are removed, the grasshopper population might explode, destroying the grass).

Food Chains, Food Webs, and Energy Flow

Energy Flow: The ultimate source of all energy in (almost) every ecosystem is the SUN. Energy flows in one direction.
Food Chain: A simple, single pathway of energy flow in an ecosystem.
Example: Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Hawk (Producer) → (Primary) → (Secondary) → (Tertiary) → (Quaternary)
Food Web: A more complex and realistic model that shows all the interconnected food chains in an ecosystem. A food web shows that one animal (like a hawk) might eat snakes, rats, and frogs.

Energy Transfer: When a grasshopper eats grass, it only gets about 10% of the grass's energy. The other 90% is lost as heat or used for living. This means the Hawk at the top gets very little of the original sun's energy.

Disruption of Ecosystems When the "balance of nature" is disturbed, the ecosystem can be damaged. This is often caused by human activities:

Deforestation: Cutting down trees removes producers, destroys habitats, and leads to soil erosion.
Pollution: (To be covered in Week 4) Chemicals and waste kill plants and animals.
Overhunting/Overfishing: Removing too many consumers from a food web, causing an imbalance.
Introducing Invasive Species: Adding a new animal or plant that has no natural predators can cause it to take over.

Lesson Content:

What are Natural Resources?

Definition: Natural Resources are materials or substances that exist naturally in the environment and are considered valuable to humans. They are "gifts of nature" that we use for survival and economic development.
Examples in Nigeria: Crude oil, natural gas, coal, tin, limestone, forests, water, sunlight, fertile soil.

Types of Natural Resources

A. Renewable Resources:
Definition: Resources that can be replaced or replenished naturally over a short
period of time. They will not run out if we manage them wisely.
Examples:
Sunlight (Solar Energy): Will not run out.
Water (Hydropower): Replenished by the water cycle.
Wind: Will not run out.
Forests/Trees: Can be replanted (this is "managed" renewable).
Living Organisms: (Crops, Animals) Can be regrown/reproduced.
B. Non-Renewable Resources:
Definition: Resources that exist in a fixed amount and are formed over millions of years. Once they are used up, they are gone forever (or for a very, very long time).
Examples:
Fossil Fuels: (Formed from dead plants/animals)
Crude Oil (Petroleum): The backbone of Nigeria's economy.
Natural Gas: Often found with oil.
Coal: Found in places like Enugu.
Minerals: (e.g., Tin (Jos), Limestone (for cement), Iron Ore (Ajaokuta),
Gold).

Importance to National Economy

1. Government Revenue: Nigeria's government gets most of its money by selling crude oil. This money is used to build roads, schools, and hospitals.
2. Foreign Exchange: When Nigeria sells oil and gas to other countries, it earns foreign currency (like US Dollars), which it uses to buy things it doesn't produce.
3. Raw Materials: Resources are the raw materials for industries.
Limestone → Cement Industry
Forests (Timber) → Furniture and Paper Industry
Crude Oil → Petrol, Kerosene, Plastics
4. Employment: The oil and gas, mining, and agriculture sectors create millions of jobs.

Conservation and Management

Definition: Conservation is the wise, careful, and efficient use and protection of natural resources to prevent waste, loss, or depletion (running out).
Why Conserve? To ensure that these resources are available for future generations (this is Sustainability).
Methods of Conservation:
Reduce, Reuse, Recycle (The 3 R's):
Reduce: Use less (e.g., turn off lights, use less water).
Reuse: Use items multiple times (e.g., reuse plastic bags, water bottles).
Recycle: Turn waste materials into new products (e.g., recycling plastic, glass, paper).
Afforestation: Planting new trees to replace those that were cut down.
Using Alternatives: Shifting from non-renewable (like petrol) to renewable (like solar power) for energy.
Government Laws: Creating national parks to protect forests and wildlife; punishing illegal mining or oil spillage.

Lesson Content:

Meaning of Environmental Hazard

An Environmental Hazard is a natural or man-made event that has the potential to cause harm to people, property, and the environment.
When a hazard actually causes harm, it is called a disaster.

Types of Environmental Hazards in Nigeria

A. Drought:
Meaning: A long period of time with abnormally low rainfall, leading to a severe shortage of water.
Causes: Natural lack of rain (climate patterns); human activities like deforestation (trees help create rain).
Effects: Crop failure, famine (lack of food), death of livestock, drying up of rivers (like Lake Chad).
Prevention/Control:
Irrigation: Building systems to bring water to farms.
Afforestation: Planting trees (e.g., the "Great Green Wall" project) to attract rain and stop desert spread.
Dams: Building dams to store water during rainy seasons.
B. Desertification:
Meaning: The process by which fertile land (good for farming) in dry regions turns into a desert. This is a major problem in Northern Nigeria (Sokoto, Kano, Borno, etc.).
Causes:
Drought (see above).
Overgrazing: Allowing too many animals (cows, goats) to eat all the grass, leaving the soil bare.
Deforestation: Cutting down trees for firewood, which exposes the soil to the sun and wind.
Prevention/Control:
Afforestation: Planting trees (especially "shelter belts") to block the wind.
Controlled Grazing: Moving animals from one plot to another to allow grass to regrow.
C. Erosion (Soil Erosion):
Meaning: The washing or blowing away of the topsoil (the most fertile part of the soil).
Types:

Wind Erosion: Wind blows dry, bare soil away (common in the North).
Water Erosion: Rain washes soil away. This is a massive problem in Southeastern Nigeria (e.g., the Agulu-Nanka gully erosion in Anambra).
Causes: Deforestation, overgrazing, poor farming methods (like tilling down a slope).
Prevention/Control:

Cover Cropping/Planting Trees: Plant roots hold the soil together.
Terracing: Cutting "steps" into a hillside to slow down water.
Contour Ploughing: Ploughing across a slope, not down it.
D. Flooding:
Meaning: An overflow of a large amount of water onto land that is normally dry. This is a major problem in coastal cities (like Lagos) and along rivers (like Benue).
Causes:
Heavy Rainfall.
Poor Drainage: Blocked gutters and canals (often by human waste).
Building on Waterways: People building houses on land meant to be a drainage channel.
Prevention/Control:
Good Drainage: Constructing and clearing gutters and canals.
Legislation: Banning construction on waterways.
Dams: To control river water flow.
Disaster Preparedness

Definition: Taking steps before a hazard strikes to reduce its impact.
Examples:
Listening to warnings (e.g., from NIMET - Nigerian Meteorological Agency).
Educating the public on what to do (e.g., move to higher ground during a flood).
Storing emergency food and water.
Clearing your gutters before the rainy season.

Lesson Content:

Meaning and Components

Definition: Environmental Health is the branch of public health that focuses on the relationships between people and their environment. It aims to promote human health and well-being by ensuring the environment is safe and clean.
Components of a Healthy Environment:
Clean Air: Free from smoke, dust, and chemical pollutants.
Safe and Clean Water: Free from germs, chemicals, and waste (potable water).
Effective Waste Management: Proper collection and disposal of refuse and sewage.
Safe Food: Food that is free from contamination (germs, chemicals).
Vector Control: Controlling pests that spread disease (e.g., mosquitoes, rats, flies).
Noise Control: An environment free from harmful or stressful levels of noise.

Importance of a Clean Environment A clean environment is not just "nice to have"; it is
essential for human life.

Prevents Disease: This is the most important.
Clean water prevents cholera and typhoid.
Vector control prevents malaria (mosquitoes) and Lassa fever (rats).
Clean air reduces respiratory problems like asthma.
Improves Quality of Life: A clean, quiet, and green environment reduces stress and improves mental health.
Economic Benefits: People are healthier, so they are more productive at work and school. The government spends less money on treating preventable diseases.
Protects the Ecosystem: A clean environment supports local wildlife (birds, fish, etc.).

Roles in Maintaining Environmental Health

A. Role of the Individual (Your Role):
Proper Waste Disposal: Do NOT throw trash in the gutter. Bag your waste properly.
Personal Hygiene: Keep your body, clothes, and home clean.
Sanitation: Clear your gutters; don't allow stagnant water (where mosquitoes breed).
Reduce Pollution: Avoid burning trash; walk or use public transport instead of cars for short trips.
Report Issues: Report broken sewage pipes or large waste dumps to the authorities.
B. Role of the Government (Local, State, Federal):
Provide Infrastructure: Build water treatment plants, drainage systems, and landfills.
Waste Management: Organize and fund agencies (like LAWMA in Lagos) to collect and dispose of waste.
Create and Enforce Laws: Set pollution limits for factories; arrest people for improper waste disposal.
Public Education: Run campaigns to teach people the importance of hygiene.
Employ Health Officers: To inspect the community and enforce standards.

Environmental Health Officers

These are the (formerly called "Sanitary Inspectors" or "Wole-Wole" in Yoruba).
Role: They are government workers who:
Inspect houses, markets, and restaurants to ensure they are clean.
Enforce sanitation laws (e.g., during "Environmental Sanitation Day").
Educate the public on hygiene.
Control pests and disease outbreaks.

Lesson Content:

Meaning and Types of Waste

Waste (Refuse): Any material that is no longer wanted or needed and is thrown away.
Types of Waste:
Solid Waste (Refuse): Garbage from homes, schools, and markets (e.g., plastic bottles, food scraps, paper, cans).
Liquid Waste (Sewage/Effluent): Wastewater from sinks, toilets, and bathrooms (sewage); and chemical waste from factories (effluent).
Gaseous Waste: Harmful gases released from factories, car exhaust, and burning trash (e.g., carbon monoxide).

Methods of Waste Disposal

A. Bad/Unsafe Methods:
Dumping in Gutters/Rivers: Causes flooding and water pollution.
Uncontrolled Burning: Causes air pollution.
B. Proper/Modern Methods:

Landfill (Sanitary Landfill): The most common modern method. A large, deep pit is dug far from the city. Waste is dumped in thin layers and covered with soil each day. This prevents smells and stops rats/flies from breeding.
Incineration: Burning waste at very high temperatures (over 1000°C) in a controlled factory. This reduces the volume of waste by 90% and can be used to generate electricity (called "Waste-to-Energy").
Recycling: (See below).
Composting: (For organic waste) Allowing biodegradable waste (food scraps, leaves) to rot down and be used as fertilizer.

The 3 R's: The Waste Hierarchy This is the best strategy for managing waste, in order of importance.
REDUCE: The best solution. Use less in the first place.
Example: Buy products with less packaging. Don't take a plastic bag if you can carry the item.
REUSE: Use an item more than once.
Example: Reuse glass jars for storage. Refill your water bottle. Give old clothes to someone else.
RECYCLE: The last option.
Definition: The process of converting waste materials into new materials and objects.
Importance:
Conserves natural resources (e.g., recycling paper saves trees).
Saves energy (it takes less energy to recycle aluminum than to make it from raw ore).
Reduces the amount of waste in landfills.
Common Recyclables: Plastic (PET bottles), glass, paper, aluminum cans, steel.

Effects of Poor Waste Management (This is linked to Week 7 - Environmental Health)

Disease Outbreaks: Piles of waste attract rats, flies, and mosquitoes, which spread diseases like Lassa fever, cholera, and malaria.
Flooding: Waste blocks gutters and drains, causing severe flooding.
Pollution: Waste pollutes water (making it unsafe to drink) and air (from burning).
Ugly Environment: It makes the community look dirty and unpleasant, which can reduce property values and tourism.

Lesson Content:

Weather vs. Climate

Weather: The short-term state of the atmosphere (e.g., "It is raining today," "It is hot this afternoon").
Climate: The average weather conditions of a region over a long period of time (30 years or more). (e.g., "Lagos has a tropical wet climate," "Kano has a hot, dry climate").

Meaning of Climate Change and Global Warming

Climate Change: A significant, long-term change in the average weather patterns of a region (or the whole Earth).
Global Warming: The long-term heating of Earth's climate system observed since the pre-industrial period (around 1850-1900) due to human activities. Global warming is the main cause of climate change.

Causes of Climate Change (The Greenhouse Effect)

The Earth is surrounded by a "blanket" of gases (the atmosphere).
Sunlight (shortwave) passes through this blanket and warms the Earth.
The Earth radiates heat (longwave) back out.
Greenhouse Gases (GHGs) in the atmosphere (like Carbon Dioxide - CO₂) trap this outgoing heat, keeping the planet warm. This is natural and good.
Human Problem: Since the Industrial Revolution, humans have been burning fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) for factories, cars, and electricity.
This releases extra CO₂ into the atmosphere, making the "blanket" thicker.
This "thicker blanket" traps too much heat, causing Global Warming.

Main Causes:
Burning Fossil Fuels (CO₂).
Deforestation: Trees absorb CO₂. When we cut them down, CO₂ increases in the atmosphere.
Agriculture (Methane from cows).

Effects of Global Warming and Climate Change

1. Rising Sea Levels: Ice caps (glaciers) melt, and warm water expands. This is a huge
threat to coastal cities like Lagos, Port Harcourt, and Calabar, which could be flooded.
2. More Extreme Weather: More intense rainfall and flooding (in the South) and more severe droughts and desertification (in the North).
3. Disruption of Agriculture: Crop failures due to floods or droughts, leading to food insecurity.
4. Loss of Biodiversity: Plants and animals die because they cannot adapt to the new climate.

Solutions: Mitigation and Adaptation

A. Mitigation (Solving the Problem): Actions to reduce the amount of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere.
Switch to Renewable Energy: Use solar, wind, and hydro instead of fossil fuels.
Afforestation: Plant massive numbers of trees (trees "eat" CO₂).
Energy Efficiency: Use less energy (e.g., "Reduce, Reuse, Recycle").
B. Adaptation (Living with the Effects): Actions to manage the unavoidable impacts of climate change.
For Flooding: Build sea walls (like the "Great Wall of Lagos"), improve drainage.
For Drought: Teach farmers to use drought-resistant crops; build irrigation systems.

Practical: School-Based Tree Planting

The single best mitigation strategy a school can do is plant trees.
Trees fight climate change (absorb CO₂), provide shade (reduce heat), and prevent erosion.
(Class activity: Students will plant one or two saplings on the school grounds as a practical contribution).

Lesson Content:

Meaning and Principles of Sustainable Development

Famous Definition (Brundtland Report, 1987): "Sustainable Development is development that meets the needs of the present generation without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs."
Simpler Meaning: It means living well today, but not in a way that destroys the planet for our children and grandchildren.
Example:
Not Sustainable: Cutting down all the trees in a forest to sell the wood today. (The forest is gone, and future generations get nothing).
Sustainable: Cutting down 100 trees but planting 200 new ones. (You get wood today, and the forest remains for the future).

The Three Pillars of Sustainability For development to be truly sustainable, it must balance three things:

Environmental Sustainability: Protecting the planet, conserving resources, reducing pollution. (e.g., using solar power).
Economic Sustainability: Creating jobs and wealth in a way that can last. (e.g., a business that doesn't destroy its own raw materials).
Social Sustainability: Ensuring a good quality of life for all people (e.g., education, healthcare, human rights).

Environmental Protection and Sustainability Environmental protection is the core of sustainability. We must protect our environment so it can continue to provide for us.

This involves:
Conservation of resources (Week 3).
Protecting ecosystems (Week 2).
Managing waste and pollution (Weeks 4, 8).
Tackling climate change (Week 10).
The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)

The SDGs (or "Global Goals") are a set of 17 goals adopted by all United Nations countries in 2015, to be achieved by 2030. They are a "to-do list" for a sustainable future.
Environmental SDGs (Examples):
Goal 6: Clean Water and Sanitation
Goal 7: Affordable and Clean Energy (Solar, Wind)
Goal 11: Sustainable Cities and Communities
Goal 13: Climate Action
Goal 14: Life Below Water

Lesson Content:

Definition and The Cell Theory
Definition: The cell is the smallest, most basic structural, functional, and biological unit of all known living organisms. It is the "building block" of life. Some organisms (like Amoeba) are made of just one cell (unicellular), while others (like humans) are made of trillions (multicellular).
The Cell Theory: This is the foundational theory of biology, developed by scientists Matthias Schleiden, Theodor Schwann, and Rudolf Virchow. It states three main principles:
All living organisms are composed of one or more cells.
The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living organisms.
All new cells arise from pre-existing cells (through cell division).

Cell Organelles and Their Roles

Organelles ("little organs") are the specialized structures within a cell that perform specific jobs to keep the cell alive.
A. Cytoplasm: The jelly-like substance that fills the cell and surrounds the organelles. It is the site of many chemical reactions.
B. Cell Membrane (Plasma Membrane):
Function: A thin, flexible barrier that surrounds all cells.
It is semi-permeable (or selectively permeable), meaning it controls what substances (like food, oxygen, and waste) can enter and leave the cell.
C. Nucleus:
Function: The "control center" or "brain" of the cell.
It contains the chromosomes/DNA (genetic material), which carry the instructions for all cell activities, including growth and reproduction.
D. Mitochondrion (plural: mitochondria):
Function: The "powerhouse" of the cell.
This is where respiration occurs, a process that breaks down food (glucose) to release energy (ATP) for the cell to use.
E. Ribosomes:
Function: The tiny "factories" that build proteins. Proteins are needed for growth and repair.
F. Vacuole:
Function: A "storage bag" inside the cell. It stores water, food, and waste.
In Plant Cells: Usually one, large, central vacuole that is full of water (cell sap). This water presses against the cell wall (turgor pressure) and keeps the plant firm.
In Animal Cells: Usually small, numerous, and temporary vacuoles, if present at all.
G. Cell Wall (Plant Cells Only):
Function: A tough, rigid outer layer found outside the cell membrane in plants, fungi, and bacteria.
It is made of cellulose (in plants).
It provides structural support and protection to the plant cell, giving it a fixed, rectangular shape.
H. Chloroplasts (Plant Cells Only):
Function: The site of photosynthesis.
They contain a green pigment called chlorophyll, which absorbs sunlight.
This sunlight energy is used to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose (food) for the plant.

Lesson Content:

The Levels of Organization In multicellular organisms (like humans), cells are organized into a hierarchy to perform complex functions.

Level 1: Cells: The basic unit (e.g., muscle cell, nerve cell, skin cell).
Level 2: Tissues: A group of similar cells that work together to perform a specific function.
Level 3: Organs: A structure made of different types of tissues that all work together to perform a complex job.
Level 4: Organ Systems: A group of different organs that work together to carry out a major life process.
Level 5: Organism: The complete living being (e.g., a human, a dog, a tree).

Analogy: A school.

Cell = A single student.
Tissue = All the JSS3 students (a group of similar units with a specific role).
Organ = The Science Department (made of different tissues: JSS1, JSS2, JSS3 students, and teachers).
Organ System = The whole school (made of different organs: Science Dept., Admin, Arts Dept.).
Organism = The entire Education System.

Types of Animal Tissues
Epithelial Tissue:
Function: Covers and protects. It forms the skin (outer covering) and the linings
of internal organs (like the stomach and intestines).
Example: Skin, lining of the mouth.
Connective Tissue:
Function: Connects, supports, and binds other tissues together.
Examples: Bone, Cartilage (in your nose and ears), Blood (a liquid connective tissue), tendons, and ligaments.
Muscle Tissue:
Function: Contracts and relaxes to produce movement.
Examples: Biceps, heart muscle, stomach wall.
Nervous Tissue:
Function: Transmits electrical signals (messages) throughout the body.
Examples: Found in the brain, spinal cord, and nerves.

Types of Plant Tissues

Meristematic Tissue: Found at the tips of roots and shoots; responsible for growth.
Dermal Tissue: The "skin" of the plant (epidermis); protects it.
Vascular Tissue: The "transport" system.
Xylem: Transports water up from the roots.
Phloem: Transports food (sugar) down from the leaves.
Ground Tissue: Fills the space in between; site of photosynthesis.

Organs and Organ Systems

Organ: A group of different tissues.
Example: The Stomach (Organ) is made of muscle tissue (to churn), epithelial tissue (to line it), and nervous tissue (to control it).
Other Organs: Heart, Lungs, Brain, Kidney, Leaf (a plant organ).
Organ System: A group of organs working together.
Digestive System: (Organs: Stomach, Small Intestine, Large Intestine, Liver).
Function: To break down and absorb food.
Circulatory System: (Organs: Heart, Blood Vessels, Blood). Function: To transport oxygen and nutrients.
Respiratory System: (Organs: Lungs, Trachea). Function: To take in oxygen and remove carbon dioxide.
Excretory System: (Organs: Kidneys, Bladder). Function: To remove waste from the blood.
Nervous System: (Organs: Brain, Spinal Cord, Nerves). Function: To control and coordinate the body.

Importance of Coordination All these systems must work together ("coordinate") to keep the organism alive.

Example: When you run (Muscular System), your Lungs (Respiratory System) work faster to get oxygen, and your Heart (Circulatory System) works faster to pump that oxygen to the muscles. This is all controlled by your Brain (Nervous System).

Lesson Content:

Meaning of Heredity

Heredity: The passing of traits (characteristics) from parents to their offspring
(children). It is the reason why children look like their parents.
Genetics: The scientific study of heredity and how traits are passed on.
Genes: The units of heredity. They are segments of DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid) found on chromosomes inside the nucleus of our cells. Genes are the "instruction manual" that determines our traits.

Inherited vs. Acquired Traits

A. Inherited Traits:
Definition: Characteristics that you are born with. They are passed down from your parents through genes. You have no control over them.
Examples:
Blood group (A, B, AB, O)
Skin colour
Eye colour
Natural hair colour and texture (curly/straight)
Ability to roll your tongue
Attached or free earlobes
B. Acquired Traits:
Definition: Characteristics that you "get" or develop during your lifetime. They are not in your genes and cannot be passed on to your children.
Examples:
Scars
Muscles from exercise
Ability to speak a language (e.g., English, Yoruba)
Knowledge from school
A tattoo or tribal mark
Bleached skin

Variation

Definition: The differences (in traits) that exist among individuals of the same species. It is why everyone in your class (except identical twins) looks different from each other.
Causes of Variation:
Sexual Reproduction: The mixing of genes from two different parents (a father and a mother) creates new, unique combinations.
Environment: Your environment can also cause differences (e.g., two plants with the same genes, but one gets more sun and grows taller).
Importance of Variation:

Variation is the raw material for evolution and adaptation.
It allows a species to survive changes in the environment.
Example: In a population of goats, some have thick fur, and some have thin fur (variation). If the climate suddenly becomes very cold, the thin-furred goats might die, but the thick-furred goats (thanks to variation) will survive, reproduce, and pass on the "thick fur" gene.

Simple Genetic Inheritance (Mendelian Genetics)

For each trait, you get two genes (one from your father, one from your mother).
Dominant Gene: The "stronger" gene. If it is present, its trait will always show. (Represented by a capital letter, e.g., T for Tall).
Recessive Gene: The "weaker" gene. Its trait is hidden if a dominant gene is present. It will only show if both genes are recessive. (Represented by a small letter, e.g., t for short).
Example (Height):
If a plant gets TT (from both parents) → It is Tall.
If a plant gets tt (from both parents) → It is short.
If a plant gets Tt (one of each) → It is Tall (because the T is dominant and hides
the t).

Meaning of Adaptation and Habitat

Habitat: The natural home or environment where an organism lives (e.g., the desert, a river, a forest).
Adaptation: A special trait or feature (a change in the body or behavior) that helps an organism to survive and reproduce successfully in its specific habitat.
Importance: Organisms that are well-adapted to their environment survive, find food, and have offspring. Organisms that are poorly-adapted will die.

Types of Adaptations

A. Structural (or Physical) Adaptations:
Definition: These are physical features of an organism's body that help it survive.

Lesson Content:

Basic Structure of an Atom

Definition: An atom is the smallest particle of an element that can take part in a chemical reaction. It is the basic "building block" of all matter.
Structure: An atom is made of two main parts:
The Nucleus: The tiny, dense, center of the atom. It contains two particles:
Protons (p⁺): Positively charged particles (+).
Neutrons (n⁰): Neutral particles (no charge).
The Electron Shells (or Orbits): The "paths" outside the nucleus. They contain:
Electrons (e⁻): Negatively charged particles (-). They orbit the nucleus at high speed.
In a neutral atom, the number of protons (+) is equal to the number of electrons (-), so the overall charge is zero.

Elements, Compounds, and Mixtures (Review)

Element: A pure substance made of only one type of atom. (e.g., Gold (Au), Oxygen (O)).
Compound: A pure substance made of two or more different types of atoms chemically bonded together in a fixed ratio. (e.g., Water (H₂O) - always 2 Hydrogen atoms, 1 Oxygen atom).
Molecule: The smallest particle of an element or compound that can exist on its own. (e.g., a molecule of Oxygen, O₂; a molecule of Water, H₂O).
Mixture: Two or more substances (elements or compounds) physically mixed together, not chemically bonded. (e.g., Salt water).

Lesson Content:

Physical Changes

Definition: A physical change is a change that affects the form or appearance of a substance, but does not change its chemical identity.
Key Features:
No new substance is formed. (Water is still H₂O, whether it is ice, liquid, or steam).
The change is usually reversible.
No new energy is produced (or very little).
Examples:

Change of State:

Freezing (liquid to solid) - e.g., water turning to ice.
Evaporation (liquid to gas) - e.g., boiling water.

Dissolving: Salt or sugar dissolving in water. (You can get the salt back by evaporating the water).
Changing Shape: Cutting paper, crushing a can, breaking glass.

Chemical Changes

Definition: A chemical change (or chemical reaction) is a change that results in the formation of one or more new substances with different properties.
Key Features:
A new substance is formed. (Ash is not the same as wood).
The change is usually irreversible (you can't "un-burn" wood).
Energy (heat, light, sound) is often produced or absorbed.
Examples:

Burning (Combustion): Burning wood, paper, or gas. (Produces ash, carbon dioxide, etc.).
Rusting: Iron + Oxygen → Iron Oxide (Rust).
Cooking/Baking: An egg, once fried, cannot be made raw again.
Digestion: The food in your stomach is broken down by acids into new chemicals.
Decay/Rotting: Food or leaves rotting.

Reversible Change: A change that can be easily "undone" to get the original materials back. (Most physical changes).
Example: Melting ice. You can freeze it back.
Irreversible Change: A change that cannot be undone. (Most chemical changes).
Example: Frying an egg. You cannot make it raw again.

Air Pressure

Definition: Air pressure is the weight of the air in the atmosphere pressing down on the Earth's surface. We don't feel it because it presses on us equally from all directions.
Experiment 1: The Crushing Can
Put a small amount of water in an empty aluminum can.
Heat the can until the water boils and steam comes out (this pushes all the air out).
Quickly turn the can upside-down and plunge it into a bowl of cold water.
Observation: The can will be instantly crushed.
Explanation: The steam inside cooled, condensed, and created a vacuum (low pressure). The high air pressure outside the can was then strong enough to crush it.
Experiment 2: The Inverted Glass
Fill a glass to the brim with water.
Place a flat piece of cardboard over the top.
Holding the cardboard, turn the glass upside-down.
Observation: The cardboard stays in place, and the water does not fall out.
Explanation: The force of the air pressure pushing up on the cardboard is stronger than the weight of the water pushing down.

Content: (I) Conversion from one form to another (II) Energy conservation (III) Simple demonstrations.

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Lesson Content:

Review: Forms and Sources of Energy

Energy: Energy is the capacity or ability to do work. Work is done when a force causes an object to move. The standard unit of energy is the Joule (J).
Forms of Energy (Recap):
Chemical Energy: Stored in bonds of atoms and molecules (e.g., food, batteries, fuel).
Electrical Energy: The movement of electrons (e.g., electricity in wires).
Mechanical Energy: Energy of motion (Kinetic) or position (Potential).
Heat (Thermal) Energy: The vibration of atoms in a substance.
Light (Radiant) Energy: Energy from electromagnetic waves.
Sound Energy: Energy from vibrations that travel as waves.

Energy Transformation (Conversion)

Definition: Energy Transformation is the process of changing energy from one form into another.
We do not "use up" energy; we simply convert it into a form we need. In every conversion, some energy is also "lost" or wasted, usually as heat.

Examples of Energy Transformation: It is crucial to be able to trace the path of energy.

A Battery-Powered Torch:
Chemical Energy (stored in battery) → Electrical Energy (in wires) → Light Energy (from the bulb) + Heat Energy (wasted).
A Human Running:
Chemical Energy (stored in food) → Mechanical Energy (Kinetic energy of moving muscles) + Heat Energy (body gets warm).
A Solar Panel System:
Light Energy (from the sun) → Electrical Energy (in the panel) → (e.g., powers a fan) → Mechanical Energy (blades spinning).
An Electric Fan:
Electrical Energy (from socket) → Mechanical Energy (blades spinning) + Sound Energy + Heat Energy (motor gets warm).
A Hydroelectric Dam:
Potential Energy (water stored high up) → Kinetic Energy (water falling) → Mechanical Energy (turbines spinning) → Electrical Energy (in generators).
Burning Wood (Fire):

Chemical Energy (stored in wood) → Heat Energy + Light Energy.

The Principle of Conservation of Energy

This is a fundamental law of physics.
Statement: "Energy cannot be created or destroyed, but it can be transformed (converted) from one form to another."
Meaning: The total amount of energy in the universe is constant. When you use a torch, the chemical energy in the battery is not "gone"; it has simply changed into an equal total amount of light and heat energy.

Simple Demonstrations
A. The Dropped Ball (Potential to Kinetic):
Hold a ball high up. It has stored Potential Energy (due to its height).
Drop the ball. As it falls, it loses height (loses Potential) and gains speed (gains Kinetic Energy).
Just before it hits the ground, all its Potential Energy has transformed into Kinetic Energy.
B. A Simple Pendulum:
o
Pull a pendulum bob to one side (Position A). It has maximum Potential Energy.
Release it. It swings down, and at the bottom (Position B), its energy is maximum Kinetic Energy.
It swings up to the other side (Position C), converting its Kinetic Energy back into Potential Energy.
This shows a continuous transformation between potential and kinetic energy.

WEEK 2: HEAT TRANSFER

Content: (I) Conduction, convection, radiation (II) Practical examples (III) Insulators and conductors.

Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Explain the principle behind a vacuum flask.

Lesson Content:

Definition of Heat
Heat (Thermal Energy): Heat is a form of energy that is transferred from a hotter object to a colder object. Heat energy is the total kinetic energy of the atoms or molecules in a substance.
Heat always flows from hot to cold.

Methods of Heat Transfer There are three ways heat can move:

A. Conduction:
Definition: The transfer of heat through a substance by direct contact, from molecule to molecule, without the molecules themselves moving from their position. They just vibrate and "pass" the energy along.
Medium: Primarily occurs in Solids.
Example 1: If you put a metal spoon in hot tea, the handle of the spoon will eventually become hot. The heat conducted from the tea, up the spoon, particle by particle.
Example 2: Frying an egg. The heat from the stove conducts to the frying pan, which conducts to the egg.
B. Convection:
Definition: The transfer of heat through fluids (liquids and gases) by the actual movement of the fluid itself.
Process (Convection Current):
The fluid at the bottom (e.g., water in a pot) gets heated.
When it gets hot, it expands and becomes less dense (lighter).
This hot, light fluid rises.
The colder, denser fluid at the top sinks to take its place.
This cold fluid gets heated, and the cycle repeats. This circular movement is a convection current.
Examples:
Boiling Water: The water at the bottom rises, and the cooler water at the top sinks.
Sea Breeze: During the day, the land heats up faster than the sea. The hot air over the land rises, and the cool air from the sea blows in to replace it (a sea breeze).
C. Radiation:
Definition: The transfer of heat in the form of electromagnetic waves
(specifically, infrared waves).
Medium: Requires NO MEDIUM (no solids, liquids, or gases). It can travel through a vacuum (empty space).
Examples:
The Sun: The heat from the sun travels millions of kilometers through the vacuum of space to warm the Earth.
A Fire: You can feel the heat from a bonfire from a distance, even if the air between you is not moving. The heat radiates to your skin.
A microwave oven.

Conductors and Insulators

Conductors (Heat): Materials that allow heat to pass through them easily and quickly.
Examples: All metals (like silver, copper, aluminum, iron). This is why cooking pots are made of metal.
Insulators (Heat): Materials that do not allow heat to pass through them easily. They are poor conductors.
Examples: Wood, Plastic, Glass, Rubber, Fabric (cloth), Air (trapped air is a very good insulator). This is why pot handles are made of plastic or wood.
Application: The Vacuum (Thermos) Flask
A vacuum flask (or "thermos") is designed to stop all three forms of heat transfer.
It has two walls (like a bottle inside a bottle).
Conduction: The stopper (lid) is made of plastic (an insulator). The walls are separated, so heat can't conduct.
Convection: The space between the two walls is a vacuum (all the air has been removed). Since there is no fluid (air), convection cannot occur.
Radiation: The two walls are coated with a shiny, silver layer (like a mirror) to

reflect heat radiation (keeping hot things hot and cold things cold).

Solar Energy (Light Energy): Solar panels (photovoltaic cells) convert light energy directly into electrical energy.

Electric Circuit and Symbols

Electric Circuit: A complete, closed path that allows electric current to flow. If the path is broken (e.g., a cut wire or an open switch), it is an open circuit, and the current stops.

Simple Circuit Diagram:
Types of Circuits:
Series Circuit: Components are connected in a single path, one after another.
Advantage: Simple to build.
Disadvantage: If one bulb breaks, the entire circuit is open, and all other bulbs go out. (Like old Christmas lights).

Your house is wired in parallel.

NEVER poke metal objects (like forks or fingers) into a socket.
ALWAYS switch off an appliance before unplugging it.
DO NOT fly kites near overhead power lines.

Turn off lights and fans when you leave a room.
Use energy-efficient bulbs (LEDs) instead of old incandescent bulbs.
Unplug electronics (like TVs and chargers) when not in use.
Ensure your refrigerator door is sealed tightly.

Lesson Content:

Definition of Work and Machine
Work (Scientific Definition): Work is done when a force (Effort) moves an object (the
Load) over a distance.
Machine: A machine is any device that makes work easier by:
Reducing the amount of Effort needed (e.g., a lever).
Changing the direction of the force (e.g., a pulley).
Increasing the speed or distance (e.g., a bicycle gear).

Key Terms

Load (L): The force or weight that you are trying to move (the resistance).
Effort (E): The force you apply to the machine to move the load.
Pivot (or Fulcrum): The fixed point around which a lever turns.

Simple Machines vs. Compound Machines

Simple Machine: A device that has one or two parts and makes work easier. There are
six types.
Compound Machine: A machine made up of two or more simple machines working together.
o Examples: A wheelbarrow (a lever + a wheel & axle), a bicycle (levers, pulleys, wheels & axles), a car.

The Six (6) Types of Simple Machines

Lever: A rigid bar that turns around a fixed point (the pivot).
Examples: See-saw, crowbar, bottle opener, wheelbarrow, scissors (two levers).
Pulley: A wheel with a groove that a rope or chain passes over. Used for lifting.
Examples: A flagpole, a crane, a well.
Wheel and Axle: A large wheel fixed to a smaller rod (the axle). When one turns, the other turns.
Examples: A doorknob, a car's steering wheel, a tap handle.
Inclined Plane: A flat, sloping surface. It allows you to move an object to a greater height with less effort (but over a longer distance).
Example: A ramp, a slide.
Wedge: Two inclined planes put together, used to split things apart.
Examples: An axe, a knife, a nail, your front teeth.
Screw: An inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder (a spiral). Used to hold things together or lift.
Examples: A screw, a bolt, a car jack.

Mechanical Advantage (MA)

Definition: This is the advantage you get from using a machine. It is a number that tells you how many times the machine multiplies your effort.
Formula:
Mechanical Advantage (MA) = Load / Effort
Example:
I want to lift a Load of 100 Newtons.
Using a lever, I only have to push down with an Effort of 20 Newtons.
o My MA = 100 N / 20 N = 5.
This means the machine multiplied my effort by 5, making the work 5 times easier.

Practical Demonstration (Lever)

Use a ruler (lever) and a pencil (pivot).
Place a heavy book (load) on one end (e.g., at 5cm).
Place the pencil (pivot) under the 10cm mark.
Push down on the other end (effort) to lift the book.
Now, move the pivot closer to the load (e.g., at 8cm). It becomes easier to lift (MA has increased).

Lesson Content:

What are Science and Technology Careers? These are jobs that require the knowledge of science, mathematics, and technology to solve problems and create new things. They are some of the most important jobs for building a modern country.

Career Fields and Examples

A. Medical Field (Health Sciences):
Doctor (Physician): Diagnoses and treats illnesses and injuries.
Pharmacist: Prepares and dispenses medicines; an expert on drugs.
Medical Laboratory Scientist: Runs tests on blood, tissue, and fluids to help doctors diagnose diseases.
Nurse: Provides care for patients in hospitals and clinics.
Radiographer: Operates X-ray, CT, and MRI scanners.
B. Engineering Field:
Civil Engineer: Designs and builds infrastructure (roads, bridges, dams, large buildings).
Electrical Engineer: Designs electrical systems (power grids, electronics, computers).
Mechanical Engineer: Designs and builds machines (engines, generators, cars, robots).
Chemical Engineer: Designs processes to turn raw materials into useful products (e.g., refining petroleum, making soap, processing food).
C. Information & Comm. Technology (ICT) Field:
Software Developer (Programmer): Writes the code that creates computer programs, apps, and websites.
Network Administrator: Manages and secures a company's or school's computer network.
Data Scientist: Analyzes large amounts of data to find patterns and make predictions.
Cybersecurity Expert: Protects computer systems from hackers and viruses.
D. Research Field (Pure Science):
Biologist/Biotechnologist: Studies living things; may develop new medicines or crops.
Chemist: Studies substances; may invent new materials (like plastics or batteries).
Physicist: Studies energy and matter; may work in space exploration or energy.
Geologist: Studies the Earth; explores for oil, gas, and minerals.

Skills Required for S&T Careers
1. Strong Foundation Subjects: You must be good at Mathematics, Physics, Chemistry, and Biology.
2. Problem-Solving: The main job is to find solutions to difficult problems.
3. Analytical Skills: The ability to look at data and understand what it means.
4. Creativity and Curiosity: Thinking of new ideas and asking "Why?"
5. ICT Literacy: Almost all science and technology jobs require strong computer skills.

Importance to National Growth

A nation cannot develop without its own scientists, doctors, and engineers.
Health: More doctors and nurses mean a healthier population, which is more productive.
Infrastructure: Civil engineers build the roads and bridges needed to transport goods.
Economy: ICT professionals and engineers create new companies (like Paystack, Flutterwave), create jobs, and solve local problems.
Self-Reliance: When Nigeria has its own experts, it doesn't have to depend on other countries to build its infrastructure or solve its problems.

Lesson Content:

What is a Science Project?

A science project is a practical, hands-on investigation where you use the Scientific Method to answer a question or solve a problem.
It is not just building a model; it is an experiment designed to find an answer.

The Scientific Method (Steps for a Project):

Ask a Question (Problem): Start with something you are curious about.
Bad Question: "What is a plant?" (This is just a fact).
Good Question: "Does the colour of light affect how fast a plant grows?"
Do Background Research: Read books or use the internet to see what is already known.
Form a Hypothesis: Make an "educated guess" to answer your question.
Hypothesis: "I predict that the plant under the blue light will grow tallest."
Design and Conduct an Experiment: Plan a fair test to check your hypothesis.
Fair Test: You must have a control group (plant with normal light) and variables. You only change one thing (the independent variable - light colour). Everything else (water, soil, plant type) must be kept the same (the constants).
Collect and Record Data: Observe and measure what happens. Write it down in a table or chart.
Analyze Data: Look at your results. Did the blue light plant grow tallest?
Draw a Conclusion: State your final answer.
"My hypothesis was correct/incorrect. The plant under blue light grew tallest."
Present Your Findings: (See below).

Planning, Execution, and Teamwork

Planning: This is the most important step. You must write down your question, hypothesis, and a list of all materials and steps.
Teamwork: Most projects are too big for one person.
Roles: Divide the work. (e.g., One person does research, one person gathers materials, one person takes measurements).
Collaboration: Share ideas and listen to each other to find the best solution.

Presentation and Exhibition

A science exhibition is where you present your project to others.
Presentation: You must communicate your findings clearly. This usually involves:
A Display Board: A large cardboard (like a triptych) that shows all the steps of your project (Title, Hypothesis, Method, Data, Conclusion).
The Experiment: Your project materials, so you can demonstrate.
An Oral Presentation: A 2-5 minute speech where you explain what you did and what you found.

Assessment and Reflection

Your project will be assessed (marked) on:
Creativity of the question.
Correct use of the Scientific Method.
Clarity of your data and results.
How well you present and explain your project.
Reflection: Ask yourself: "What went wrong?" "What went right?" "What would I do differently next time?"

Plan a Simple Project:

Hypothesis: "I predict that [Brand A] will be the most absorbent."
How to Test (Method): "I will (1) Get 3 brands, (2) Pour 20ml of water, (3) Place one sheet of [Brand A] on the water for 10 seconds, (4) Measure the water left... repeat for B and C."
