WEEK 3 - POSITION, DISTANCE, AND DISPLACEMENT

DEFINITION OF POSITION, DISTANCE AND DISPLACEMENT

Position: This is the location of an object relative to a reference point (origin).Often described in terms of direction (left/right, north/south) and distance from the reference point.
Example:
A student sitting 2 meters to the right of the board is said to be at position +2m.

Distance: This is the total length of the path covered by a moving object, regardless of direction. It is a scalar quantity (has magnitude but no direction).It is always positive and measured in metres (m).
Example:1
If a person walks 4 meters forward and 4 meters back, the total distance is: 4m + 4m = 8m
Example: 2
If you walk 6 m east and 4 m west, the total distance is: 6m+ 4m= 10m

Displacement: This is the shortest distance from the starting point to the final position of the object in a straight line and specific direction.It is a vector quantity (has both magnitude and direction).It can be positive, negative, or zero.
Example:
Using the same movement:
From starting point to 4 m forward, and 4 m back = 0 m displacement (you returned to where you started).

GRAPHICAL AND PHYSICAL REPRESENTATION
Position, distance, and displacement can be graphically represented using graphs that plot position or distance against time. A position-time graph shows an object's location at different points in time, while a displacement-time graph shows the change in position (including direction) over time. Distance-time graphs, on the other hand, only show the total distance traveled, regardless of direction.

Position-Time Graph:
The x-axis represents time, and the y-axis represents the object's position.
A straight, horizontal line indicates the object is stationary.
An upward-sloping line indicates the object is moving away from the origin (positive direction).
A downward-sloping line indicates the object is moving towards the origin (negative direction).
The slope of the line at any point represents the object's instantaneous speed.

Displacement-Time Graph:
Similar to a position-time graph, the x-axis represents time, and the y-axis represents displacement.
Displacement is the change in position from the starting point, including direction.
The slope of the line at any point represents the object's instantaneous velocity.

Distance-Time Graph:
The x-axis represents time, and the y-axis represents the total distance traveled.
A straight, horizontal line indicates the object is not moving.
An upward-sloping line indicates the object is moving.
The slope of the line at any point represents the object's speed.

Distance-time graphs do not show direction.

Velocity–time sketch

Velocity starts at 0, rises linearly to 25 m/s in 20 s, stays constant at 25 m/s for 30 s (from t = 20 s to t = 50 s), then falls linearly to 0 in the last 10 s (t = 50 → 60 s).

Total distance — area under the v–t graph

Acceleration phase (triangle): area = ½ × base × height = ½ × 20 s × 25 m/s = 250 m.
Constant speed phase (rectangle): area = 30 s × 25 m/s = 750 m.
Deceleration phase (triangle): area = ½ × 10 s × 25 m/s = 125 m.

Total distance = 250 + 750 + 125 = 1125 m. (Whole-number answer: 1125 m.)

WEEK 9 & 10 - MOTION CONCEPT OF MOTION
Motion is the change in position of a body with respect to time and a reference point.

Rest: A body is said to be at rest if it does not change its position with time.
Motion: A body is in motion if it changes position with time.

TYPES OF MOTION
Translational Motion: Movement in a straight or curved path.
Rotational Motion: When a body spins about an axis.
Oscillatory Motion: Back-and-forth motion about a fixed point (e.g., pendulum).
Random Motion: Irregular, unpredictable movement (e.g., gas molecules).
Linear Motion: A type of translational motion in a straight line.

WEEK TWO - WORK, ENERGY AND POWER

DEFINITION OF WORK: Work is said to be done when a force moves an object through a distance in the direction of the force.

Formula for Work Done
W = Force x distance cosθ W = F x d x cosθ

Where:
W = Work done (Joules, J)
F = Force applied (Newtons, N) d = Displacement (meters, m)
θ = Angle between the force and the direction of motion
Conditions for Work to Be Done
A force must be applied.
There must be displacement.
The displacement must have a component in the direction of the force.

Types of Work
Positive Work: Force and displacement in same direction.
Negative Work: Force opposes displacement (e.g., friction).
Zero Work: No displacement or force perpendicular to displacement (e.g., carrying a load horizontally).
DEFINITION OF ENERGY: Energy is the capacity to do work. The S. I. Units of work is in Joule (J).

INTRODUCTION TO MACHINES
A machine is any device that allows us to do work more easily by either:

Multiplying the force we apply (making a small effort lift a large load),
Changing the direction of the applied force, or
Increasing the speed or distance moved.

Examples: Lever, Pulley, Inclined Plane, Screw, Wedge, Wheel and Axle.

BASIC FUNCTIONS OF A MACHINE
To multiply force (make work easier)
To change the direction of force (e.g., fixed pulley)
To multiply distance or speed (e.g., bicycle gears)
To transfer force from one point to another

DEFINITION AND NATURE OF MATTER
Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. Matter exists in different states and is made up of tiny, indivisible particles known as atoms, molecules, or ions depending on the substance. Particulate Nature of Matter:
The particulate nature refers to the concept that all matter is made up of small discrete units. These particles are:
Very small and cannot be seen with the naked eye.
Continuously in motion.
Held together by antiparticle forces.
Separated by empty spaces (especially in gases).

EXPLANATION OF CHANGES OF STATE:
Heating gives particles energy → increases motion → change in state (e.g., melting = solid to liquid).
Diffusion:
The process by which particles spread from a region of high concentration to a region of low concentration.

Examples/Demonstration:
Perfume spreading in a room.
Potassium permanganate crystals placed in water spread without stirring.
Ink dropped in water.

Explanation: Particles move and collide with each other, eventually spreading uniformly.

Brownian Motion:
The random and haphazard movement of tiny visible particles (like pollen grains or smoke) in a fluid due to collisions with smaller invisible molecules.

Demonstration/Experiment:

Smoke cell observed under a microscope.
Online simulation of Brownian motion.

Conclusion: This movement supports the theory that matter is made of constantly moving particles.

Change of State:
Melting, boiling, evaporation, condensation, etc., all demonstrate molecular motion and energy.

KINETIC THEORY OF MATTER
The kinetic theory explains the behavior of matter in terms of the motion of its particles.
Assumptions of Kinetic Theory:
Matter is made up of small particles.
These particles are in constant, random motion.
There are spaces between the particles.
Collisions between particles and with the container walls are perfectly elastic (no energy loss).
The temperature of a substance is a measure of the average kinetic energy of its particles.
Intermolecular forces vary with state—strong in solids, weaker in liquids, and negligible in gases.

APPLICATIONS AND REAL-LIFE EXAMPLES
Perfume spreads in air – diffusion in gases.
Sugar dissolving in water – molecular interaction.
Tyre expanding in heat – gas molecules gain kinetic energy.
Refrigerators – based on latent heat and molecular motion.
Cooking gas smell – diffusion of gas particles.
Boiling water – molecules move faster and escape as steam.

WEEK 1 - FLUIDS AT REST AND IN MOTION

DEFINITION AND TYPES OF FLUIDS
A fluid is any substance that can flow and take the shape of its container. This includes liquids and gases.
Key Properties of Fluids:
They have no fixed shape.
They exert pressure in all directions.
They can be compressed (especially gases).
They can flow (low internal resistance).
FLUIDS AT REST (FLUID STATICS)
Pressure in a Fluid at Rest
Formula:
P = ρgh
Where:
P = pressure (N/m² or Pascal)
ρ = density of fluid (kg/m³)
g = acceleration due to gravity (9.8 m/s²)
h = depth from the fluid surface (m)
Characteristics:
Pressure increases with depth.
It acts equally in all directions at the same depth.
Pressure is independent of shape or volume of the container.

FACTORS THAT AFFECT PRESSURE AT A POINT IN A LIQUID

Depth of the point below the surface of the liquid
Density of the liquid

PASCAL’S PRINCIPLE
Pressure applied to a confined fluid is transmitted equally and undiminished in all directions.

Mathematical Expression:

F1/A1	=	F2/A2
Where:
F1	, F2= input and output forces
A1	, A2= areas of pistons
Applications:
Hydraulic lifts
Car brakes
Jacks and presses
Dentist chairs

ARCHIMEDES’ PRINCIPLE
The principle states that a body wholly or partially submerged in a fluid experiences an upward force (upthrust) equal to the weight of the fluid displaced.

Formula for Upthrust:

Fu	= ρgV
Where:
Fu	= upthrust (N)
ρ = density of fluid
V = volume of fluid displaced

Apparent Weight:
Apparent weight = True weight − Upthrust

Applications:

Design of ships and submarines
Hydrometers used for measuring density
Floating of ice on water

How to Determine the Relative Density Of A Stone Using Archimedes’ Principle.

Weigh the stone in air (W₁).
Immerse the stone fully in water and weigh it again (W₂).
Upthrust = W₁ – W₂
Relative density = Weight in air / Upthrust = W₁ / (W₁ – W₂)

LAW OF FLOTATION
The Law of Floatation states that a body immersed in a fluid will float in the fluid when its weight is equal to the upthrust on it.
Conditions for Floating:
Weight of object = weight of fluid displaced

Explanation:
An object will float if its density is less than the fluid’s.
It will sink if its density is greater.
Applications:
Ship design
Floating logs
Life jackets
Icebergs

DEFINITION OF PROJECTILE
A projectile is any object that once projected or dropped continues in motion by its own inertia and is influenced only by the downward force of gravity. or
It is an object that is propelled forward and then moves under the influence of gravity and air resistance. It's commonly associated with objects launched in warfare or sports, like bullets or baseballs. For an object to be considered a projectile, an initial force must be applied to it, after which it moves freely, with gravity and air resistance being the primary forces acting upon it.
Examples of projectiles are:
A stone thrown vertically upwards.
A ball rebouncing from a wall.
An athlete doing high jump.
A stone release from a catapult.
A bullet fired from a gun.

PROJECTILE MOTION
Projectile motion is a form of motion where an object moves in a parabolic path. The path followed by the object is called its trajectory. Projectile motion refers to the movement of an object thrown or projected into the air and influenced by gravity.

Projectile motion can be considered under three headings as follows:
Motion of an object thrown vertically into the air.
Motion of an object projected horizontally from a certain height.
Motion of an object projected into the air at an angle of inclination to the horizontal/vertical.

In a Projectile Motion, there are two simultaneous independent rectilinear motions:
Along the x-axis: uniform velocity, responsible for the Horizontal (forward) motion of the particle.
Along the y-axis: uniform acceleration, responsible for the Vertical (downwards) motion of the particle.

WEEK 9 - TYPES OF WAVES

DEFINITION OF WAVE
A wave is a disturbance that travels through a medium or space, carrying energy without the transport of matter.
Waves originate from vibrations.
Waves are everywhere: light, sound, water waves, etc.

DIFFERENT PARTS OF A WAVE:
Here are the most important parts of a wave.
Rest position – This is the position of particles when there is no energy or wave passing through them; these particles are undisturbed. On a wave diagram, this is the line that is drawn through the centre of the wave.
Displacement – This is the distance that a particle has moved from its rest position due to its disturbance.
Amplitude – The measurement of displacement, we call the amplitude the maximum disturbance of a particular point in the medium. This is the distance between the rest position and the highest peak or trough.
Peaks and troughs – The points of greatest disturbance or maximum displacement, above and below the rest position.
Oscillation – The repetition of a wave – i.e. the space from peak to peak.
Wavelength – The actual distance covered by one oscillation – usually measured from peak to peak.
Frequency or wave speed – The number of times a wave oscillates in a second.

Period: The time taken for one complete wave to pass a given point.
Unit: Seconds (s)

Formula: T = 1
�

Wave Speed (v):The speed at which the wave travels through a medium.
Formula: v=f × λ
Unit: metres per second (m/s) Where:
v = wave speed (m/s)
f = frequency (Hz)
λ = wavelength (m)
Phase: The relative position of a point within a wave cycle.
Same phase: Points that have the same displacement and direction of motion.
Out of phase: Points that are not aligned in displacement and motion.

DETAILED TYPES OF WAVES
Transverse Waves: The particles of the medium move perpendicularly to the direction of wave propagation.
Features:
Consist of crests and troughs
Can travel through solids and the surface of liquids.
Examples:
Light waves
Water waves
Waves on strings or ropes
Longitudinal Waves: The particles of the medium vibrate parallel to the direction of wave propagation.
Features:
Composed of compressions and rarefactions
Travel through solids, liquids, and gases
Examples:
Sound waves
Slinky waves (along the spring)

PROPERTIES OF WAVES
Reflection: This is the bouncing back of a wave when it hits a surface. Light reflecting off a mirror is a common example.
Refraction: This is the bending of a wave as it passes from one medium to another. This happens because the wave changes speed when it enters the new medium.
Diffraction: Diffraction is the spreading of waves as they pass through an opening or around an obstacle.
Interference: Interference occurs when two or more waves overlap, creating a new wave pattern. This can result in constructive interference (increased amplitude) or destructive interference (decreased amplitude).
Stationary Wave: These are wave obtained when two progressive waves of equal amplitude and frequency are travelling in opposite direction and combine together.
Polarization: These involve the restriction of the vibration to only one plane.
