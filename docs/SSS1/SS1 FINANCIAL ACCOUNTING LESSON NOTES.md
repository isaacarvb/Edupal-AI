MEANING OF BOOK-KEEPING
BOOK-KEEPING:
The process of recording financial transactions in a systematic and chronological manner in the books of accounts.

Key Points:
Recording of transactions only
Routine and repetitive work
Does not require special skills
Concerned with "how to record"
Clerical in nature

Examples of Book-keeping Activities:
Recording sales in sales book
Recording purchases in purchase book
Posting entries to ledger
Maintaining cash book
Filing vouchers and invoices

MEANING OF ACCOUNTING
ACCOUNTING:
The process of identifying, measuring, recording, and communicating financial information to permit informed judgments and decisions by users of the information.

Key Points:
Includes book-keeping plus analysis and interpretation
Requires professional judgment
Analytical and interpretative
Concerned with "why" and "what does it mean"
Professional in nature

Examples of Accounting Activities:
Preparing financial statements
Analyzing profitability
Calculating ratios
Providing financial advice
Auditing accounts

CONTENT:
MEANING OF DOUBLE ENTRY
DOUBLE ENTRY SYSTEM:
A method of recording transactions where every transaction affects at least two accounts - one account is debited and another account is credited with equal amounts.

Principle:
FOR EVERY DEBIT, THERE MUST BE A CORRESPONDING CREDIT
Developed by: Luca Pacioli (Italian monk, 1494) Example:
Bought goods for cash ₦5,000
Cash Account is CREDITED ₦5,000 (cash going out)
Purchases Account is DEBITED ₦5,000 (goods coming in)

Benefits:
Complete record of transactions
Helps detect errors (if debit ≠ credit)
Shows cause and effect
Enables preparation of Trial Balance
Provides check on accuracy

TYPES OF ACCOUNTS
PERSONAL ACCOUNTS
Accounts of persons (individuals, firms, companies)

Examples:
John's Account
ABC Limited Account
Creditors Account
Debtors Account

Rule: DEBIT the receiver, CREDIT the giver

REAL ACCOUNTS
Accounts of assets and properties

Examples:
Land Account
Building Account
Furniture Account
Machinery Account
Stock Account

Rule: DEBIT what comes in, CREDIT what goes out

NOMINAL ACCOUNTS
Accounts of expenses, losses, incomes, and gains

Examples:
Expenses/Losses:	Income/Gains:
Salaries	• Sales
Rent	• Commission Received
Electricity	• Interest Received
Bad Debts	• Discount Received

Rule: DEBIT all expenses and losses CREDIT all incomes and gains

GOLDEN RULES OF ACCOUNTING
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ TYPE OF ACCOUNT │ DEBIT	│ CREDIT	│
├───────────────────┼──────────────────┼─────────────────┤

└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘ MEMORY AID:
"Dear Rosie, Give Credit" D - Debit the Receiver
C - Credit the Giver
"Come In Dear, Go Credit" Debit what Comes In Credit what Goes out

UNDERSTANDING DEBIT AND CREDIT
DEBIT (Dr):
Left side of account
Increases: Assets, Expenses, Losses
Decreases: Liabilities, Capital, Income

CREDIT (Cr):
Right side of account
Increases: Liabilities, Capital, Income
Decreases: Assets, Expenses, Losses

ACCOUNTING EQUATION:
Assets = Capital + Liabilities
Effect of Debit and Credit: ASSETS:
Debit (+) | Credit (-) Increase | Decrease

LIABILITIES:
Debit (-) | Credit (+) Decrease | Increase

CAPITAL:
Debit (-) | Credit (+) Decrease | Increase

EXPENSES:
Debit (+) | Credit (-) Increase | Decrease

INCOME:
Debit (-) | Credit (+) Decrease | Increase

SIMPLE EXAMPLES
Example 1:
Started business with cash ₦50,000
Analysis:
Cash (Asset) comes in - DEBIT Cash Account
Capital (Owner's contribution) - CREDIT Capital Account

Entry:
Debit: Cash Account	₦50,000 Credit: Capital Account	₦50,000

Example 2:
Bought goods for cash ₦10,000

Analysis:
Purchases (Expense) - DEBIT Purchases Account
Cash goes out - CREDIT Cash Account

Entry:
Debit: Purchases Account	₦10,000 Credit: Cash Account	₦10,000

Example 3:
Sold goods for cash ₦15,000

Analysis:
Cash comes in - DEBIT Cash Account
Sales (Income) - CREDIT Sales Account

Entry:
Debit: Cash Account	₦15,000 Credit: Sales Account	₦15,000

Example 4:
Bought furniture from ABC Ltd on credit ₦20,000

Analysis:
Furniture (Asset) comes in - DEBIT Furniture Account
ABC Ltd (Creditor/Giver) - CREDIT ABC Ltd Account

Entry:
Debit: Furniture Account	₦20,000 Credit: ABC Ltd Account	₦20,000

Example 5:
Paid salary ₦5,000 in cash
Analysis:
Salary (Expense) - DEBIT Salary Account
Cash goes out - CREDIT Cash Account

Entry:
Debit: Salary Account	₦5,000 Credit: Cash Account	₦5,000

BALANCING ACCOUNTS
STEPS TO BALANCE AN ACCOUNT:

Total both sides (Debit and Credit)

Find the difference

Write the difference on the side with smaller total as "Balance c/d" (carried down)

Make both sides equal

Double underline to show completion

Bring down the balance on the opposite side as "Balance b/d" (brought down)

RULES:
Assets and Expenses: Debit balance
Liabilities, Capital, and Income: Credit balance

c/d = carried down (at end of period)
b/d = brought down (at start of next period)

MEANING OF ACCOUNTING CONCEPTS
ACCOUNTING CONCEPTS:
Basic assumptions, rules, and principles that guide
the recording and presentation of financial information. Also called: Accounting assumptions or Accounting principles
Purpose:
Ensure uniformity in accounting
Make accounts comparable
Ensure reliability of financial statements
Guide accountants in decision making
Provide framework for accounting practice

GOING CONCERN CONCEPT
GOING CONCERN CONCEPT:
Assumes that a business will continue to operate for the foreseeable future and has no intention of closing down or significantly reducing operations.

Implications:

ASSET VALUATION
Assets recorded at cost, not selling price
Depreciation calculated over useful life
No need to value assets at liquidation value

Example:
Machine bought for ₦100,000
Recorded at ₦100,000 (not current selling price)
REVENUE AND EXPENSES
Matched over accounting periods
Long-term view taken
Prepayments and accruals recognized

Example:
Rent paid for 3 years in advance is spread over 3 years

LIABILITIES
Long-term liabilities appropriate
No need to settle all debts immediately

Example:
10-year loan recorded, not treated as current liability

When Going Concern is NOT Applicable:
Business is bankrupt
Business is closing down
Business in liquidation
Severe financial difficulties

In such cases:
Assets valued at break-up value (what they can be sold for)

ACCRUAL CONCEPT (MATCHING CONCEPT)
ACCRUAL CONCEPT:
Revenue and expenses should be recognized when they are earned or incurred, not when cash is received or paid.
Also known as: Matching Concept Principle:
MATCH expenses with the revenue they help generate in
the same accounting period. Key Points:
REVENUE RECOGNITION Recognize when:
Goods are delivered/services rendered
Not necessarily when payment received

Example:
Sold goods in December, payment in January
· Record sale in December (when earned)

EXPENSE RECOGNITION
Recognize when:
Incurred, not when paid
In period when benefit received

Example:
Electricity used in December, bill paid in January
· Record expense in December (when incurred)

PREPAYMENTS
Payment made in advance
Treat as asset until period of benefit

Example:
Paid ₦12,000 rent for one year in January
· Record ₦1,000 expense each month

ACCRUALS
Expense incurred but not yet paid Record as liability

Example:
December salary paid in January
· Record in December accounts

Benefits:
True profit/loss calculated
Accurate financial position
Comparable periods
Better decision making

Vs. CASH BASIS:
Cash Basis: Record when cash changes hands
Accrual Basis: Record when earned/incurred (better method)

MATERIALITY CONCEPT
MATERIALITY CONCEPT:
Only items that are significant enough to affect decisions need to be reported separately. Insignificant items can be grouped or ignored.

Principle:
Don't waste time on trivial matters. Focus on what matters.

What is Material? Depends on:
Size of amount (absolute or relative)
Nature of item
Context of the business Examples:
MATERIAL ITEMS:
₦1,000,000 expense for small business
Fraud of any amount
Large bad debt
Major lawsuit
Change in accounting policy

IMMATERIAL ITEMS:
₦500 expense for large company
Small office supplies
Minor repairs
Petty cash shortages Application:
Example 1:
Bought stapler for ₦1,000
Material for small business? Maybe not
Record as expense, don't treat as asset
Not worth depreciating over 5 years

Example 2:
Bought delivery van ₦2,000,000
Material for most businesses
Record as asset
Depreciate over useful life

Example 3:
Loss of ₦50,000 in petty cash
Material? YES (regardless of company size)
Should be investigated
Separately disclosed

Guidelines:
If omission/misstatement would influence decisions → Material
If amount is more than 5-10% of profit → Material
Use professional judgment
Consider cumulative effect

CONSISTENCY CONCEPT
CONSISTENCY CONCEPT:
Once an accounting method is adopted, it should be used consistently from one period to another unless there is a good reason to change.

Principle:
Don't keep changing methods - be consistent!

Purpose:
Makes accounts comparable over time
Prevents manipulation of profits
Ensures reliability
Facilitates trend analysis

Examples of Areas Requiring Consistency:

DEPRECIATION METHOD
If using straight-line method for vehicles, continue using it every year
Don't switch to reducing balance method without reason

STOCK VALUATION
If using FIFO (First In First Out), continue using FIFO
Don't switch to LIFO or Average method

ACCOUNTING POLICIES Revenue recognition method
Bad debt provision percentage Asset capitalization policy

When Can Methods Change?

Acceptable Reasons:
Change in accounting standards
New method gives fairer presentation
Change in nature of business
Regulatory requirement

Requirements When Changing:
Must be disclosed in financial statements
Effect of change must be stated
Reason for change must be given
Previous year figures may need adjustment Example:
Year 1: Depreciation using straight-line @ 10% = ₦50,000 Year 2: Changed to reducing balance @ 20% = ₦80,000 ✗

Problem: Profit not comparable
Year 1 profit may look higher (less depreciation) Year 2 profit may look lower (more depreciation)

Correct Approach:
Use same method unless justified reason to change

DUAL ASPECT CONCEPT
DUAL ASPECT CONCEPT:
Every transaction has two aspects - a debit and a credit. These two aspects are always equal.
Foundation of Double Entry System! Basic Equation:
ASSETS = CAPITAL + LIABILITIES

Or:
ASSETS = EQUITY + LIABILITIES
This MUST always balance! Principle:
Every debit has equal credit
Every transaction affects at least 2 accounts
Total debits = Total credits
What business owns = What business owes Examples:

Transaction 1: Started business with ₦100,000 cash Two Aspects:
Cash (Asset) increases by ₦100,000 [DEBIT]
Capital increases by ₦100,000	[CREDIT] Result: Assets ₦100,000 = Capital ₦100,000 ✓

Transaction 2: Bought goods ₦30,000 cash Two Aspects:
Purchases (Expense) ₦30,000	[DEBIT]
Cash (Asset) decreases ₦30,000	[CREDIT]

Result: Assets ₦70,000 = Capital ₦100,000 - Expense ₦30,000 ✓

Transaction 3: Bought furniture ₦50,000 on credit Two Aspects:
Furniture (Asset) increases ₦50,000	[DEBIT]
Creditor (Liability) increases ₦50,000 [CREDIT]

Result:
Assets (₦70,000 + ₦50,000) = Capital ₦100,000 +
Liability ₦50,000 - Expense ₦30,000 ✓

Benefits:
Enables checking accuracy (Trial Balance)
Complete record of transactions
Shows sources and uses of funds
Basis for financial statements

PRUDENCE CONCEPT (CONSERVATISM)
PRUDENCE CONCEPT:
When in doubt, choose the option that is less likely to overstate assets or income.
Also called: Conservatism Concept Motto:
"Anticipate no profits, but provide for all possible losses"

Rules:

REVENUE
Don't recognize until reasonably certain
Don't anticipate future profits
Be cautious in revenue recognition

Example:
Don't record profit on goods not yet sold Even if you expect to sell at high price

EXPENSES
Recognize as soon as possible
Provide for potential losses
Better to overstate expenses than understate

Example:
Provide for doubtful debts even if not yet bad Stock valued at lower of cost or market value

ASSETS
Don't overvalue
Write down if value decreases
Don't write up if value increases

Example:
If asset cost ₦100,000 but now worth ₦80,000, write down to ₦80,000
If asset cost ₦100,000 but now worth ₦120,000, keep at ₦100,000 (don't write up)

LIABILITIES
Don't understate
Provide for possible liabilities
Record when probable

Example:
Provide for pending lawsuit losses Even if not yet settled

Applications:

STOCK VALUATION
Rule: Lower of Cost or Net Realizable Value

Example:
Stock cost: ₦50,000 Market value: ₦45,000
Record at: ₦45,000 (lower)

Stock cost: ₦50,000 Market value: ₦60,000
Record at: ₦50,000 (lower)

PROVISION FOR DOUBTFUL DEBTS Debtors: ₦100,000
Estimate 5% may not pay Provide: ₦5,000 for bad debts
(Reducing profit now rather than wait)

DEPRECIATION
Depreciate assets even if not used Reduces asset value cautiously

CONTINGENT LIABILITIES Disclose possible losses Even if not certain

Balance Required:
⚠ Don't be too prudent (understating everything)
Be reasonably cautious
Aim for fair presentation
Don't deliberately understate profits

Vs. Optimism:
Prudence ✓ : Expected loss ₦10,000 → Provide ₦10,000 Optimism ✗ : Expected profit ₦10,000 → Record now (WRONG!)

MEANING OF BANK RECONCILIATION STATEMENT
BANK RECONCILIATION STATEMENT:
A statement prepared to reconcile (agree) the balance shown in the cash book with the balance shown in the bank statement on a particular date.

Key Points:
Shows reasons for differences
Not part of double entry system
Prepared periodically (monthly, quarterly)
Helps detect errors and omissions
Ensures accuracy of records Two Documents Involved:
CASH BOOK (Bank Column)
Maintained by the business
Records all bank transactions
Shows balance from business perspective

BANK STATEMENT
Prepared by the bank
Shows all transactions in customer's account
Shows balance from bank's perspective

WHY BANK RECONCILIATION IS NECESSARY
REASONS FOR BANK RECONCILIATION:

DETECT ERRORS
Errors in cash book
Errors in bank statement
Wrong postings
Incorrect amounts

IDENTIFY OMISSIONS
Bank charges not recorded
Interest not recorded
Standing orders forgotten
Direct credits missed

VERIFY ACCURACY
Ensures cash book is correct
Confirms bank statement accuracy
Provides independent check

INTERNAL CONTROL
Prevents fraud
Detects unauthorized transactions
Ensures accountability

MANAGEMENT INFORMATION
Shows true bank balance
Aids cash flow planning
Helps decision making

AUDIT REQUIREMENT
Required by auditors
Part of year-end procedures
Evidence of proper control

RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN CASH BOOK AND BANK STATEMENT
IMPORTANT CONCEPT:
What is DEBIT in Cash Book is CREDIT in Bank Statement What is CREDIT in Cash Book is DEBIT in Bank Statement

Why?

FROM BUSINESS PERSPECTIVE (Cash Book):
Money deposited = Asset increases = DEBIT
Money withdrawn = Asset decreases = CREDIT FROM BANK PERSPECTIVE (Bank Statement):
Money deposited = Bank owes customer = LIABILITY = CREDIT
Money withdrawn = Bank's liability reduces = DEBIT

EXAMPLE:
Transaction: Deposited ₦10,000 in bank CASH BOOK (Business):
Dr. Bank ₦10,000 (Asset increased)

BANK STATEMENT:
Cr. Customer's Account ₦10,000 (Bank owes more)

Transaction: Withdrew ₦5,000 from bank CASH BOOK (Business):
Cr. Bank ₦5,000 (Asset decreased)

BANK STATEMENT:
Dr. Customer's Account ₦5,000 (Bank owes less)

CAUSES OF DIFFERENCES
TWO MAIN CATEGORIES:

ITEMS IN CASH BOOK BUT NOT YET IN BANK STATEMENT (Timing Differences)

ITEMS IN BANK STATEMENT BUT NOT YET IN CASH BOOK (Unrecorded Items)

ITEMS IN CASH BOOK BUT NOT IN BANK STATEMENT
UNPRESENTED CHEQUES (OUTSTANDING CHEQUES)

Definition:
Cheques issued by the business but not yet presented to the bank for payment by the recipient.

Example:
Jan 28: Paid supplier by cheque ₦50,000
Jan 31: Supplier hasn't banked the cheque yet
Cash book: Shows payment (credit)
Bank statement: Doesn't show yet
Effect:
Cash book balance < Bank statement balance

Treatment:
DEDUCT from bank statement balance

UNCREDITED CHEQUES (LODGEMENTS NOT YET CREDITED) Definition:
Cheques/cash deposited by business but not yet recorded by bank (usually deposited late in the day).

Example:
Jan 31: Deposited ₦30,000 at 4:00 PM
Bank processed it on Feb 1
Cash book: Shows deposit (debit)
Bank statement: Doesn't show yet

Effect:
Cash book balance > Bank statement balance

Treatment:
ADD to bank statement balance

MEANING OF TRIAL BALANCE
TRIAL BALANCE:
A statement showing all ledger balances (debit and credit) at a particular date to verify the arithmetical accuracy of the books of accounts.

Key Points:
Prepared at end of accounting period
Lists all account balances
Total debits must equal total credits
Not part of double entry
Not a financial statement
Helps detect certain errors

Definition Breakdown:

STATEMENT: List/summary (not account)
ALL LEDGER BALANCES: Every account balance
PARTICULAR DATE: As at specific date (e.g., 31 Dec 2024) ARITHMETICAL ACCURACY: Check if Dr = Cr
BOOKS OF ACCOUNTS: All ledger accounts

PURPOSE/OBJECTIVES OF TRIAL BALANCE
CHECK ARITHMETICAL ACCURACY
Verify total debits = total credits

TYPES OF ERRORS
TWO MAIN CATEGORIES:

ERRORS THAT AFFECT TRIAL BALANCE AGREEMENT (One-sided errors - Trial balance won't agree)

ERRORS THAT DON'T AFFECT TRIAL BALANCE AGREEMENT (Two-sided errors - Trial balance will still agree)

WHY SOME ERRORS DON'T AFFECT TRIAL BALANCE:

Double entry maintained even though entries are wrong Both debit and credit sides equally affected Therefore, total debits still equal total credits But accounts are still incorrect!

ERRORS AFFECTING TRIAL BALANCE
TYPE 1: ERROR OF OMISSION (PARTIAL)

Definition:
One side of a transaction recorded, other side omitted.

Examples:
Sales ₦10,000 debited to customer, not credited to Sales Account
Purchase ₦5,000 credited to supplier, not debited to Purchases Account
Cash received ₦8,000 debited to Cash, not credited anywhere

Effect:
Trial balance won't agree (one side more than the other)
Correction:
Complete the omitted entry

TYPE 2: ERROR OF CASTING (ADDITION)

Definition:
Mistake in totaling/adding figures in an account.

Examples:
Total of Sales Day Book wrong
Total of Purchases Day Book wrong
Wrong total in Cash Book

Sub-types:
Overcast - Total more than it should be
Undercast - Total less than it should be

Effect:
Trial balance won't agree

Correction:
Adjust the account with wrong total

TYPE 3: ERROR OF POSTING TO WRONG SIDE

Definition:
Entry made on wrong side of an account.

Examples:
Debtor payment ₦5,000 debited instead of credited to debtor
Purchase ₦3,000 credited instead of debited to Purchases

Effect:
Double the error amount in difference
Trial balance won't agree

Correction:
Cancel wrong entry
Make correct entry
Or adjust by double the amount

TYPE 4: ERROR OF POSTING WRONG AMOUNT

Definition:
Correct account but wrong amount posted to one side.

Examples:
₦7,000 purchase debited to Purchases but ₦700 credited to Supplier
₦5,000 sale credited to Sales but ₦50,000 debited to Customer

Effect:
Trial balance won't agree

Correction:
Adjust the account with wrong amount

TYPE 5: ERROR OF OMISSION (COMPLETE)

Definition:
Transaction completely omitted from books
(Not recorded at all - both debit and credit missing)

Example:
Purchase ₦10,000 not recorded anywhere

Effect:
DOES NOT affect trial balance (both sides equally missing) But accounts are incomplete!

Correction:
Record the complete transaction

ERRORS NOT AFFECTING TRIAL BALANCE
TYPE 1: ERROR OF COMMISSION

Definition:
Entry made in wrong account but correct class and correct side.

Examples:
Payment to Ade ₦5,000 debited to John's Account (both personal)
Rent ₦3,000 debited to Rates Account (both expenses)
Sales to Bola ₦8,000 credited to Tunde's Account (both debtors)

Characteristics:
Wrong account
Same class (personal to personal, expense to expense)
Correct side (Dr or Cr)
Correct amount

Effect:
Trial balance WILL agree (double entry maintained) But wrong accounts affected!

Correction:
Transfer from wrong account to correct account

TYPE 2: ERROR OF PRINCIPLE

Definition:
Entry violates fundamental accounting principles. Wrong classification of account.

Examples:
Purchase of machinery ₦100,000 debited to Purchases Account (Should be Asset, but treated as Expense)
Repairs ₦5,000 debited to Machinery Account
(Should be Expense, but treated as Asset - Capitalizing Revenue)
Revenue receipt treated as capital receipt

Characteristics:
Wrong classification
Capital vs Revenue confusion
Asset vs Expense confusion

Effect:
Trial balance WILL agree
But financial statements will be wrong!

Correction:
Transfer to correct class of account

TYPE 3: ERROR OF COMPLETE OMISSION

Definition:
Transaction completely not recorded (both Dr and Cr)

Example:
Sold goods ₦20,000 - no entry made at all

Effect:
Trial balance WILL agree (nothing recorded on either side) But accounts incomplete!

Correction:
Record the complete transaction

TYPE 4: COMPENSATING ERROR

Definition:
Two or more errors that cancel each other out

Examples:
Sales overcast by ₦1,000 (extra credit) Purchases overcast by ₦1,000 (extra debit) Net effect = Zero

Debtor undercast by ₦5,000 (₦5,000 less debit) Creditor undercast by ₦5,000 (₦5,000 less credit) Net effect = Zero

Effect:
Trial balance WILL agree (errors compensate) But individual accounts are wrong!

Correction:
Correct each error separately

TYPE 5: ERROR OF ORIGINAL ENTRY

Definition:
Wrong amount recorded in book of original entry, then correctly posted from there.

Example:
Invoice for ₦7,000 recorded as ₦700 in Day Book
Both debit and credit are ₦700 (wrong amount but consistently applied)

Effect:
Trial balance WILL agree
But amount is wrong in both accounts!

Correction:
Adjust both affected accounts

TYPE 6: REVERSAL OF ENTRIES

Definition:
Correct accounts but sides reversed (Debits and credits interchanged)

Example:
Paid rent ₦5,000
Wrong: Dr. Cash ₦5,000, Cr. Rent ₦5,000 Right: Dr. Rent ₦5,000, Cr. Cash ₦5,000

Effect:
Trial balance WILL agree
But both accounts show opposite of correct!

Correction:
Reverse both entries and record correctly Or make adjustment of double the amount

SUSPENSE ACCOUNT
SUSPENSE ACCOUNT:
A temporary account used when trial balance doesn't agree and the difference cannot be immediately located.

Purpose:
Make trial balance agree temporarily
Allow preparation of financial statements
Hold difference until error is found

When Created:
Trial balance doesn't balance
Difference can't be quickly found
Need to proceed with accounts

How Created:
If Debits > Credits: Credit Suspense Account If Credits > Debits: Debit Suspense Account

Example:
Trial Balance Totals:
Debit: ₦500,000 Credit: ₦495,000
Difference: ₦5,000 (Debits more)

Entry to Balance:
Dr. (No entry)
Cr. Suspense Account ₦5,000 Now both sides = ₦500,000

CLEARING SUSPENSE ACCOUNT:

When errors are found, correct them.
Suspense Account will be debited or credited until its balance becomes zero.

If suspense account doesn't clear to zero, there are still undetected errors!

CORRECTING ERRORS USING JOURNAL ENTRIES
PRINCIPLES OF CORRECTION:

IDENTIFY THE ERROR What was done wrong?

IDENTIFY THE CORRECTION NEEDED What should have been done?

CANCEL THE WRONG ENTRY Reverse what was wrongly done

MAKE THE CORRECT ENTRY
Record what should have been done

OR

USE DIRECT CORRECTION:
One entry that fixes everything

EXAMPLE 1: Error of Commission

Wrong: Paid ₦5,000 to Ade, debited to John Right: Should be debited to Ade

Correction Journal Entry:

(Being correction of payment wrongly debited to John)

Correction Journal Entry:

Dr. Suspense Account	₦10,000
Cr. Sales Account	₦10,000 (Being omitted credit to Sales Account)

EXAMPLE 4: Overcast

Wrong: Sales Day Book overcast by ₦2,000 Effect: Sales credited ₦2,000 extra

Correction Journal Entry:
Dr. Sales Account	₦2,000
Cr. Suspense Account	₦2,000
(Being correction of overcast in Sales Day Book)

EXAMPLE 5: Wrong Side Posting

Wrong: Received ₦8,000 from debtor, credited to debtor instead of debited Effect: Should reduce debt but increased it

Correction Journal Entry:
Dr. Debtor's Account	₦16,000
Cr. Suspense Account	₦16,000
(Being correction of amount posted to wrong side - adjust by double)

Explanation: ₦8,000 wrongly credited = ₦8,000 + ₦8,000 correct debit =
₦16,000

EXAMPLE 6: Error of Original Entry

Wrong: Invoice ₦7,500 recorded as ₦750 throughout Effect: Both Dr and Cr short by ₦6,750

Correction Journal Entry:
Dr. Customer's Account	₦6,750 Cr. Sales Account	₦6,750 (Being correction of understatement)

EXAMPLE 7: Compensating Errors

Wrong: Sales overcast ₦3,000; Purchases overcast ₦3,000 Effect: Both need reducing

Correction Journal Entries:
Entry 1:
Dr. Sales Account	₦3,000
Cr. Suspense Account	₦3,000 (Being correction of overcast in Sales)

Entry 2:
Dr. Suspense Account	₦3,000
Cr. Purchases Account	₦3,000
(Being correction of overcast in Purchases)

Net Effect on Suspense: Zero (as expected for compensating error)

MEANING OF TRADING ACCOUNT
TRADING ACCOUNT:

Key Points:

DEFINITIONS:

Excess of sales over cost of goods sold Sales > Cost of Goods Sold

GROSS LOSS:
Excess of cost of goods sold over sales Cost of Goods Sold > Sales

COST OF GOODS SOLD (COGS):
Total cost of goods actually sold during the period
= Opening Stock + Purchases + Direct Expenses - Closing Stock

PROFIT AND LOSS ACCOUNT:
An account prepared to determine the net profit or net loss of a business by comparing all revenues with all expenses.

Overall profit of business

CLOSING STOCK:
Value of goods unsold at the end of accounting period.

Why Adjust:
Not sold yet, so not part of COGS
Represents asset
Must be valued and recorded

LOWER OF COST OR NET REALIZABLE VALUE (NRV)

Cost = Purchase price + Direct expenses NRV = Selling price - Expenses to make sale

Example:

Stock cost: ₦50,000 NRV: ₦60,000
Value in accounts: ₦50,000 (lower)

Already recorded
Show ONLY in Balance Sheet as asset

If closing stock given as adjustment (not in trial balance):
Must record it
Show in TWO places:
Trading Account (deduct from COGS)
Balance Sheet (as current asset)

DEPRECIATION
DEPRECIATION:
The systematic allocation of the cost of a fixed asset over its useful life.

Why Provide:
Assets lose value over time
Match cost with revenue generated
Show true value of assets
Apply accrual concept
Comply with prudence concept

Causes of Depreciation:
Wear and tear (usage)
Obsolescence (outdated)
Passage of time
Depletion (for natural resources)

METHODS OF DEPRECIATION:

STRAIGHT LINE METHOD (Fixed Installment) Same amount charged each year
Formula:
Annual Depreciation = (Cost - Salvage Value) ÷ Useful Life

Or:
Annual Depreciation = Cost × Rate%

Example:
Asset cost: ₦100,000 Salvage value: ₦10,000 Useful life: 10 years

Annual depreciation = (₦100,000 - ₦10,000) ÷ 10
= ₦9,000 per year

REDUCING BALANCE METHOD (Diminishing Balance) Depreciation calculated on reducing book value Higher depreciation in early years

Formula:
Depreciation = Book Value × Rate%

Example:
Asset cost: ₦100,000 Rate: 20% per annum

Year 1: ₦100,000 × 20% = ₦20,000
Year 2: (₦100,000 - ₦20,000) × 20% = ₦16,000 Year 3: (₦80,000 - ₦16,000) × 20% = ₦12,800

ACCOUNTING EQUATION
THE FUNDAMENTAL EQUATION: ASSETS = CAPITAL + LIABILITIES
Or rearranged:
CAPITAL = ASSETS - LIABILITIES
This equation MUST always balance! Explanation:
Assets = Resources owned by business
Capital = Owner's investment (equity)
Liabilities = Amounts owed to outsiders

The business uses capital and borrowed funds (liabilities) to acquire assets.

EXAMPLES:

Example 1:
Assets: ₦500,000 Liabilities: ₦200,000
Capital: ₦500,000 - ₦200,000 = ₦300,000 Check: ₦500,000 = ₦300,000 + ₦200,000 ✓

Example 2:
Capital: ₦400,000 Liabilities: ₦150,000
Assets: ₦400,000 + ₦150,000 = ₦550,000 Check: ₦550,000 = ₦400,000 + ₦150,000 ✓

EFFECT OF TRANSACTIONS:

Transaction: Owner introduces additional capital ₦50,000 Effect:
Assets (Cash) increase by ₦50,000
Capital increases by ₦50,000 Equation remains balanced ✓

Transaction: Bought goods on credit ₦30,000 Effect:
Assets (Stock) increase by ₦30,000
Liabilities (Creditors) increase by ₦30,000 Equation remains balanced ✓

Transaction: Paid creditor ₦20,000 Effect:
Assets (Cash) decrease by ₦20,000
Liabilities (Creditors) decrease by ₦20,000 Equation remains balanced ✓

COMMON ERRORS TO AVOID
ERROR 1: Wrong classification
✗ Putting stock under fixed assets
Stock is a current asset

ERROR 2: Wrong order
✗ Listing cash before stock
Stock → Debtors → Bank → Cash (liquidity order)

ERROR 3: Forgetting depreciation
✗ Showing fixed assets at cost only
Show cost less accumulated depreciation

ERROR 4: Wrong capital calculation
✗ Capital = Opening + Profit (forgetting drawings)
Capital = Opening + Profit - Drawings + Additional

ERROR 5: Bank overdraft as asset
✗ Showing overdraft with bank on asset side
Bank overdraft is always a liability

ERROR 6: Not deducting provision from debtors
✗ Showing debtors gross without deducting provision
Debtors less provision = net amount

ERROR 7: Missing equation check
✗ Not verifying Assets = Capital + Liabilities
Always check the equation balances

ERROR 8: Wrong date format
✗ "For the year ended..." (that's for P&L)
"As at [date]" (specific point in time)

ERROR 9: Including nominal accounts
✗ Showing expenses/income in balance sheet
Only real accounts and capital in balance sheet

ERROR 10: Poor presentation
✗ No headings, messy format
Clear headings, neat lines, proper totals

MEANING OF DEPRECIATION
DEPRECIATION:
The systematic allocation of the cost of a fixed asset over its useful life.

OR

The decrease in value of a fixed asset due to use, passage of time, or obsolescence.

Key Points:
Applies to fixed assets only (not current assets)
Non-cash expense (no money paid out)
Accounting estimate (not exact science)
Reduces asset value on balance sheet
Charged as expense in P&L Account
Matches cost with revenue (accrual concept)

Assets that Depreciate:
Buildings
Machinery
Motor Vehicles
Furniture
Equipment
Computers
Tools

Assets that DON'T Depreciate:
✗ Land (usually appreciates)
✗ Stock/Inventory (different treatment)
✗ Investments (market value changes)
✗ Antiques (may appreciate)

CAUSES OF DEPRECIATION
WEAR AND TEAR (PHYSICAL DETERIORATION)
Normal use damages asset
Moving parts wear out
Physical breakdown

Example:
Motor vehicle engine wears out from use Furniture gets scratched and damaged Machinery parts break down

OBSOLESCENCE (TECHNOLOGICAL/ECONOMIC)
New technology makes asset outdated
Better models available
Asset still works but less efficient

Example:
Old computer replaced by faster model Manual machine vs automated machine Old software becomes obsolete

PASSAGE OF TIME
Asset loses value with age
Even if not used
Natural deterioration

Example:
Leather seats crack with age Rubber parts perish
Paint fades

DEPLETION (FOR NATURAL RESOURCES)
Extraction/usage reduces quantity
Applies to mines, quarries, oil wells

Example:
Coal mine gets exhausted Oil wells dry up
Quarry stones extracted

EFFLUXION OF TIME (LEGAL RIGHTS)
Time-limited assets
Leases, patents, licenses
Expire after period

Example:
10-year lease expires Patent protection ends License needs renewal

INADEQUACY
Asset no longer suitable
Business outgrows asset
Insufficient capacity

Example:
Small delivery van for growing business Old server can't handle traffic Limited production capacity

ACCIDENTS AND DAMAGE
Unexpected damage
Reduces asset value
May be sudden

Example:
Vehicle accident
Fire damage to building Equipment breakdown

REASONS FOR PROVIDING DEPRECIATION
WHY DEPRECIATE ASSETS?

APPLY ACCRUAL CONCEPT
Match expenses with revenue
Charge cost over useful life
Not all in year of purchase

Example:
Machine costs ₦100,000, lasts 10 years Charge ₦10,000 expense each year

SHOW TRUE PROFIT
Without depreciation, profit overstated
Using asset has a cost
Must be recognized

SHOW REALISTIC ASSET VALUE
Assets don't retain original cost forever

Fair presentation

Example:

PROVIDE FOR REPLACEMENT
Build up funds for new asset
When old asset wears out
Money available for replacement

Legal obligation

STRAIGHT LINE METHOD
STRAIGHT LINE METHOD:
Equal amount of depreciation charged each year throughout the asset's useful life.

Also called:
Fixed Installment Method
Equal Installment Method
Original Cost Method Formula:
ANNUAL DEPRECIATION = (COST - SCRAP VALUE) ÷ USEFUL LIFE OR
ANNUAL DEPRECIATION = COST × RATE%

Where:
Cost = Purchase price of asset
Scrap Value = Estimated value at end of life
(Also called Residual/Salvage Value) Useful Life = Estimated years of use

CHARACTERISTICS:
Same amount each year
Simple to calculate
Easy to understand
Asset value reduces equally
Reaches zero (or scrap value) at end

ADVANTAGES:
Simple and easy
Equal annual charge
Suitable for assets with steady use
Easy to understand
Predictable expense

DISADVANTAGES:
✗ Ignores pattern of use
✗ Assumes equal benefit each year
✗ May not match actual wear
✗ Repairs increase with age (cost not matched)

BEST SUITED FOR:
Buildings
Furniture
Leasehold property
Patents
Copyrights
Assets with steady use

CONTENT:
MEANING OF REDUCING BALANCE METHOD
REDUCING BALANCE METHOD:
A method where depreciation is calculated on the reducing (diminishing) book value of the asset each year.

Also called:
Diminishing Balance Method
Written Down Value Method
Declining Balance Method

Principle:
Each year's depreciation is calculated on the NET BOOK VALUE (not original cost)

Net Book Value = Cost - Accumulated Depreciation to date

Formula:

YEAR 1: Depreciation = Cost × Rate%

YEAR 2: Depreciation = (Cost - Year 1 Depreciation) × Rate%

YEAR 3: Depreciation = (Cost - Year 1 - Year 2 Depreciation) × Rate% OR
EACH YEAR: Depreciation = Opening NBV × Rate%

KEY FEATURES:
Decreasing annual charge
Higher depreciation in early years
Lower depreciation in later years
Never reaches zero value
Always a residual value remains

CHARACTERISTICS OF REDUCING BALANCE METHOD
PATTERN OF DEPRECIATION:

Year 1: Highest depreciation Year 2: Lower than Year 1
Year 3: Lower than Year 2
...and so on (decreasing each year)

EXAMPLE VISUALIZATION:

Asset Cost: ₦100,000 Rate: 20%

Year 1: ₦100,000 × 20% = ₦20,000 ████████████████████ Year 2: ₦80,000 × 20% = ₦16,000 ████████████████ Year 3: ₦64,000 × 20% = ₦12,800 ████████████
Year 4: ₦51,200 × 20% = ₦10,240 ██████████ Year 5: ₦40,960 × 20% = ₦8,192 ████████

Depreciation decreases each year!

COMPARISON WITH STRAIGHT LINE:

Straight Line = Equal bars (same height)
Reducing Balance = Decreasing bars (getting shorter)

WHY IT NEVER REACHES ZERO:
Each year, you take a % of remaining value Always leaving some value behind

Example:
₦100 × 20% = ₦20 (leaves ₦80)
₦80 × 20% = ₦16 (leaves ₦64)
₦64 × 20% = ₦12.80 (leaves ₦51.20)
...continues indefinitely

ADVANTAGES AND DISADVANTAGES
ADVANTAGES OF REDUCING BALANCE METHOD:

REALISTIC DEPRECIATION PATTERN
Higher when asset is new and efficient
Lower when asset is old and less efficient
Matches actual value loss

MATCHES COSTS WITH REVENUE
New asset = High depreciation + Low repairs
Old asset = Low depreciation + High repairs
Total cost more even each year

TAX BENEFITS
Higher depreciation early = Lower tax early
Tax deferral advantage
Cash flow benefit

FASTER WRITE-OFF
More depreciation in early years
Reflects rapid obsolescence
Good for technology assets

CONSERVATIVE APPROACH
Prudent accounting
Lower profits initially
Reduces risk of overvaluation

DISADVANTAGES OF REDUCING BALANCE METHOD:

COMPLEX CALCULATION
✗ Different amount each year
✗ More difficult to compute
✗ Requires calculation each period

NEVER REACHES ZERO
✗ Always leaves residual value
✗ May not match actual scrap value
✗ Continues indefinitely

VARIABLE PROFIT
✗ Higher profit in later years
✗ Makes comparison difficult
✗ Less consistent results

DIFFICULT TO DETERMINE RATE
✗ Hard to calculate exact rate
✗ Requires complex formula
✗ Often approximated

NOT SUITABLE FOR ALL ASSETS
✗ Poor for buildings (steady use)
✗ Not good for leasehold property
✗ Limited applicability

BEST SUITED FOR:
Motor Vehicles
Computers and IT equipment
Machinery with technological obsolescence
Equipment with high early usage
Assets losing value quickly

WHEN TO USE EACH METHOD
STRAIGHT LINE METHOD - Use for:
Buildings
Furniture
Leasehold property
Patents and copyrights
Assets with predictable lifespan
Assets with steady usage pattern
When simplicity is important

Example:
Office building depreciates steadily over time
Use straight line: ₦10M over 50 years = ₦200,000/year

REDUCING BALANCE METHOD - Use for:
Motor Vehicles
Computers
Mobile phones
Machinery
Technology equipment
Assets with rapid obsolescence
Assets with high early usage

Example:
New car loses value quickly in first years
Use reducing balance: Higher depreciation initially

FACTORS TO CONSIDER:

PATTERN OF USE
Steady → Straight Line
Intensive early use → Reducing Balance

OBSOLESCENCE RATE Slow → Straight Line
Fast → Reducing Balance

REPAIRS AND MAINTENANCE
If repairs increase with age → Reducing Balance (Balances total cost)

MANAGEMENT PREFERENCE
Consistent profit → Straight Line Tax deferral → Reducing Balance

ASSET TYPE
Building → Straight Line Technology → Reducing Balance

COMMON MISTAKES TO AVOID
ERROR 1: Including non-factory items
✗ Office rent in Manufacturing Account
Only factory rent in Manufacturing Account Office rent goes to P&L

ERROR 2: Mixing up stocks
✗ Finished goods stock in Manufacturing Account
Finished goods in Trading Account
Raw materials in Manufacturing Account

ERROR 3: Wrong treatment of purchases
✗ Including all purchases together
Raw material purchases → Manufacturing A/c Finished goods purchases (if any) → Trading A/c

ERROR 4: Forgetting WIP
✗ Not adjusting for Work-in-Progress
Add opening WIP, deduct closing WIP

ERROR 5: Including selling expenses
✗ Sales commission in Manufacturing Account
Sales commission in P&L Account Only factory costs in Manufacturing

ERROR 6: Wrong calculation of RM consumed
✗ Just using purchases
Opening + Purchases - Closing = Consumed

ERROR 7: Not transferring to Trading Account
✗ Leaving factory cost isolated
Transfer to Trading Account as cost of production

KEY POINTS TO REMEMBER:
BALANCE SHEET:
Shows financial position at a point in time
Assets = Capital + Liabilities (must balance)
Fixed assets shown at cost less depreciation
Working Capital = Current Assets - Current Liabilities
Two formats: Horizontal and Vertical

CONTROL ACCOUNTS:
Sales Ledger Control = Total Debtors (Asset)
Purchases Ledger Control = Total Creditors (Liability)
Sales Control has debit balance normally
Purchases Control has credit balance normally
Used for quick information and error detection

DEPRECIATION:
Straight Line = Equal amounts annually
Reducing Balance = Decreasing amounts annually
Straight Line on original cost
Reducing Balance on reducing book value
Provision method better than direct write-off

MANUFACTURING ACCOUNT:
Shows cost of producing goods
Prime Cost = Direct Materials + Direct Labour + Direct Expenses
Factory Cost = Prime Cost + Factory Overheads
Adjust for Work-in-Progress
Result transferred to Trading Account
