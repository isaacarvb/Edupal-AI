SPOKEN ENGLISH: ORAL COMPOSITION SKILLS I (NARRATIVE) Meaning of Oral Composition
Oral composition is the act of speaking to express thoughts, feelings, and experiences in an organized and fluent manner. It involves constructing sentences and paragraphs in speech form
that is, speaking in a logical order to make meaning clear to the listener.

Meaning of Narrative Composition

A narrative composition is a story or account of events, either real or imagined. It tells what happened, how it happened, when, and to whom. Narratives can be personal experiences, folk tales, legends, or imaginary stories.

Features of a Good Narrative

Title: Reflects the main idea of the story.
Example: “The Day I Got Lost,” “My First Day in Boarding School.”
Introduction: Introduces the setting and main characters.
Body: Describes the sequence of events in clear order.
Climax: The most exciting or important part of the story.
Conclusion: The end of the story, showing what was learned.

Language Features of Narratives

Use of past tense verbs: went, saw, heard, felt, ran.
Use of connectors for sequencing: first, then, later, afterwards, suddenly, finally.
Use of descriptive words: beautiful, noisy, frightened, bright, terrible.
Use of direct speech to make it lively:
“Stop!” my mother shouted.

Illustration / Example

Topic: A Day I Will Never Forget

Meaning of Number in Nouns

“Number” in grammar shows whether we are referring to one (singular) or more than one (plural) item.

Singular Noun: Refers to one item. Example: boy, table, apple.

READING COMPREHENSION Topic: Word Meanings through Context Meaning of Context
In reading, context refers to all the words, phrases, sentences, and even the surrounding paragraphs that come before and after an unfamiliar word. It's the "neighborhood" the word lives in.

This skill is one of the most powerful tools a reader has. Instead of stopping to use a dictionary for every new word (which breaks your reading flow), you can use clues from the text to make an intelligent guess—an inference—about its meaning. This builds reading speed, confidence, and vocabulary.

Techniques for Getting Word Meanings through Context

We can use several types of clues, often called context clues, to unlock the meaning of a word.
Definition or Explanation Clues

The text directly provides the definition or an explanation of the word.

Signal Words: Look for words and punctuation like is, are, means, is called, or, that is, in other words, as well as commas (,), dashes (—), or parentheses ( ).
Example 1: The anthology, which is a collection of poems, was beautifully bound.
Example 2: Semantics, the study of word meanings, is a fascinating branch of linguistics.
Example 3: A pandemic—a disease that spreads across the entire world—changed life for everyone.
Synonym or Restatement Clues

A more familiar word or phrase with a similar meaning is used nearby.
Signal Words: Look for words like or, that is, in other words. Sometimes, the synonym is just used in the same sentence without a signal.
Example 1: The boy was elated, or very happy, after winning the prize.
Example 2: He was known for his timorous nature; he was scared of almost everything.
Example 3: She found the task arduous and difficult to complete.
Antonym or Contrast Clues

The text provides a word or phrase with the opposite meaning, which helps you understand the unfamiliar word.

Signal Words: Look for words like but, however, unlike, on the other hand, whereas, not.
Example 1: Unlike his gloomy sister, John is always cheerful. (Gloomy must mean the opposite of cheerful, so sad or unhappy.)
Example 2: Some students were diligent in their studies, but others were simply lazy. (Diligent must mean the opposite of lazy, so hardworking.)
Example 3: I expected a cacophony, however, the music was surprisingly harmonious. (Cacophony must mean a harsh, unharmonious sound.)
Example Clues

The text gives examples that help illustrate the meaning of the word.

Signal Words: Look for words like such as, for example, including, like.
Example 1: Nigerian delicacies such as jollof rice, moi-moi, and pounded yam are delicious. (Delicacies must mean special, tasty foods.)
Example 2: Celestial bodies, including the sun, moon, and stars, have fascinated humans for centuries. (Celestial must mean related to the sky or outer space.)
Example 3: He enjoys aquatic sports like swimming and water polo. (Aquatic must mean
related to water.)
Inference or General Context Clues

The reader must use reasoning and the information in the surrounding text to figure out the word's meaning. This is the most common clue type.
Example 1: After walking for hours under the hot sun, she was completely exhausted. (The situation—walking for hours in the heat—leads you to infer that exhausted means very tired.)
Example 2: The culprit was identified by the police from the CCTV footage and was promptly arrested. (The words "police," "identified," and "arrested" help you infer that culprit means the person guilty of a crime.)
Example 3: The classroom was in disarray; books were on the floor, chairs were overturned, and a broken window let in the rain. (The description of the room helps you infer that disarray means a state of untidiness or chaos.)

Reading Practice

Read the passage below. Using the context clues, determine the meanings of the words in bold.

The annual village festival was a boisterous event. Unlike the quiet, sombre mood of the previous month following the drought, this celebration was full of life. The air was filled with the aroma of roasting yams and simmering stew. Children ran everywhere, their shouts and laughter adding to the happy clamour. The elders, remembering past celebrations, prayed that the prolific harvest would continue for many years to come.

Boisterous: The passage says the event was "full of life" and filled with "shouts and laughter." This is an inference clue. Boisterous means noisy, energetic, and cheerful.
Sombre: The passage uses the antonym clue "Unlike" to contrast this word with the "boisterous" (happy, lively) event. Sombre must mean dark, dull, or sad.
Aroma: This word is followed by the example clues "of roasting yams and simmering stew."
Aroma means a pleasant smell, especially from food.
Clamour: The text mentions "shouts and laughter" as part of the clamour. This is an inference clue. Clamour means a loud and confused noise.
Prolific: The elders "prayed that the... harvest would continue." This implies the harvest was very good. Prolific means producing a great quantity; plentiful.

Meaning of Listening Comprehension
Listening comprehension is an active skill. It is not just hearing sounds. It is the ability to understand, interpret, evaluate, and remember spoken language. This includes identifying main ideas, specific details, the speaker's tone, and any underlying messages or moral lessons.

Listening Activity: Identifying National Values

National Values are the shared beliefs and principles that are considered important for the well- being of a nation. These include honesty, integrity, discipline, unity, patriotism, and respect for elders.

"Go and plant it. In six months, whoever brings me the most impressive harvest will receive a great bounty and be declared 'Farmer of theYear'."

Adewale, a young farmer known for his integrity, received his seeds. He planted them, watered them, and tended the soil, but nothing grew. His pot remained empty. He watched in despair as his neighbours boasted of their tall, green stalks of corn.

✍ D. CONTINUOUS WRITING

Topic: Narrative Composition – "My First Day at College"

Meaning of Narrative Writing

A narrative composition (or narrative essay) is a piece of writing that tells a story. The story can be a real-life experience (non-fiction) or one that you have imagined (fiction).

The main purpose of narrative writing is to entertain, share an experience, or teach a lesson. A good narrative is interesting, organized, and makes the reader feel like they are partof the story.

Steps and Features of a Good Narrative

A narrative essay is typically structured with a clear beginning, middle, and end.

The Introduction (The Beginning)

Goal: To "hook" the reader and introduce the setting (where/when) and main characters (who).
Techniques:
Action: Start with an exciting event. (e.g., The alarm blared, jolting me from a dream.)
Dialogue: Start with someone speaking. (e.g., "Wake up! You'll be late for your first day!" my mother shouted.)
Description: Paint a vivid picture. (e.g., The new, stiff cotton of my college uniform scratched my neck.)
Feeling: Start with a strong emotion. (e.g., My stomach was a knot of excitement and terror.)
The Body (The Middle)

Goal: To develop the story and build up to the main event. This is where most of the action happens.
Features:
Chronological Order: Events are told in the order they happened (first, next, then, later, finally).
Sensory Details: Use the five senses (sight, sound, smell, taste, touch) to make the story "real."
Vivid Verbs & Adjectives: Use strong words. (Don't say "walk," say "trudged," "raced," or "strolled.")
Conflict: Introduce a problem or a challenge (e.g., getting lost, meeting a bully, feeling homesick).
The Climax

Goal: This is the peak of the story, the most exciting or important moment, the "turning point." The main conflict is faced here.
Example: For "My First Day," the climax could be mistakenly entering the SS3 classroom, or finally meeting a kind friend.
The Conclusion (The End)

Goal: To wrap up the story and give the reader a sense of completion.
Techniques:
Reflection: State what you learned or how you felt afterwards. (e.g., I learned that day that being brave wasn't about not being_...)
Feeling: End with the final emotion you had. (e.g., As I lay in bed that night, I smiled, knowing I was finally a college student.)
Full Circle: Link back to the introduction.

Sample Outline: "My First Day at College"

Title: My First Day at College

E. GRAMMAR Topic: Nouns and Gender
Meaning of Gender

In English grammar, gender is a way of classifying nouns as masculine (male), feminine (female), common (either male or female), or neuter (no gender).

It is important to note that grammatical gender in English is natural—it is based on the real- world sex of the object, unlike in languages like French or German where even inanimate objects are "male" or "female."

THE FOUR GENDERS IN ENGLISH

Masculine Gender

Refers to male beings (people or animals).

Examples: man, boy, uncle, king, father, bull, actor, prince, husband, widower, stallion, wizard, fox, groom.

Feminine Gender

Refers to female beings (people or animals).

Examples: woman, girl, aunt, queen, mother, cow, actress, princess, wife, widow, mare, witch, vixen, bride.

Common Gender

Refers to beings that can be either male or female.

Examples: teacher, child, doctor, student, cousin, friend, parent, baby, monarch, person, relative, singer, artist, driver.

Neuter Gender

Refers to non-living things, objects, or ideas that have no gender.

Examples: table, pen, book, car, stone, chair, school, computer.
Note: Abstract nouns (e.g., love, honesty, fear, happiness) are also neuter gender.

Topic: Selected Consonant Sounds (The Fricatives: /θ/, /ð/, /ʃ/, /ʒ/, /z/)

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

Identify the five consonant sounds: /θ/, /ð/, /ʃ/, /ʒ/, and /z/.

Describe the correct production of each sound (place and manner of articulation).

Distinguish between voiced and voiceless sounds.

Recognize these sounds in different word positions (initial, medial, final).

Accurately pronounce words containing these sounds in isolation and in sentences.

Introduction to Fricatives

The sounds we are studying today are called fricatives. A fricative is a consonant sound produced by forcing air through a narrow gap so that a hissing or friction sound is produced.

These sounds are often difficult for Nigerian speakers of English because many of our local languages do not use the same tongue and teeth positions. For example, we often replace:

/θ/ (as in "think") with /t/ (as in "tink")

/ð/ (as in "this") with /d/ (as in "dis")

/ʃ/ (as in "she") with /s/ (as in "see")

This lesson will teach you how to produce them correctly.

Sound Descriptions and Production
The /θ/ Sound (Voiceless Dental Fricative)

Description: This is a voiceless sound. If you touch your throat (Adam's apple), you should feel
no vibration. It is dental because your tongue touches your teeth.
How to Produce:
Place the very tip of your tongue lightly between your upper and lower front teeth (or just behind the upper teeth).
Breathe out, forcing air to pass between your tongue and teeth.

Do NOT vibrate your vocal cords. It should sound like a soft "hiss."

Common Spellings: th
Word Examples:

Initial: think, thin, theme, three, thief, thunder
Medial: author, bathroom, method, pathetic, nothing
Final: bath, mouth, path, cloth, breath, fourth

The /ð/ Sound (Voiced Dental Fricative)

Description: This is a voiced sound. If you touch your throat, you must feel a vibration. It is the "sister sound" to /θ/.
How to Produce:
Place your tongue in the exact same position as /θ/ (tip between the teeth).
As you force air out, hum or vibrate your vocal cords. It should make a "buzzing" sound.
Common Spellings: th
Word Examples:

Initial: this, that, the, them, then, though
Medial: mother, father, brother, weather, other
Final: breathe, bathe, cloothe, suithe (Note: This sound is rare at the very end of words).

Key Contrast:
breath /breθ/ (noun) - no voice
breathe /briːð/ (verb) - with voice
bath /bæθ/ (noun) - no voice
bathe /beɪð/ (verb) - with voice

The /ʃ/ Sound (Voiceless Palato-alveolar Fricative)

Description: This is a voiceless sound (no vibration). It is the "be quiet" sound.
How to Produce:

Countable Nouns (Count Nouns)

Definition: Countable nouns are nouns that represent individual items that can be counted as separate units. They have both a singular and a plural form.

Singular Form: Refers to one item.
Example: a book, one chair, an idea, a student
Plural Form: Refers to more than one item (usually formed by adding -s, -es, or -ies).
Example: books, chairs, ideas, students, watches, cities
Irregular Plurals: Some common nouns have irregular plural forms.
Example: man $\rightarrow$ men, woman $\rightarrow$ women, child $\rightarrow$ children, foot $\rightarrow$ feet, tooth $\rightarrow$ teeth, person $\rightarrow$ people, mouse
$\rightarrow$ mice

Key Usage Rules:

In their singular form, they must have a determiner before them (e.g., a, an, the, my, this).
Incorrect: I saw bird fly.
Correct: I saw a bird fly. / I saw that bird fly.
They work with quantifiers that imply number.

Quantifiers: many, few, a few, several, a number of, these, those.
Example: Many students attended the lecture.
Example: We have a few chairs to spare.

Uncountable Nouns (Mass Nouns)

Definition: Uncountable nouns refer to substances, materials, concepts, or masses that cannot be counted as individual items. They are seen as a whole or a mass.

They do not have a plural form. (We don't say "informations," "advices," or "furnitures").
They are always treated as singular in a sentence (they take a singular verb).
Example: The water is cold. (Not: The water are cold.)
Example: This information is vital. (Not: These information are vital.)

Common Categories of Uncountable Nouns:

Liquids/Gases: water, milk, oil, petrol, air, oxygen
Materials/Substances: wood, glass, paper, gold, rice, sugar, flour, cement
Abstract Ideas/Emotions: information, advice, news, knowledge, happiness, love, courage, fun
General Categories: furniture (a collection of chairs, tables, etc.), luggage/baggage (a collection of bags), money, traffic
Subjects/Activities: mathematics, physics, economics, politics, football, swimming

Key Usage Rules:

We cannot use a or an directly with them.
Incorrect: He gave me an advice.
To "count" them, we use partitives (units of measurement).
Correct: He gave me a piece of advice.
More examples:
a bottle of water
a grain of rice
a loaf of bread
a cup of tea
an item of news
a piece of furniture
They work with quantifiers that imply amount or quantity.

Quantifiers: much, little, a little, a lot of, plenty of, some, any.
Example: There isn’t much information on that topic.
Example: I need a little sugar for my tea.

Special Cases and Confusions

Some nouns can be tricky.

Plural Form, Singular Meaning: These nouns look plural but take a singular verb.
Examples: news, mathematics, physics, economics, politics, athletics, gymnastics.
Sentence: The news is on at 6 PM.
Sentence: Mathematics is an important subject.
Always Plural (Pluralia Tantum): These nouns are always plural and take a plural verb. They often refer to items with two parts.
Examples: trousers, scissors, spectacles (glasses), shorts, jeans, pliers, congratulations, thanks, premises.
Sentence: My trousers are new. (Not: My trouser is new.)
Sentence: The scissors are on the table.
To make them singular: We use "a pair of..." (e.g., "I bought a pair of trousers.")

Meaning of Narrative Writing

A narrative composition (or narrative essay) is a piece of writing that tells a story. The story can be a real-life experience (non-fiction) or one that you have imagined (fiction). Its main purpose is to entertain your reader and/or share a significant experience and the lesson you learned from it.

Features of a Good Narrative

Tense: Written mostly in the past tense (e.g., I saw, he ran, we were shouting, it had happened).
Structure: Events are arranged in chronological order (the order in which they happened).
Point of View: Usually written in the first person (using "I," "me," "we," "our").
Key Elements:
Characters: The people in the story (e.g., "I," "my mother," "the principal").
Setting: Where and when the story took place (e.g., "a hot afternoon on the football field").
Plot: The sequence of events (what happened). This includes an introduction, rising action, a
climax (the most exciting part), and a resolution.
Language: Uses...
Sensory Details: What you saw, heard, smelled, tasted, or felt.
Vivid Verbs: (e.g., "dashed" instead of "ran," "whispered" instead of "said").
Transition Words: (e.g., first, next, later, suddenly, finally).

Introduction: The /w/ Glide

The /w/ sound (as in "wet" or "wow") is a semi-vowel, also known as a glide. It's not a "full" consonant because air flows smoothly without friction, and it's not a vowel because it can't form a syllable on its own.

How to Produce /w/:

Round your lips tightly, pushing them forward (like you're about to say "oo").
Your tongue should be high and at the back of your mouth.

"Glide" from this position into the following vowel sound by unrounding your lips.

When /w/ follows another consonant (like /t/, /s/, /k/, /g/, /d/), it creates a consonant cluster. The key is to merge the two sounds. You should be rounding your lips for the /w/ at the same time as you are forming the first consonant.
Common Error: The most common mistake is pronouncing the two sounds separately (e.g., "tu- win" instead of twin) or ignoring the /w/ sound entirely.

Detailed Cluster Analysis

The /tw/ Cluster

Description: The voiceless alveolar stop /t/ followed by the /w/ glide.
How to Produce: Place the tip of your tongue on the alveolar ridge (just behind your top teeth) for the /t/. Before you release the /t/, round your lips for the /w/. Release them together as one sound.
Examples: twin, twelve, twenty, twist, twig, twilight, between.
The /dw/ Cluster

Description: The voiced alveolar stop /d/ followed by the /w/ glide.
How to Produce: Same position as /tw/, but you must use your vocal cords for the /d/ sound. Release the voiced /d/ and the /w/ glide together.
Examples: dwell, dwarf, dwindle, dwelling.
The /sw/ Cluster

Description: The voiceless alveolar fricative /s/ followed by the /w/ glide.
How to Produce: Start by making the /s/ sound (air hissing over the tongue). While you are making the /s/, round your lips for the /w/.
Examples: sweet, swim, sweep, switch, swear, swing, swan.
The /kw/ Cluster

Description: The voiceless velar stop /k/ (spelled c, k, or q) followed by the /w/ glide (spelled u or w). This sound is almost always spelled qu.
How to Produce: Raise the back of your tongue to the soft palate (velum) for the /k/. Before you release the /k/, round your lips for the /w/.
Examples: queen, quick, quiet, quake, quarry, question, equal, request.
The /gw/ Cluster

Description: The voiced velar stop /g/ followed by the /w/ glide.
How to Produce: Same position as /kw/, but you must use your vocal cords for the /g/ sound.
Examples: language, anguish, linguist, Gwen, gwava, penguin.

What is a Noun Phrase (NP)?

A noun is a single word (e.g., farmer, tractor).

A noun phrase is a group of words built around a noun that acts as a single unit. It is the noun plus all the words that describe it (modifiers).

The main noun is called the head noun.

farmer (Head Noun)
a farmer (Noun Phrase)
the tall farmer (Noun Phrase)
the hardworking farmer with the blue hat (A more complex Noun Phrase)

Structure: A noun phrase has a head noun and (optionally) modifiers before or after it. PRE-MODIFIERS + HEAD NOUN + POST-MODIFIERS
The + hardworking + farmer + in the field

The Definite Article: "The"
The word "the" is called the definite article. It is a determiner (a word that introduces a noun).

We use "the" when we are talking about a specific person, place, or thing that both the speaker and the listener know about.

"The" = You know which one I mean. "A/An" = A general one, not a specific one.
Compare:
"I need a hoe." (Any hoe will do.)
"I need the hoe." (The specific hoe that we both know about, maybe the one by the door.)

When to Use "The"

Specific Mention: When the item is specific and clear in the context.
Example: "Please pass me the cutlass." (We both see the cutlass).
Example: "The farmer is working." (We are talking about a specific farmer).
Second Mention: When you mention something for the second time.
Example: "I saw a tractor. The tractor was red."
Unique Items: When there is only one of something in the world or in our context.
Example: The sun, the moon, the sky, the internet, the principal (of our school).
Superlatives: Used with adjectives that show the most (e.g., -est, most).
Example: The tallest boy, the most hardworking farmer.
With some Proper Nouns: Used with names of rivers, oceans, and groups of countries.
Example: The River Niger, The Atlantic Ocean, The United Kingdom.

Functions of Noun Phrases

A noun phrase can do any job a single noun can do.

As Subject: (Performs the action)
[The hardworking farmer] won a prize.
[The old tractor in the shed] is broken.
As Object: (Receives the action)
The man repaired [the broken plough].
We saw [the beautiful farm].
As Complement: (Follows a verb like "is," "was," "are" to rename or describe the subject)
My father is [the manager].
This is [the best hoe for the job].

Topic: Grasping Main Ideas and Differentiating from Supporting Details

Learning Objectives

Introduction to the Skill

Listening comprehension is the active process of receiving, interpreting, and understanding spoken messages. The single most important skill within this is grasping the main idea.

The main idea is the central, most important point the speaker is trying to make. It's the "big picture" or the "so what?" of the message.

Supporting details, on the other hand, are the specific pieces of information, examples, statistics, stories, or facts that are used to prove, explain, or illustrate the main idea.

Analogy: A Table

The Main Idea is the flat tabletop. It's the one thing that holds everything together and provides the main surface.
The Supporting Details are the legs of the table. They hold up the tabletop, giving it strength and proving its existence. You need the legs, but the purpose of the table is the tabletop.
Strategies for Identifying Main Ideas

To become an active listener, you must listen for something. Listen for these clues:

Listen for the Topic Sentence: In formal speeches or lectures, the main idea is often stated directly at the beginning (an introduction) or at the end (a summary).
Listen for Repetition: Speakers will often repeat key words, phrases, or concepts related to their main point. If you hear "The key issue is..." or "What's most important is...," pay attention.
Listen for Signal Words: Transition words can tell you what's important.
Main Idea Signals: In summary, therefore, in conclusion, the point is, above all...
Supporting Detail Signals: For example, for instance, specifically, first, second, furthermore...
Listen to Tone and Emphasis: A speaker's voice will often rise in volume or slow down when they are making their most important point.
Summarize Mentally: As you listen, constantly ask yourself, "What is the speaker really trying to tell me?" Try to rephrase the core message in your own head.

Topic: Contextual Word Meaning (Using Context Clues)

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

Introduction

As a reader, what do you do when you see a word you don't know? Stopping to look up every word in a dictionary can be slow and interrupt your reading flow.

A more powerful skill is using contextual word meaning. This means figuring out a word's meaning by looking at the clues in the words, phrases, and sentences surrounding it. The "context" is the word's neighborhood, and it tells you a lot about the word.

Methods of Finding Meaning from Context (Types of Clues)
Definition (or Restatement) Clues

This is the easiest clue. The author directly defines the word in the sentence.

Signal Words: is, are, means, is called, which is, or, in other words, that is.
Example 1: "The anthology, which is a collection of poems, was on the desk."
Meaning: An anthology is a collection of poems.
Example 2: "Civics, the study of citizens’ rights and duties, is taught in schools."
Meaning: Civics is the study of citizens' rights and duties.
Synonym (or Comparison) Clues

The author uses a similar word or phrase nearby to hint at the meaning.

Example 1: "The teacher was stern, or very strict, with the students who came late."
Meaning: Stern means strict.
Example 2: "He felt great remorse, or guilt, after cheating on the test."
Meaning: Remorse means guilt.
Antonym (or Contrast) Clues

The author uses an opposite word or idea to help you figure out the meaning.

Signal Words: but, however, unlike, on the other hand, whereas.
Example 1: "Unlike his generous brother, Tunde is stingy with money."
Meaning: Generous must be the opposite of stingy (so, willing to give).
Example 2: "Some students were diligent, but others were lazy and refused to work."
Meaning: Diligent must be the opposite of lazy (so, hardworking).
Example (or Illustration) Clues

The author provides examples that help you understand the category the word belongs to.

Signal Words: such as, for example, for instance, including.
Example 1: "Domestic animals, such as goats, cows, and sheep, live near man."
Meaning: Domestic animals must mean animals that are tamed or live at home.
Example 2: "He enjoys many culinary activities, for instance, cooking, baking, and grilling."
Meaning: Culinary must be related to cooking or food.
Inference (or General Context) Clues

This is the most common, but most difficult, clue. The author doesn't give a direct hint; you must use the logic of the whole sentence (or paragraph) to figure out the meaning.

Example 1: "When the old woman entered, everyone in the room stood up and greeted her
respectfully."
Meaning: Standing up for an elder is a polite action. Respectfully must mean in a polite manner.
Example 2: "The car careened off the wet road, smashed through the barrier, and tumbled into the ditch."
Meaning: The other actions (smashing, tumbling) are wild and uncontrolled. Careened must mean to move wildly or swerve out of control.

Recall: The Definite Article "The"

As we learned, "the" is the definite article. We use it to refer to a specific noun that both the speaker and listener know about.
Example: "The teacher (our specific teacher) entered the class (our specific class)."

Exceptions: When NOT to Use "The" (Zero Article)

While "the" is common, it is incorrectly used in many situations. Here are the rules for when to
omit it.
Diseases

We do not use "the" before the names of most diseases.

Wrong: The malaria is dangerous.
Correct: Malaria is dangerous.
Wrong: He is suffering from the typhoid.
Correct: He is suffering from typhoid.
Exception: For plural-sounding diseases or specific outbreaks, we use "the."
Example: He had the measles / the mumps.
Example: The malaria outbreak in Kano was severe. (Here, "outbreak" is the main noun).
Geographical Names (Mountains, Lakes, Continents)

We do not use "the" before the names of single mountains, single lakes, or continents.

Wrong: The Mount Everest is high.
Correct: Mount Everest is high.
Wrong: The Lake Chad is drying up.
Correct: Lake Chad is drying up.
Wrong: He visited the Africa.
Correct: He visited Africa.
Exception: We do use "the" for Mountain Ranges, Groups of Lakes, and Archipelagos
(groups of islands).
Example: The Alps, The Himalayas (Ranges)
Example: The Great Lakes (Group)
Example: The Philippines, The West Indies (Groups of islands)
Institutions (for their primary purpose)

We omit "the" when we talk about the purpose or idea of an institution, not the physical building.

Examples:
"He is at school." (Meaning: He is studying as a student.)
"She went to church on Sunday." (Meaning: She went to worship.)
"My father is in prison." (Meaning: He is a prisoner.)
"She is in hospital." (Meaning: She is a patient.)
"He went to bed." (Meaning: He went to sleep.)
When we DO use "the": We use "the" when we mean the specific building or object.
"He went to the school to paint it." (He is a painter, not a student.)
"She visited the church to admire its design." (She is a tourist, not a worshipper.)
"The governor visited the prison." (He is a visitor, not a prisoner.)
"I sat on the bed." (Referring to the piece of furniture.)
Countries

Generally, we do not use "the" before names of most countries.

Correct: Nigeria, Ghana, Canada, France, China.
Exception: We use "the" for countries with plural names or names that are descriptive
(containing "States," "Kingdom," "Republic").
Plural: The Netherlands, The Philippines
Descriptive: The United States (USA), The United Kingdom (UK), The Federal Republic of Nigeria, The People's Republic of China.
Meals, Languages, and Subjects

We do not use "the" for general meals, languages, or academic subjects.

Meals: "We had breakfast at 8 a.m." (Not: The breakfast)
Languages: "She speaks English fluently." (Not: The English)
Subjects: "Mathematics is my best subject." (Not: The Mathematics)

Introduction

Descriptive oral composition is the art of "painting a picture" using spoken words. The goal is to make your listener see, hear, feel, smell, and taste what you are describing.

Narrative (Week 4) vs. Descriptive (Week 9):
A narrative tells a story (what happened). It focuses on action and time (e.g., "A Day I Will Never Forget").
A descriptive composition paints a picture (what something is like). It focuses on details and
senses (e.g., "My Classroom").

In an oral description, your voice is your paintbrush. How you speak is just as important as the words you choose.

Key Strategies for a Good Descriptive Oral Composition
Use of Sensory Details (The 5 Senses)

Your description is only effective if it makes the listener use their imagination.

| Sense | Questions to Ask | Example Words |

| :--- | :--- | :--- |

| Sight | What does it look like? (Colour, shape, size, light) | gleaming, dull, massive, snake-like, shadowy, vibrant |

| Sound | What does it sound like? (Loud, soft, music, noise) | whispering, rumbling, screeching, buzzing, silent, melodious |

| Smell | What does it smell like? (Pleasant, bad, strong, faint) | acrid, fragrant, damp, fresh-baked, rotten, smoky |

| Touch | What does it feel like? (Temperature, texture) | rough, slimy, sharp, velvety, icy, oppressive (heat) |

| Taste | What does it taste like? (Sweet, sour, etc.) | bitter, zesty, salty, bland, spicy, delicious |

Use Specific Details (Vivid Language)

Avoid vague, general words. Be precise.

Vague: "There was a nice tree in the big compound."
Vivid: "A massive, ancient Iroko tree, with a gnarled, twisted trunk, stood in the centre of the
dusty, sun-baked compound."
Vague: "The market was loud."
Vivid: "The market buzzed with the shouts of traders bargaining, the screeching of wheelbarrows, and the blaring of a nearby speaker."
Logical Ordering

Organize your description so the listener isn't confused.
Spatial Order: Describe from top-to-bottom, left-to-right, or near-to-far. (e.g., "As you enter the classroom, you first see... then at the back...")
Order of Importance: Start with the most noticeable feature. (e.g., "The first thing you notice about my uncle is his booming, loud laugh...")
Effective Use of Voice (The "Oral" Part)

This is what makes it an oral composition.

Pace: Speak slowly when describing something calm or sad. Speak faster for excitement or chaos.
Tone: Let your voice show emotion. If you describe a sad scene, sound sad. If you describe an exciting one, sound excited.
Volume: Get louder to describe a storm or a shout. Get softer (whisper) to describe a secret or a quiet morning.
Pause: Pause for a second after describing an important detail. This gives the listener time to "see" it in their mind.

Topic: Nominalization of Adjectives

Learning Objectives

Introduction

Nominalization (or "nouning") is the process of turning a word that is not a noun (like an adjective or a verb) into a noun.

Adjective: honest (describes a person)
Noun: honesty (the concept or quality of being honest)
Why do we do this?

Nominalization makes our writing sound more formal, academic, and objective. It allows us to talk about concepts and ideas rather than just people and actions.

Informal (Adjective): "The minister was wise, so the king trusted him."
Formal (Noun): "The minister's wisdom earned him the king's trust."

Formation of Nouns from Adjectives (How-To)

We usually form these nouns by adding a suffix (an ending).
Rule 1: Adding -ness

(Used for many common adjectives to show a state or quality)

happy $\rightarrow$ happiness
kind $\rightarrow$ kindness
sad $\rightarrow$ sadness
weak $\rightarrow$ weakness
good $\rightarrow$ goodness
Rule 2: Adding -ity or -ty

(Often used for Latin-based adjectives)

able $\rightarrow$ ability
pure $\rightarrow$ purity
formal $\rightarrow$ formality
generous $\rightarrow$ generosity
difficult $\rightarrow$ difficulty
stupid $\rightarrow$ stupidity
Rule 3: Adding -ance or -ence

important $\rightarrow$ importance
arrogant $\rightarrow$ arrogance
ignorant $\rightarrow$ ignorance
patient $\rightarrow$ patience
innocent $\rightarrow$ innocence
Rule 4: Irregular / Stem Change

(These are common and must be memorized)

strong $\rightarrow$ strength
long $\rightarrow$ length
wide $\rightarrow$ width
high $\rightarrow$ height
deep $\rightarrow$ depth
true $\rightarrow$ truth
brave $\rightarrow$ bravery
hot $\rightarrow$ heat
poor $\rightarrow$ poverty

Sentence Examples (Showing the Function)

Adjective: The soldiers were brave.
Noun: The soldiers showed great bravery.
Adjective: The room is wide.
Noun: What is the width of the room?
Adjective: It is important to be on time.
Noun: Punctuality is of great importance.
Adjective: She is very honest.
Noun: Her honesty is her best quality.

Introduction

Listening comprehension is not just about hearing sounds; it is an active mental process of
understanding and interpreting spoken information.

One of the most important skills in active listening is grasping the main points. Think of yourself as a detective. You are not just collecting all the sand (every single word); you are looking for the gold nuggets (the key ideas). The main points are the speaker's primary messages—the "big ideas" they want you to leave with.

Skills for Identifying Main Points

To find the "gold nuggets" (main points), you need the right tools (skills):

Active Concentration: You must consciously decide to focus. This means ignoring distractions, not thinking about your reply, and focusing only on the speaker's message.
Prediction: As you listen, your brain should be thinking ahead. "Based on the title, what will they talk about?" "They just said 'firstly,' so I expect a 'secondly'."
Identifying Signal Words (Signposts): Speakers use "signposts" to guide you. Listen for these:
To introduce a big idea: "My main point is...", "The most important thing...", "What this all means is..."
To list points: "First," "Second," "Next," "Finally..."
To conclude: "In conclusion," "Therefore," "To summarize..."
To show contrast (and a new idea): "However," "But," "On the other hand..."
Note-Taking (Keywords only): Do not try to write full sentences. This is the biggest mistake. You will stop listening and start writing. Instead, jot down only keywords and phrases that capture the idea.
Mental Summarization: After the speaker finishes a section, ask yourself, "In one short sentence, what was the one thing they just said?"

Main Point vs. Supporting Detail

This is the most crucial distinction.

Main Point (The Tabletop): This is the core idea. It's the general statement.
Supporting Details (The Legs): These prove or explain the main point. They are the examples, statistics, stories, or facts.

Example:

Main Point: Nigeria's film industry, Nollywood, is globally recognized.
Supporting Detail: It is the second-largest film producer by volume in the world.
Supporting Detail: Its movies are popular across Africa, the Caribbean, and Europe.

You need the details to understand the main point, but the main point is the message.

Topic: Comprehending Main Ideas

Learning Objectives

Introduction

Reading comprehension is the ability to understand what you read. Just like in listening, the most important skill is to find the main idea.

What's the difference between reading and listening for a main idea? In reading, you have a huge advantage: you can go back. You can re-read, pause, and think. The writer often leaves clear clues.

Topic vs. Main Idea

This is a very important difference.

Topic: The subject of the passage. It's a short word or phrase (e.g., "Corruption," "Football," "HIV/AIDS").
Main Idea: The point the writer is making about the topic. It's a full sentence (e.g., "Corruption is Nigeria's biggest problem," "HIV/AIDS can be prevented.").

How to Find the Main Idea

Skim the Passage: Read the first and last sentences of each paragraph. Look at the title. This gives you the topic.
Identify the Topic Sentence: In most paragraphs, the writer states the main idea directly in one sentence. This is the topic sentence. It's often the first sentence, but it can also be the last (as a summary).
Distinguish Key Ideas from Supporting Details: Ask yourself, "Does this sentence explain
another sentence, or is it the main point?"
Lagos is crowded. (Main Idea)
It has over 20 million people. (Supporting Detail)
The traffic is terrible. (Supporting Detail)
Summarize in Your Own Words: After reading, ask, "What is the one thing the author wants me to know?" If you can say it in one sentence, you have found the main idea.

Sample Passage 1 (Topical Issue: Corruption)

Corruption has become one of the greatest problems facing many developing countries. It involves the misuse of public office for personal gain. Bribery, embezzlement, and nepotism are common forms of corruption. In Nigeria, corruption affects every sector—from education to health, and even the police. It leads to poverty, poor infrastructure, and a total loss of trust in leadership. To reduce corruption, there must be transparency, accountability, and severe punishment for offenders.

Topic: Corruption.
Main Idea: Corruption is a serious national problem that causes poverty and underdevelopment, and it must be fought through transparency and accountability.
Topic Sentence (clue): The very first sentence.

Practice Passage 2 (HIV/AIDS Awareness)

Education, safe practices, and regular testing are essential to prevention. In many African communities, ignorance and stigma make it difficult for people to seek help. People are afraid to get tested because they fear their families and friends will reject them. However, with awareness campaigns and proper medical support, the disease can be controlled and managed effectively, allowing people to live long, productive lives.

Topic: HIV/AIDS.
Main Idea: (Find it yourself... what is the one point the author is making?)
Possible Answer: HIV/AIDS is a serious disease, but it can be prevented and managed through education, awareness, and medical care (despite the challenges of stigma).

We blamed the government, but we also knew the truth: we had blocked the drains with our own hands, and now, we were paying the price.

Introduction

Nominalization is a big word for a simple idea. It is the process of turning a word (like a verb or adjective) into a noun.

Today, we are looking at Nominalization of Verbs. This means we are turning an action or
process (a verb) into a thing or concept (a noun).

Verb (Action): He decided.
Noun (Concept): His decision was final.

Why Use Nominalization?
We nominalize verbs for two main reasons:

To sound more formal and academic: It's common in textbooks, official reports, and academic essays.
Informal: "We need to manage the project better."
Formal: "Better management of the project is required."
To make an action the subject of a sentence: It allows us to talk about the action itself.
"Growth is important for the company." (from the verb grow)
"Failure is not an option." (from the verb fail)

Introduction

A regular verb forms its past tense by adding -ed (walk $\rightarrow$ walked).

An irregular verb does not follow this rule. It changes its form, often by changing its vowel.

The Three Key Forms:

Base Form (Present): The basic verb (e.g., eat, go, see).
Past Tense: Used for a completed action in the past. It stands alone.
"I went to the market yesterday."
Past Participle: Used with a "helper" (auxiliary) verb (e.g., has, have, had, was, were).
"I have gone to the market."
"The food was eaten."

The Most Common Error: Using the Past Tense form when you should use the Past Participle.

Wrong: "She has wrote the letter."
Correct: "She has written the letter."
Wrong: "The glass was broke."
Correct: "The glass was broken."

EVALUATION (Grammar)

Define an irregular verb in your own words.

What is the main difference between the Past Tense and the Past Participle in terms of how they are used in a sentence?
Complete the following sentences with the correct form of the verb in brackets.

(a) She has	(begin) to read the book.

(b) The boy	(run) home from school yesterday.
(c) We	(see) that movie last week.

(d) The song was	(sing) by the choir.
(e) I have	(eat) all my food.

Write the Past Tense and Past Participle forms for: go, write, drink, speak, take.

ASSIGNMENT (Grammar)

List ten (10) irregular verbs that were not in today's lesson (e.g., fly, know, grow, etc.). Write their Past Tense and Past Participle forms.
Write five (5) original sentences that correctly use the Past Participle form with a helper verb. (e.g., "The window was broken by the ball.")
Write a short paragraph (3-5 sentences) about something you did last weekend. You must use at least five Past Tense irregular verbs. Underline them.
