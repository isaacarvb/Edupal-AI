WEEK 1 – INTRODUCTION TO CHEMISTRY

Definition of Chemistry

Chemistry is the branch of science that studies the composition, structure, properties, and changes of matter.

Composition refers to the kinds of elements or compounds a substance is made of.
Structure refers to how atoms or molecules are arranged in a substance.
Properties include physical properties (colour, boiling point, density) and chemical properties (reactivity, flammability).
Changes include physical changes (melting, dissolving) and chemical changes (rusting, burning, fermentation).
Key Point: Chemistry is often called the “central science” because it connects physics, biology, geology, medicine, engineering, and many other sciences.

History of Chemistry

The development of chemistry can be divided into four main historical periods:

Ancient Chemistry (Practical Beginnings)

Early humans learned to use fire, cook food, extract oils, make pottery, and use natural dyes.
Egyptians mastered techniques such as glassmaking, papyrus processing, mummification, and extraction of metals like gold and copper.
The Chinese developed gunpowder from saltpetre, charcoal, and sulphur.
These early practices were based on observation and trial-and-error, not scientific theory.

Alchemy (Middle Ages, 500 – 1600 AD)

Originated in Egypt, Arabia, and later spread to Europe.
Alchemists sought to transform base metals like lead into gold (transmutation) and discover the "elixir of life" for immortality.
Although their goals were mystical, alchemists discovered many laboratory techniques: distillation, filtration, crystallization, and use of apparatus like alembics.
Famous alchemists include Jabir ibn Hayyan (Geber), who is considered the father of chemistry in the Arab world.

The Age of Modern Chemistry (1600s – 1800s)

Robert Boyle (1627–1691) emphasized experimentation and introduced Boyle’s Law on gases.
Antoine Lavoisier (1743–1794), called the “Father of Modern Chemistry,” introduced the Law of Conservation of Mass, named oxygen and hydrogen, and helped develop the metric system.
John Dalton proposed the Atomic Theory (1803), explaining that matter is made of indivisible particles called atoms.

Contemporary Chemistry (1900s – Present)

Development of the Periodic Table by Dmitri Mendeleev (1869) and later refinements.
Discovery of subatomic particles (electrons, protons, neutrons) and the structure of the atom.
Advancement in organic chemistry, biochemistry, materials science, and nuclear chemistry.
Chemistry now impacts industries such as pharmaceuticals, agriculture, electronics, cosmetics, and environmental management.

Branches of Chemistry

Chemistry is vast and can be divided into major and applied branches.

Major Branches

Organic Chemistry – Study of carbon-containing compounds (except carbonates and simple oxides). Examples: fuels, plastics, pharmaceuticals.
Inorganic Chemistry – Study of non-carbon compounds such as salts, metals, and minerals.
Physical Chemistry – Study of how matter behaves on a molecular and atomic level, and how chemical reactions occur, using principles of physics.
Analytical Chemistry – Concerned with identifying substances (qualitative analysis) and determining their quantities (quantitative analysis).
Biochemistry – Study of chemical processes within living organisms (e.g., enzymes, DNA, metabolism).
Applied / Specialized Branches

Environmental Chemistry – Studies chemical processes in the environment and pollution control.
Industrial Chemistry – Application of chemical knowledge in manufacturing processes.
Pharmaceutical Chemistry – Design and production of drugs.
Nuclear Chemistry – Study of radioactive substances and nuclear reactions.
Forensic Chemistry – Application of chemistry in criminal investigations.

Importance of Chemistry

Chemistry plays a central role in everyday life, industry, and scientific development.

Daily Life

Production of soap, detergents, and cosmetics.
Cooking processes (baking, boiling, frying).
Preservation of food using chemical additives.

Industry

Petrochemical industries produce fuels, plastics, synthetic fibres.
Agricultural chemicals (fertilizers, pesticides) improve crop yield.
Cement, glass, paint, textile, and paper industries all depend on chemical processes.

Medicine and Health

Discovery and manufacture of drugs, vaccines, antiseptics.
Diagnostic techniques (blood tests, imaging using contrast chemicals).

Environmental Management

Water purification, waste management, pollution control.
Understanding climate change and developing renewable energy technologies.
Scientific and Technological Advancement

Development of new materials (nanomaterials, superconductors).
Space exploration (rocket fuels, life support systems).

WEEK 2 – CHEMICAL INDUSTRIES AND LABORATORY APPARATUS

Chemical Industries (Overview) Meaning of Chemical Industries
Chemical industries are industries that use chemical substances at one stage or another to produce other chemicals or finished goods.

Raw materials: Substances used as starting materials in production.
Products: Finished goods from an industry, which may also serve as raw materials for another industry.

Types of Raw Materials
Natural raw materials – Obtained directly from nature without major alteration. Examples: air, water, coal, limestone, petroleum, iron ore, sulphur deposits.
Synthetic raw materials – Man-made materials produced for further manufacturing. Examples: synthetic fibres, plastics, palm kernel oil for soaps, thread for textiles.

Classification of Chemical Industries

Heavy Chemicals – Needed in large quantities; purity is not the top priority. Examples: tetraoxosulphate (VI) acid (H₂SO₄), sodium hydroxide, cement, palm kernel oil.
Fine Chemicals – Required in small quantities; produced in very pure form. Examples: sodium chloride (NaCl), dyes, preservatives, analytical reagents.

Divisions According to Products

Glass industries – Use sand, clay, sodium carbonate, and colourants to make glass.
Ceramics industries – Use clay and fine chemicals to make ceramics.
Pharmaceutical industries – Use organic, inorganic, and synthetic chemicals to produce drugs.
Metallurgical industries – Extract and process metals from ores.
Fertilizer industries – Produce nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium compounds for agriculture.
Paint industries – Use pigments and inorganic chemicals to make paints.

Importance of Chemical Industries

Provide raw materials for other industries.
Generate income and employment.
Serve as research centres and educational resources.

Disadvantages of Chemical Industries

Cause environmental pollution.
Can lead to rural-urban migration.
Capital intensive to establish.

WEEK 3 - NATURE OF MATTER – STATES, PROPERTIES, AND CHANGES OF STATE

Meaning of Matter

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. All physical objects—whether visible (like a table) or invisible (like air)—are made of matter. Matter is composed of tiny particles called atoms and molecules that are in constant motion.

States of Matter
Matter exists in three primary states: solid, liquid, and gas. In advanced science, two more states—plasma and Bose–Einstein condensate—are recognized, but at the secondary school level, we focus on the main three.

Solid

Particle arrangement: Particles are closely packed in a fixed, orderly arrangement.
Movement: Particles vibrate in fixed positions.
Shape and volume: Has a definite shape and definite volume.
Compressibility: Cannot be easily compressed due to little space between particles.
Examples: Wood, metal, stone, ice.

Liquid

Particle arrangement: Particles are close together but not in a fixed arrangement.
Movement: Particles can slide over each other, allowing liquids to flow.
Shape and volume: Has definite volume but no definite shape—it takes the shape of its container.
Compressibility: Almost incompressible.
Examples: Water, oil, alcohol, mercury.

Gas

Particle arrangement: Particles are far apart and not in a fixed position.
Movement: Move freely at high speed in all directions.
Shape and volume: Has neither definite shape nor definite volume—it expands to fill its container.
Compressibility: Highly compressible due to large spaces between particles.
Examples: Air, oxygen, carbon dioxide, nitrogen.

Changes of State

Changes of state occur when matter is transformed from one state to another by heating or cooling. These processes involve energy changes but do not alter the chemical composition of the substance.

Melting

Definition: Change from solid to liquid upon heating.
Example: Ice melting into water.
Energy change: Heat energy is absorbed to overcome the forces holding particles in fixed positions.

Freezing (Solidification)

Definition: Change from liquid to solid upon cooling.
Example: Water freezing into ice.
Energy change: Heat energy is released as particles slow down and become fixed.

Vaporization (Boiling/Evaporation)

Boiling: Rapid change from liquid to gas at a specific temperature (boiling point).
Evaporation: Slow change from liquid to gas at the surface, at any temperature.
Example: Water boiling at 100°C; drying of wet clothes.
Condensation

Definition: Change from gas to liquid upon cooling.
Example: Water vapour forming droplets on a cold surface.

Sublimation

Definition: Direct change from solid to gas without passing through the liquid state.
Examples: Iodine crystals, dry ice (solid CO₂), naphthalene balls.

Deposition

Definition: Change from gas directly to solid without becoming liquid.
Example: Formation of frost.

Particle Theory of Matter

The Kinetic Theory of Matter explains that:

All matter is made up of tiny particles (atoms or molecules).
Particles are in constant motion.
The higher the temperature, the faster the particles move.
The state of matter depends on the spacing and movement of the particles.

Arrangement of particles in solids, liquids, and gases

Common Misconceptions

Misconception: Gases have no mass.
Correction: Gases have mass, though they are light and invisible.
Misconception: Melting and dissolving are the same.
Correction: Melting changes state due to heat; dissolving is mixing with a solvent.

Practical Examples

Melting: Ice in a glass on a hot day.
Boiling: Water boiling for cooking.
Evaporation: Sweat drying from the skin.
Condensation: Fogging of mirrors after a hot bath.
Sublimation: Disappearance of mothballs in clothes.

WEEK 4 – SEPARATION TECHNIQUES

In chemistry, separation techniques are methods used to divide mixtures into their individual components, based on differences in physical or chemical properties such as particle size, density, solubility, boiling point, or magnetic properties. The choice of a suitable technique depends on:

The nature of the mixture (solid–solid, solid–liquid, liquid–liquid, gas mixtures).
The properties of the components.
The level of purity required.

Filtration

Definition: A process of separating an insoluble solid from a liquid using a porous barrier (filter paper).
Principle: Based on particle size — solid particles are too large to pass through the filter pores.

Distillation
Example: Separating sand from water.
Definition: Separation of a liquid from a mixture using differences in boiling points.
Principle: Components with lower boiling points vaporize first, then condense back to liquid.
Example: Purifying water from saltwater.

Fractional Distillation

Definition: A refined form of distillation used to separate a mixture of liquids with close boiling points.
Principle: Utilizes a fractionating column to allow repeated condensation and vaporization for better separation.
Example: Separating ethanol from water, crude oil refining.

Sublimation

Definition: Direct conversion of a solid to gas without passing through the liquid phase.
Principle: Based on differences in sublimation tendencies between substances.
Example: Separating iodine crystals from a mixture.

Chromatography

Definition: Technique for separating components of a mixture based on their movement through a medium.
Principle: Components move at different rates due to differences in solubility and adsorption.
Example: Separating plant pigments on paper.

Centrifugation

Definition: Separation of mixtures using centrifugal force by spinning at high speed.
Principle: Denser particles move outward faster than less dense ones.
Example: Separating plasma from blood cells.

Decantation

Definition: Pouring off a liquid to separate it from sediment or an immiscible liquid.
Principle: Relies on density differences between substances.
Example: Removing water from settled sand.

Evaporation

Definition: Removal of a liquid by heating until only the solid residue remains.
Principle: Components with lower boiling points evaporate, leaving non-volatile solids behind.
Example: Obtaining salt from seawater.

Magnetic Separation

Definition: Use of magnets to separate magnetic materials from non-magnetic ones.
Principle: Based on magnetic properties of substances.

Example: Separating iron filings from sand.

Winnowing

Definition: Winnowing is a separation technique used to separate lighter particles (such as husks or chaff) from heavier particles (such as grains or seeds) using the action of wind or air currents.
Principle: The method works on the difference in weight (density) between the two substances — lighter materials are blown away by air, while heavier materials fall straight down.
Example: Separating paddy rice grains from husk after threshing.

Sieving
Definition: Sieving is a separation technique used to separate particles of different sizes by passing a mixture through a sieve or mesh that allows smaller particles to pass through while retaining the larger ones.
Principle: The method works based on the difference in particle size.
Example: Separating wheat flour from bran using a fine mesh sieve.

Sedimentation

Definition: Sedimentation is a separation technique in which insoluble, heavier particles in a liquid settle at the bottom under the influence of gravity.
Principle: The method is based on the difference in density between solid particles and the liquid.
Example: Allowing muddy water to stand so that sand and mud settle at the bottom.

WEEK 6 – ATOMIC STRUCTURE – STRUCTURE OF THE ATOM, SUBATOMIC PARTICLES

Meaning of Atom

An atom is the smallest particle of an element that takes part in chemical reactions and retains the chemical properties of that element.
Atoms are extremely small: e.g., radius ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m.

Historical Development of the Atomic Model

Dalton’s Atomic Theory (1803)

Atoms are indivisible, indestructible particles.

Atoms of the same element are identical; atoms of different elements differ.

Atoms combine in simple ratios to form compounds.

Limitation: Failed to explain subatomic particles and isotopes.

Thomson’s Model (1897) – Plum pudding model

Discovered the electron.

Proposed that an atom is a sphere of positive charge with electrons embedded.

Limitation: Could not explain the stability of atoms.

Rutherford’s Model (1911) – Nuclear model

Gold foil experiment showed atoms are mostly empty space.

Nucleus at the center, containing positive charges (protons).
Electrons revolve around nucleus like planets.

Limitation: Could not explain why electrons don’t collapse into the nucleus.

Bohr’s Model (1913) – Planetary model

Electrons revolve in fixed orbits (energy levels/shells).

Energy is absorbed or released when electrons jump between shells.

Limitation: Could not fully explain multi-electron atoms.

Modern Atomic Theory (Quantum Mechanical Model)

Developed by Schrödinger, Heisenberg, etc.

Electrons are in regions called orbitals, not fixed paths.

Atoms consist of a dense nucleus (protons + neutrons) with electrons in shells/subshells.

Atomic Number and Mass Number

Atomic Number (Z): Number of protons in the nucleus.
Mass Number (A): Number of protons + neutrons.

Neutron number (N): N = A – Z.

Atoms of the same element (same Z) but different mass numbers (A).

Example: ¹²C, ¹³C, ¹⁴C are isotopes of carbon.

They have similar chemical properties but different physical properties.

Electrons are arranged in shells (K, L, M, N) according to the 2n² rule:

K shell → 2 electrons

M shell → 18 electrons

N shell → 32 electrons

Example:

WEEK 8: PERIODIC TABLE – GROUPS, PERIODS, AND PERIODICITY

The periodic table is one of the most important tools in Chemistry. It is a systematic arrangement of all the known elements in rows and columns in such a way that elements with similar properties are placed in the same vertical column (group). This arrangement is based on increasing atomic number, which is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. The periodic table not only helps chemists to classify elements but also enables them to predict the properties of elements and their compounds.

Historical Development of the Periodic Table

Johann Döbereiner (1829): Proposed the Law of Triads. He noticed that elements with similar chemical properties could be grouped in threes (triads). The atomic mass of the middle element was approximately the average of the other two. Example: Lithium (Li), Sodium (Na), and Potassium (K).
John Newlands (1864): Proposed the Law of Octaves. He observed that when elements were arranged in increasing order of atomic mass, every eighth element had properties similar to the first. This was not universally applicable.
Dmitri Mendeleev (1869): Developed the first widely accepted periodic table. He arranged elements in order of increasing atomic mass but placed elements with similar properties under the same group. Importantly, he left gaps for undiscovered elements and correctly predicted their properties (e.g., Germanium).
Henry Moseley (1913): Through X-ray experiments, Moseley showed that the atomic number, not atomic mass, is the correct basis for arrangement. This solved the anomalies in Mendeleev’s table and gave rise to the modern periodic table.

Structure of the Modern Periodic Table

The periodic table is arranged in 7 horizontal rows (Periods) and 18 vertical columns (Groups).
Elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic number.

The periodic table is divided into blocks based on electron configuration:

s-block: Groups 1 and 2 (alkali and alkaline earth metals).

p-block: Groups 13–18 (includes halogens and noble gases).

d-block: Transition metals (Groups 3–12).

f-block: Lanthanides and actinides, usually placed separately at the bottom.

Groups in the Periodic Table

A group is a vertical column of elements in the periodic table. Elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons, which explains their similar chemical properties.
Group 1 (Alkali metals): Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr. Very reactive, soft metals, low ionization energies, form +1 cations, react vigorously with water to produce hydrogen gas and alkaline hydroxides.
Group 2 (Alkaline earth metals): Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra. Reactive metals but less than Group 1, form +2 cations, react with acids to form hydrogen gas.
Groups 3–12 (Transition metals): Have partially filled d-orbitals, show variable oxidation states, form colored compounds, many act as catalysts (e.g., Fe, Ni, Pt).
Group 17 (Halogens): F, Cl, Br, I, At. Very reactive non-metals, form -1 anions, exist as diatomic molecules, strong oxidizing agents.
Group 18 (Noble gases): He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn. Inert gases, stable due to full valence shell configuration, very low chemical reactivity, used in lighting and welding.

Periods in the Periodic Table

A period is a horizontal row of elements in the periodic table. There are 7 periods.

In a period, the atomic number increases from left to right.

All elements in the same period have the same number of electron shells but different numbers of valence electrons.
Across a period, properties change progressively:

Metallic character decreases from left to right.

Non-metallic character increases from left to right.

Electronegativity and ionization energy increase.

Atomic radius decreases because of increased nuclear charge pulling electrons closer.

Periodicity in Properties of Elements

Periodicity is the repeating pattern of properties of elements across different periods and groups of the periodic table. These periodic trends arise due to changes in atomic structure (mainly nuclear charge and electron configuration).
Atomic Radius: Distance from nucleus to the outermost electron.

Decreases across a period (due to increasing nuclear charge).
Increases down a group (due to addition of electron shells).

Ionization Energy: Energy required to remove the outermost electron.

Increases across a period (greater nuclear attraction).

Decreases down a group (outer electrons farther from nucleus, easier to remove).

Electronegativity: Ability of an atom to attract shared electrons.

Increases across a period.

Decreases down a group.

Electron Affinity: Energy change when an atom gains an electron.

Generally increases across a period.

Decreases down a group.

Metallic Character: Tendency of an element to lose electrons.

Decreases across a period.

Increases down a group.

WEEK 9: CHEMICAL COMBINATION – LAWS OF CHEMICAL COMBINATION

Meaning of Chemical Combination

Chemical combination is the process by which two or more substances (elements or compounds) unite to form a new substance with new properties.
The substances that react are called reactants, while the new substance formed is called the product.
Chemical combination differs from physical combination (e.g., sand + salt, iron + sulphur mixture) because:
In chemical combination, the substances lose their individual properties and form a new substance (e.g., iron + sulphur → iron(II) sulphide).
In physical combination, the components retain their properties and can usually be separated by physical means.
Example:

Physical: Mixture of iron filings and sulphur can be separated by magnet.

Chemical: Heating iron filings and sulphur gives iron(II) sulphide (FeS) – a compound with entirely new properties.
This process follows certain natural rules or principles called the Laws of Chemical Combination, which were discovered by early chemists through experiments and form the foundation of modern chemistry.

The Laws of Chemical Combination

Law of Conservation of Mass

Proposed by: Antoine Lavoisier (1789) – often called the Father of Modern Chemistry.

Statement: Matter is neither created nor destroyed in the course of a chemical reaction. The total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products.
Explanation: During a chemical reaction, atoms are simply rearranged, not lost or created. This means the overall mass remains constant.
Equation Example:

2H2 + O2 ⟶ 2H2O

Mass of reactants = (4 g H₂ + 32 g O₂) = 36 g

Mass of products = 36 g H₂O
→ Mass is conserved.

Experimental Verification:
When mercuric oxide (HgO) is heated, it decomposes into mercury (Hg) and oxygen (O₂). Careful weighing before and after reaction shows no change in total mass.
Application: Forms the basis of stoichiometric calculations in chemistry (mole concept, chemical equations).

Law of Definite Proportions (Law of Constant Composition)

Proposed by: Joseph Proust (1799).

Statement: A given compound always contains the same elements combined in the same fixed proportion by mass, regardless of the method of preparation or the source.
Explanation: Water from a river, rain, or laboratory experiment always contains hydrogen and oxygen in the ratio 1:8 by mass.
Example:

Water (H₂O):

Atomic mass of H = 1, O = 16

2 atoms of H = 2 × 1 = 2

1 atom of O = 16

Ratio of H: O = 2:16 = 1:8 (constant always).

Application: Used to determine chemical formulae of compounds.

Law of Multiple Proportions

Proposed by: John Dalton (1803).

Statement: If two elements combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in simple whole-number ratios.
Explanation: Same elements can combine in different ways to form different compounds, but their combining masses remain in simple ratios.
Example (Carbon + Oxygen):

Carbon monoxide (CO): 12 g C combines with 16 g O.

Carbon dioxide (CO₂): 12 g C combines with 32 g O.

Ratio of O in the two compounds = 16:32 = 1:2 (simple whole number).

Application: Supports Dalton’s Atomic Theory (atoms combine in whole numbers, not fractions).

Law of Reciprocal Proportions

Proposed by: Jeremias Richter (1792).

Statement: If element A combines separately with element B and with element C, then the ratio in which B and C combine with each other is either the same or a simple multiple of the ratio in which they combine with A.
Example:

H combines with O to form water (H₂O).

H combines with Cl to form HCl.

O and Cl combine to form compounds like Cl₂O.
→ Their masses follow simple ratios that are consistent with the law.

Application: Helps in establishing chemical relationships among different compounds.

Law of Combining Volumes (Gay-Lussac’s Law)

Proposed by: Joseph Gay-Lussac (1808).
Statement: When gases react, they do so in volumes that bear a simple whole-number ratio to one another and to the volume of the gaseous products, provided all are measured at the same temperature and pressure.
Example:

2H2(g) + O2(g) ⟶ 2H2O(g)

Volumes: H₂: O₂: H₂O = 2: 1: 2 (simple whole numbers).

Application: Foundation for Avogadro’s hypothesis and the molar volume concept
(22.4 dm³ at STP).

Experimental Verifications (Summary of Classic Experiments)

Lavoisier’s Experiment: Decomposition of mercuric oxide to verify conservation of mass.
Proust’s Experiment: Analysis of water from different sources always gave H:O = 1:8 by mass.
Dalton’s Example: CO and CO₂ to verify multiple proportions.

Gay-Lussac’s Experiment: Reaction of hydrogen and oxygen gases in a closed vessel to form water vapor in a simple volume ratio.

Importance of the Laws of Chemical Combination

Provided strong experimental evidence for Dalton’s Atomic Theory.

Laid the foundation for the mole concept and stoichiometry.

Enabled chemists to determine the formulae of compounds accurately.

Guided the development of modern atomic structure and the periodic table.

Essential for quantitative chemistry (calculating masses, moles, and volumes of substances).
Still used today in industries (pharmaceuticals, fertilizers, metallurgy, etc.) where chemical reactions must be carried out in precise proportions.

WEEK 10: ACIDS, BASES AND SALTS

Definitions of Acids, Bases, and Salts

Acids:
Acids are substances that ionize in aqueous solution to produce hydrogen ions (H⁺) or protons as the only positive ion. According to Arrhenius, an acid increases the concentration of H⁺ ions in solution. Bronsted–Lowry defines an acid as a proton donor, while Lewis defines it as an electron pair acceptor.
Examples: HCl (Hydrochloric acid), H₂SO₄ (Sulphuric acid), HNO₃ (Nitric acid), CH₃COOH (Ethanoic acid).
Bases:
A base is any substance that reacts with an acid to neutralize it and form salt and water only. Arrhenius defines a base as a substance that increases the concentration of OH⁻ ions in aqueous solution. Bronsted–Lowry defines a base as a proton acceptor, while Lewis defines it as an electron pair donor.
Examples: NaOH (Sodium hydroxide), KOH (Potassium hydroxide), Ca(OH)₂ (Calcium hydroxide), NH₃ (Ammonia).
Alkalis:
Alkalis are soluble bases which, when dissolved in water, release OH⁻ ions. All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis.
Examples: NaOH, KOH, NH₄OH.

Salts:
Salts are compounds formed when the hydrogen ion of an acid is wholly or partially replaced by a metal ion or ammonium ion. Salts can be normal, acidic, or basic, depending on the extent of replacement.
Examples: NaCl (Sodium chloride), K₂SO₄ (Potassium sulphate), NH₄NO₃ (Ammonium nitrate).

Examples of Acids, Bases, and Salts in Everyday Life

Acids:

Vinegar (contains ethanoic acid).

Citrus fruits like orange and lemon (contain citric acid).

Soft drinks (contain carbonic acid).

Battery acid (sulphuric acid).

Bases:

Soap and detergents (contain sodium or potassium hydroxides).
Toothpaste (contains mild alkalis to neutralize mouth acids).

Limewater (Ca(OH)₂) used in construction.

Baking soda (NaHCO₃).

Salts:

Table salt (NaCl).

Fertilizers (e.g., ammonium nitrate NH₄NO₃, potassium chloride KCl).

Washing soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O).

Epsom salt (MgSO₄·7H₂O).

Properties of Acids, Bases, and Salts

Properties of Acids

Taste sour (e.g., citric acid in lemon).

Turn blue litmus paper red.

Have pH less than 7.

Strong acids (like HCl, H₂SO₄) are corrosive to skin and metals.

React with metals to produce hydrogen gas (e.g., 2HCl + Zn → ZnCl₂ + H₂).

React with bases/alkalis to form salt and water (neutralization).

React with carbonates and bicarbonates to release carbon dioxide (HCl + Na₂CO₃ → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂).
Properties of Bases

Taste bitter and feel soapy or slippery.

Turn red litmus paper blue.

Have pH greater than 7.
Strong alkalis (NaOH, KOH) are corrosive and can burn skin.

React with acids to form salt and water.

React with ammonium salts on heating to release ammonia gas (NaOH + NH₄Cl → NaCl
+ H₂O + NH₃).

Some bases are insoluble in water (e.g., CuO, Fe(OH)₃).

Properties of Salts

Many salts are crystalline solids.

They can be soluble (NaCl, KNO₃) or insoluble (PbSO₄, BaSO₄).

Neutral salts (NaCl) have no effect on litmus.

Acidic salts (NaHSO₄) turn blue litmus red.

Basic salts (Pb(OH)Cl) turn red litmus blue.

Conduct electricity when molten or in aqueous solution.

The pH Scale

The pH scale is a logarithmic scale used to measure the concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺) in a solution, ranging from 0 to 14.
pH < 7: Acidic solution.

pH = 7: Neutral solution (pure water).

pH > 7: Basic/alkaline solution.

Indicators Used in pH Determination:

Litmus (red in acid, blue in base).

Methyl orange (red in acid, yellow in base).

Phenolphthalein (colourless in acid, pink in base).
Universal indicator (shows different colours corresponding to pH values).

Practical Importance of pH:

Agriculture: pH of soil determines fertility. Acidic soils are treated with lime (Ca(OH)₂).

Medicine: Stomach acid (pH 1.5 – 2.5) is neutralized by antacids when excessive.

Biology: Blood has a pH of about 7.35 – 7.45. Any deviation is dangerous.

Neutralization Reaction

A neutralization reaction is a chemical reaction between an acid and a base (or alkali) to produce a salt and water.
General equation: Acid + Base → Salt + Water Examples:
HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O

H₂SO₄ + 2KOH → K₂SO₄ + 2H₂O

HNO₃ + NH₄OH → NH₄NO₃ + H₂O

Characteristics of Neutralization:

Exothermic reaction (releases heat).

Can be used for titration experiments to determine concentration of solutions.

Applications of Neutralization:

Medicine: Antacids neutralize excess stomach acid.

Agriculture: Quicklime (CaO) or slaked lime (Ca(OH)₂) used to reduce soil acidity.

Environmental Science: Industrial effluents containing acids are neutralized before disposal.
Daily Life: Baking soda neutralizes bee stings (acidic) while vinegar neutralizes wasp stings (alkaline).

BALANCING CHEMICAL EQUATIONS

Law of Conservation of Mass:
In a chemical reaction, matter is neither created nor destroyed; the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products.
This law forms the basis of balancing equations.

General Steps:

Write down correct symbols and formulae of all reactants and products.

Write the unbalanced equation.

List the number of each atom on both sides.

Adjust the coefficients (whole numbers placed before formulae) to balance atoms.

Never change subscripts in the formula; only adjust coefficients.

Check to ensure all atoms are balanced and coefficients are in the lowest ratio.

Worked Examples:

CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

Reaction of sodium with water:

Balanced: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂

N₂ + H₂ → NH₃
Balanced: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃
Combustion of ethane:
C₂H₆ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O
Balanced: 2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O

Tips for Balancing:

Balance metals first, non-metals second, hydrogen next, and oxygen last.

If odd numbers occur, try multiplying all coefficients by 2.

Always simplify coefficients to the smallest whole numbers.

WEEK 1: QUANTITATIVE AND QUALITATIVE ANALYSIS (INTRODUCTION) – BASIC CONCEPTS

Chemistry, being an experimental science, depends largely on observation, measurement, and analysis. The term analysis refers to a set of techniques used to identify substances (what they are) and to determine the amount present (how much is there). This forms the foundation of analytical chemistry.
Analytical chemistry is broadly divided into two main branches:

Qualitative Analysis – concerned with identifying the type or nature of substances.
Quantitative Analysis – concerned with measuring the exact amount of substances present.
Both are complementary: qualitative analysis first establishes the identity of a substance, and quantitative analysis then determines its amount.

QUALITATIVE ANALYSIS

Definition

Qualitative analysis is the type of analysis used to determine the identity of elements, ions, functional groups, or compounds present in a sample. It does not give a numerical value but answers the question: “What is in the sample?”
Main Features

It is descriptive rather than numerical.

It is usually the first step in analysis.

Relies on observation of changes (colour change, precipitation, effervescence, odour, etc.).
Methods of Qualitative Analysis

Observation of Physical Properties

Colour (e.g., copper compounds are usually blue or green).

State of matter (gas, liquid, solid).

Odour (e.g., ammonia has a pungent smell).

Solubility in water or organic solvents.

Chemical Tests

Acid Tests: Reaction of carbonates with dilute acids producing effervescence due to CO₂.
Alkali Tests: Reaction of ammonium salts with alkalis releasing ammonia gas.

Flame Tests

Some metals produce characteristic colours in a non-luminous flame due to electronic transitions.
Sodium (Na⁺): Yellow

Potassium (K⁺): Lilac

Calcium (Ca²⁺): Brick red

Copper (Cu²⁺): Blue-green

Precipitation Reactions

Certain ions form insoluble salts with specific reagents.

Chloride ions (Cl⁻) + silver nitrate (AgNO₃) → White precipitate of silver chloride.
Sulphate ions (SO₄²⁻) + barium chloride (BaCl₂) → White precipitate of barium sulphate.
Confirmatory Tests

Used after preliminary results for greater accuracy.

Example: Distinguishing between carbonate and sulphite ions by adding dilute acid and then passing gas through limewater (sulphur dioxide decolourises acidified KMnO₄ while CO₂ does not).

QUANTITATIVE ANALYSIS

Definition

Quantitative analysis is the type of analysis used to determine the exact amount, concentration, or numerical value of a substance in a given sample. It answers the question: “How much of it is present?”
Main Features

It is numerical and involves measurements.

Requires calculations to determine concentration, mass, or volume.

It is essential in quality control, medicine dosage, and industrial formulations.

Methods of Quantitative Analysis

Volumetric Analysis

Involves accurate measurement of volumes of solutions that react together.

Commonly used in titrations (acid-base, redox, precipitation).

Example: Determining the molarity of hydrochloric acid by titration against standard sodium hydroxide.
Gravimetric Analysis

Involves determination of a substance by precipitation, drying, and weighing.

Example: Estimating the percentage purity of sodium chloride by precipitating silver chloride using silver nitrate and weighing it.
Instrumental/Modern Methods

More advanced and highly accurate.

Examples:

Spectrophotometry (measures light absorbed by a solution).

Chromatography (separates mixtures into components).

Mass spectrometry (gives molecular structure and mass).

Atomic absorption spectroscopy (for metal analysis).

Introduction to the Mole Concept

Chemistry deals with tiny particles like atoms and molecules, which are impossible to count individually. We need a special "counting unit," just like we use a dozen for 12 items. In chemistry, this unit is the mole (mol).
Definition: A mole is the amount of a substance that contains as many elementary particles (atoms, molecules, ions, etc.) as there are atoms in exactly 12 grams of Carbon-12.
This specific number of particles is known as Avogadro’s Number (Nₐ).

Avogadro’s Number (Nₐ)

Value: Nₐ = 6.022 × 10²³ particles per mole

Meaning:

1 mole of Hydrogen atoms = 6.022 × 10²³ H atoms.

1 mole of Water molecules = 6.022 × 10²³ H₂O molecules.

1 mole of NaCl formula units = 6.022 × 10²³ NaCl units.

� Important Note: The type of "particle" we count depends on the substance:

Elements: Atoms (e.g., Na, Fe) or molecules (e.g., O₂, H₂, Cl₂).

Covalent Compounds: Molecules (e.g., H₂O, CO₂, NH₃).

Ionic Compounds: Formula Units (e.g., NaCl, KNO₃).

Molar Mass

Definition: The mass of one mole of a substance, expressed in grams per mole (g/mol).

It is numerically equal to the Relative Atomic Mass (Aᵣ) or Relative Molecular/Formula Mass (Mᵣ) from the periodic table.
Examples:

Aᵣ of Carbon (C) = 12 → Molar Mass of C = 12 g/mol
Mᵣ of Water (H₂O) = (2×1) + 16 = 18 → Molar Mass = 18 g/mol

Mᵣ of Sodium Chloride (NaCl) = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 → Molar Mass = 58.5 g/mol

� Remember: Always express molar mass in g/mol.

Worked Examples Example 1: Moles from Mass
How many moles are in 18 g of water?

Mᵣ of H₂O = 18 g/mol

n = 18 g / 18 g/mol = 1 mol
Answer: 1 mole of water.

Example 2: Particles from Mass
How many molecules are there in 54 g of water?

Mᵣ of H₂O = 18 g/mol

Moles, n = 54 g / 18 g/mol = 3 mol
Number of molecules = 3 mol × 6.022 × 10²³ molecules/mol = 1.807 × 10²⁴ molecules
Answer: 1.807 × 10²⁴ molecules of water.

Example 3: Atoms from Mass
Calculate the number of atoms in 4 g of hydrogen gas (H₂).

Mᵣ of H₂ = 2 g/mol

Moles = 4 g / 2 g/mol = 2 mol

Number of H₂ molecules = 2 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.204 × 10²⁴

Each H₂ molecule has 2 atoms.

Total atoms = 1.204 × 10²⁴ molecules × 2 atoms/molecule = 2.408 × 10²⁴ atoms
Answer: 2.408 × 10²⁴ atoms.

Example 4: Gas Volume at STP
Find the volume occupied by 0.25 mol of oxygen gas at STP.

Volume = n × 22.4 dm³/mol

Volume = 0.25 mol × 22.4 dm³/mol = 5.6 dm³
Answer: 5.6 dm³.

Stoichiometry (Quantitative Chemistry)

Definition: Stoichiometry is the calculation of the quantities (masses, volumes, particles) of reactants and products in a chemical reaction based on the balanced chemical equation.
The Heart of Stoichiometry: The Mole Ratio

The coefficients in a balanced equation give the mole ratio of reactants and products.
Steps in Stoichiometric Calculations:

Write a balanced chemical equation.
Extract the mole ratio from the coefficients.

Convert the given quantity (mass, volume, particles) to moles (n).

Use the mole ratio to find the moles of the unknown substance.

Convert the moles of the unknown back to the required quantity (mass, volume, particles).
Stoichiometry Worked Examples

Example 5 (Mass–Mass Relationship):
How many grams of water are produced when 8 g of hydrogen reacts completely with oxygen?

Equation: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Mole Ratio: 2 mol H₂ : 1 mol O₂ : 2 mol H₂O

Moles of given: Moles of H₂ = 8 g / 2 g/mol = 4 mol

Mole Ratio to Unknown:

2 mol H₂ produces 2 mol H₂O → Ratio is 1:1.

So, 4 mol H₂ produces 4 mol H₂O.

Convert to Mass: Mass of H₂O = 4 mol × 18 g/mol = 72 g
Answer: 72 g of water.

Example 6 (Mass–Volume Relationship):
What volume of CO₂ is produced at STP when 50 g of CaCO₃ decomposes?

Equation: CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g)

Mole Ratio: 1 mol CaCO₃ : 1 mol CO₂

Moles of given: Moles of CaCO₃ = 50 g / 100 g/mol = 0.5 mol

Mole Ratio to Unknown: Moles of CO₂ = 0.5 mol (from 1:1 ratio)

Convert to Volume: Volume of CO₂ = 0.5 mol × 22.4 dm³/mol = 11.2 dm³
Answer: 11.2 dm³ of CO₂ at STP.
Example 7 (Limiting Reactant):
When 10 g of H₂ reacts with 80 g of O₂, what is the limiting reactant and what mass of water is formed?
Equation: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Mole Ratio: 2 mol H₂ : 1 mol O₂

Moles of reactants:

Moles of H₂ = 10 g / 2 g/mol = 5.0 mol

Moles of O₂ = 80 g / 32 g/mol = 2.5 mol

Identify Limiting Reactant:

The ratio requires 2 mol H₂ for every 1 mol O₂.

With 5.0 mol H₂, we need 5.0 / 2 = 2.5 mol of O₂ to react completely. We have exactly 2.5 mol of O₂.
Therefore, there is no limiting reactant; both are used up completely.

Calculate Product: Moles of H₂O produced = 5.0 mol (from 5 mol H₂ and the 1:1 H₂:H₂O ratio).
Mass of H₂O = 5.0 mol × 18 g/mol = 90 g
Answer: No limiting reactant. 90 g of water is formed.

(Note: This is a special case where the reactants are in the exact perfect ratio. Most problems will have one reactant that limits the reaction.)

Common Misconceptions & Pitfalls

Molar Mass: Thinking it has no units. It must be in g/mol.

Atoms vs. Molecules: Confusing O (an atom) with O₂ (a molecule). Specify the particle type.
Avogadro's Number: Forgetting it applies to ions and formula units, not just atoms and molecules.
Molar Volume: Using 22.4 dm³/mol for non-gases or at conditions that are not STP. (At room temperature, it's approximately 24 dm³/mol).

PERCENTAGE COMPOSITION BY MASS

The percentage composition of a compound is the percentage by mass of each element present in the compound relative to the total mass of the compound. It gives us an idea of how much of each element is present in a compound, and is particularly useful in chemical analysis and industrial chemistry.
Mathematical Formula:
Percentage of an element=Total mass of the element in the formulaMolar mass of the compound
×100\text{Percentage of an element} = \frac{\text{Total mass of the element in the formula}}{\text{Molar mass of the compound}} \times 100
Molar mass (Mᵣ) = Sum of relative atomic masses of all the atoms in the formula.

The numerator = (number of atoms of that element × relative atomic mass).

Worked Example 1

Calculate the percentage composition of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen in ethanol (C₂H₆O). Step 1: Find molar mass.
C = 12, H = 1, O = 16

Mᵣ = (2 × 12) + (6 × 1) + (16) = 24 + 6 + 16 = 46 g/mol

Step 2: Find mass contribution of each element.

Carbon: 2 × 12 = 24

Hydrogen: 6 × 1 = 6

Oxygen: 1 × 16 = 16

Step 3: Apply formula.

%C = (24 ÷ 46) × 100 = 52.2%

%H = (6 ÷ 46) × 100 = 13.0%

%O = (16 ÷ 46) × 100 = 34.8%

Final Answer: Ethanol contains 52.2% carbon, 13.0% hydrogen, and 34.8% oxygen.

Worked Example 2

Determine the percentage composition of sodium and chlorine in NaCl.
Step 1: Molar mass = Na (23) + Cl (35.5) = 58.5 g/mol
Step 2: Contributions: Na = 23, Cl = 35.5
Step 3:

%Na = (23 ÷ 58.5) × 100 = 39.3%

%Cl = (35.5 ÷ 58.5) × 100 = 60.7%

Worked Example 3
Calculate the percentage composition of Ca, C, and O in CaCO₃. Step 1: Mᵣ = Ca (40) + C (12) + (3 × 16) = 40 + 12 + 48 = 100 g/mol
Step 2: Contributions: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 48
Step 3:

%Ca = (40 ÷ 100) × 100 = 40%

%C = (12 ÷ 100) × 100 = 12%

%O = (48 ÷ 100) × 100 = 48%

Final Answer: CaCO₃ = 40% Ca, 12% C, 48% O.

Empirical Formula

The empirical formula of a compound shows the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in the compound. It does not necessarily show the actual number of atoms, but it reveals the smallest ratio.

STEPS TO DETERMINE EMPIRICAL FORMULA FROM PERCENTAGE COMPOSITION:
Assume 100 g of the compound (so percentage = grams).

Divide each element’s mass by its relative atomic mass (Ar) to find moles.
Divide all mole values by the smallest mole obtained.

If ratios are not whole numbers, multiply all by a suitable factor (e.g., ×2, ×3) to make them whole.
Write the formula using the whole number ratios.

Worked Example 1

A compound contains 40% C, 6.7% H, and 53.3% O. Find its empirical formula.

Step 1: Assume 100 g.

C = 40 g, H = 6.7 g, O = 53.3 g

Step 2: Convert to moles.

C = 40 ÷ 12 = 3.33

H = 6.7 ÷ 1 = 6.7

O = 53.3 ÷ 16 = 3.33

Step 3: Divide by smallest (3.33).

C = 3.33 ÷ 3.33 = 1

H = 6.7 ÷ 3.33 = 2

O = 3.33 ÷ 3.33 = 1

Step 4: Ratio = 1 : 2 : 1

Final Answer: Empirical formula = CH₂O

Worked Example 2

A substance is found to contain 27.3% C and 72.7% O. Find the empirical formula.

Step 1: Assume 100 g.
C = 27.3 g, O = 72.7 g

Step 2: Convert to moles.

C = 27.3 ÷ 12 = 2.28

O = 72.7 ÷ 16 = 4.54

Step 3: Divide by smallest (2.28).

C = 2.28 ÷ 2.28 = 1

O = 4.54 ÷ 2.28 ≈ 2

Final Answer: Empirical formula = CO₂

RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN EMPIRICAL AND MOLECULAR FORMULA

The molecular formula shows the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule of the compound.
Relationship:

Molecular formula = (Empirical formula)n

Where:

n = Molar mass of the compound Molar mass of empirical formula

Worked Example

The empirical formula of a compound is CH₂O. Its molar mass is 180 g/mol. Find its molecular formula.
Step 1: Find Mᵣ of empirical formula CH₂O.
= 12 + (2 × 1) + 16 = 30
Step 2: Calculate n. n = 180 ÷ 30 = 6
Step 3: Multiply subscripts by 6.
= (CH₂O)₆ = C₆H₁₂O₆

Final Answer: The molecular formula is C₆H₁₂O₆ (Glucose).

Gases are one of the fundamental states of matter, consisting of particles (atoms or molecules) in constant random motion. Unlike solids and liquids, gases have no definite shape or volume, and their particles are widely spaced. Because gases can be easily compressed or expanded, scientists have studied how gases behave under various conditions, leading to the discovery of the gas laws.

WEEK 6: STRUCTURE OF THE ATOM AND ELECTRONIC CONFIGURATION
Bohr’s Model of the Atom; Energy Levels; Rules of Electronic Configuration; Applications in the Periodic Table

Historical Development Leading to Bohr’s Model

Understanding Bohr’s theory requires knowledge of earlier attempts:

Dalton’s Atomic Theory (1803):
Proposed that atoms are indivisible, solid particles. Limitation → did not explain internal structure.
Thomson’s Model (1897):
Plum pudding model: atom as a positively charged sphere with electrons embedded in it. Limitation → could not explain Rutherford’s experiment.
Rutherford’s Nuclear Model (1911):
Gold foil experiment: showed that atoms have a dense, positively charged nucleus with electrons orbiting around it. Limitation → could not explain why electrons did not spiral into the nucleus or why atoms emitted discrete spectra.
Bohr’s Model (1913):
Improved Rutherford’s model by introducing quantized energy levels for electrons.

Bohr’s Postulates of the Atom

Bohr made the following assumptions:

Electrons revolve around the nucleus in fixed paths (orbits/shells) without losing energy.
These orbits are stable and are called stationary states.

Each orbit has a fixed energy value.

Lower orbits = lower energy; higher orbits = higher energy.

Electrons can jump between orbits:

When an electron absorbs energy, it jumps to a higher orbit (excited state).

When it falls back, it emits energy as light (photon).

This explains atomic spectra.

Energy Difference Formula:
The energy emitted or absorbed when an electron changes orbit is given by:

ΔE = E2 − E1 = hν

where h = Planck’s constant (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ Js) and ν = frequency of radiation.

Energy Levels and Shells

The nucleus is surrounded by concentric shells denoted as:

o K (n=1), L (n=2), M (n=3), N (n=4), O (n=5), P (n=6), Q (n=7).

Each shell can hold a maximum of 2n² electrons, where n = principal quantum number.

o K (n=1): 2(1)² = 2

o L (n=2): 2(2)² = 8
o M (n=3): 2(3)² = 18

o N (n=4): 2(4)² = 32

Example: Calcium (Z = 20) has 20 electrons → distribution is 2, 8, 8, 2.

Electronic Configuration of Atoms

Electronic configuration describes how electrons are arranged in shells and subshells.

Subshells: Each energy level is divided into subshells (s, p, d, f).

s → 2 electrons

p → 6 electrons
d → 10 electrons

f → 14 electrons

Aufbau Principle: Electrons occupy the lowest energy orbitals first before filling higher ones.
Pauli’s Exclusion Principle: An orbital can hold a maximum of two electrons, and they must spin in opposite directions.
Hund’s Rule: Electrons fill orbitals singly before pairing up in degenerate orbitals (orbitals of equal energy).
Order of Filling Orbitals (Aufbau order):
1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p → 6s → 4f → 5d → 6p
→ 7s → 5f → 6d → 7p

Examples of Electronic Configuration

Hydrogen (Z = 1): 1s¹ → Shell notation: 1

Carbon (Z = 6): 1s² 2s² 2p² → Shell notation: 2, 4

Sodium (Z = 11): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹ → Shell notation: 2, 8, 1

Chlorine (Z = 17): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁵ → Shell notation: 2, 8, 7

Potassium (Z = 19): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s¹ → Shell notation: 2, 8, 8, 1

Iron (Z = 26): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d⁶

Importance of Electronic Configuration

Explains the valency of elements:

Sodium (2,8,1) → valency = 1

Oxygen (2,6) → valency = 2

Explains chemical properties:
Elements with similar outer electron configurations belong to the same group.

Explains periodicity: Position of elements in the periodic table is based on electronic configuration.
Explains stability of noble gases: Neon (2,8) and Argon (2,8,8) are inert because their outer shells are completely filled.

WEEK 8: IONIC AND COVALENT BONDING – FORMATION AND PROPERTIES

All elements, except the noble gases, are chemically unstable in their free atomic state because their outermost energy levels are not completely filled. In order to achieve stability, atoms combine with one another. This combination is called chemical bonding.
The stability attained by atoms after bonding is usually explained using the octet rule. According to the octet rule:
Atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons until they have eight electrons in their outermost shell, just like the noble gases.
Hydrogen and helium are exceptions because they only need two electrons to achieve stability (duplet rule).
There are different types of chemical bonds, but the most common and important are ionic (electrovalent) bonding and covalent bonding.

IONIC (ELECTROVALENT) BONDING

Definition

Ionic bonding is the type of chemical bonding formed when atoms transfer electrons from one atom (usually a metal) to another atom (usually a non-metal). The atom that loses electrons becomes a positively charged ion (cation), while the atom that gains electrons becomes a negatively charged ion (anion). The strong electrostatic force of attraction between the oppositely charged ions is called an ionic bond.
Steps in Ionic Bond Formation

One atom loses one or more electrons to attain a stable noble gas configuration.

Another atom gains these electrons to also attain stability.

Oppositely charged ions are formed.

A strong electrostatic attraction holds them together.

Conditions for Ionic Bond Formation

Large difference in electronegativity (greater than 1.7) between the two atoms.

Usually occurs between Group 1–3 metals and Group 5–7 non-metals.

Metals have low ionization energy (lose electrons easily).

Non-metals have high electron affinity (gain electrons easily).

Examples of Ionic Bond Formation

Sodium Chloride (NaCl):

Sodium (atomic number 11): 2,8,1 → loses 1 electron → Na⁺.

Chlorine (atomic number 17): 2,8,7 → gains 1 electron → Cl⁻.
Result: Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are held together by electrostatic attraction in a giant ionic lattice.
Na→Na++e−Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ Cl+e−→Cl−Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻ Na++Cl−→NaClNa⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl
Magnesium Oxide (MgO):

Mg (atomic number 12): 2,8,2 → loses 2 electrons → Mg²⁺.

O (atomic number 8): 2,6 → gains 2 electrons → O²⁻.

Result: MgO is formed with strong ionic bonds.

Calcium Fluoride (CaF₂):

Ca (2,8,8,2) → loses 2 electrons → Ca²⁺.

Each F atom (2,7) → gains 1 electron → 2F⁻.

Result: CaF₂ crystal structure.

Properties of Ionic Compounds

State: Crystalline solids at room temperature due to strong lattice.

Melting and boiling points: Very high because of strong ionic bonds.

Solubility: Generally soluble in polar solvents like water but insoluble in non-polar solvents (benzene, petrol).
Electrical conductivity:

Do not conduct electricity in solid state (ions fixed in lattice).

Conduct electricity in molten or aqueous state (ions are mobile).

Hardness: Hard but brittle. Ionic crystals shatter when stress is applied because like charges are forced close together, causing repulsion.
COVALENT BONDING

Definition

Covalent bonding is the type of chemical bonding formed when two atoms share one or more pairs of electrons in order to attain stability (noble gas configuration).
Conditions for Covalent Bond Formation

Occurs mainly between non-metal atoms.

Difference in electronegativity is small (less than 1.7).

Atoms need to complete their octets by sharing electrons instead of losing or gaining them.
Types of Covalent Bonds

Single Covalent Bond: Formed when one pair of electrons is shared.

Example: Hydrogen molecule (H₂), H–H.

Each hydrogen contributes 1 electron to the shared pair.

Double Covalent Bond: Formed when two pairs of electrons are shared.

Example: Oxygen molecule (O₂), O=O.

Each oxygen atom shares 2 electrons.

Triple Covalent Bond: Formed when three pairs of electrons are shared.

Example: Nitrogen molecule (N₂), N≡N.

Each nitrogen atom shares 3 electrons.

Coordinate (Dative) Covalent Bond: A special type where one atom donates both electrons for the shared pair.
Example: Formation of ammonium ion (NH₄⁺) from ammonia and hydrogen ion.

Examples of Covalent Compounds
Water (H₂O): O (2,6) shares 2 electrons with 2 H atoms.

Methane (CH₄): C (2,4) shares 4 electrons with 4 H atoms.

Carbon dioxide (CO₂): C (2,4) shares 2 electrons with each O atom, forming two double bonds.
Properties of Covalent Compounds

State: Usually gases (O₂, CO₂), liquids (H₂O), or soft solids (sulphur, iodine).

Melting and boiling points: Generally low because molecules are held together by weak intermolecular forces. Exception: diamond and graphite (giant covalent structures).
Solubility: Mostly insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents.

Electrical conductivity: Poor conductors as they lack free ions or electrons. Exception: graphite (due to delocalized electrons).
Bond directionality: Bonds are directional (exist between specific atoms), unlike ionic bonds which are non-directional.

WEEK 9: OXIDATION AND REDUCTION

Definitions, Examples, and Redox Reactions

Introduction

Every chemical reaction involves the rearrangement of atoms. In many reactions, electrons are transferred from one species to another. Such reactions are called oxidation-reduction (redox) reactions.
Redox reactions play a vital role in biological systems (respiration, photosynthesis), industrial processes (extraction of metals, production of energy in batteries), and environmental processes (corrosion, water purification).
To understand redox processes, we use three main approaches:
Classical (oxygen/hydrogen transfer)

Electron transfer

Oxidation number method

Definitions of Oxidation and Reduction

Classical Definitions

Oxidation: Addition of oxygen or removal of hydrogen.

Reduction: Removal of oxygen or addition of hydrogen.

Examples:

2Mg + O2 → 2MgO2

Magnesium oxidized (oxygen added).

Oxygen reduced (electrons accepted).

CuO + H2 → Cu + H2O

CuO reduced (oxygen removed).

H₂ oxidized (oxygen added).

Electron Transfer Definitions

Oxidation: Loss of electrons. (OIL – Oxidation Is Loss).

Reduction: Gain of electrons. (RIG – Reduction Is Gain).

Examples:

Na → Na++ e− → Na is oxidized.

Cl2 + 2e− → 2Cl− → Cl₂ is reduced.
Oxidation Number Definitions

Oxidation: Increase in oxidation number.

Reduction: Decrease in oxidation number.

Examples:

1. Fe2+ → Fe3+ + e−

Fe²⁺ is oxidized (oxidation number increases from +2 to +3).

2. Cl2 + 2e−→ 2Cl−

Cl₂ is reduced (oxidation number decreases from 0 to –1).

Redox Reactions

A redox reaction occurs when oxidation and reduction take place simultaneously.

The substance oxidized is the reducing agent (it donates electrons).

The substance reduced is the oxidizing agent (it accepts electrons).

Example 1:

Zn + CuSO4 → ZnSO4 + Cu

Zn oxidized (Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻).

Cu²⁺ reduced (Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu).

Zn = reducing agent, Cu²⁺ = oxidizing agent.

Example 2:

2Na + Cl2 → 2NaCl

Na oxidized (Na → Na⁺ + e⁻).

Cl₂ reduced (Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻).
Oxidizing and Reducing Agents

Oxidizing Agent (Oxidant): Accepts electrons, gets reduced.

Examples: O₂, Cl₂, KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇, HNO₃.

Reducing Agent (Reductant): Donates electrons, gets oxidized.

Examples: H₂, CO, Na, Zn, Fe.

Examples of Redox Reactions

Combination Reactions:
4Na + O2 → 2Na2O

Displacement Reactions:
Zn + CuSO4→ZnSO4 + Cu

Combustion Reactions:
CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O

Carbon oxidized from –4 to +4.

Oxygen reduced from 0 to –2.

Corrosion (Rusting):
4Fe + 3O2 + 6H2O → 4Fe(OH)3

Balancing Redox Reactions

There are two main methods:

Oxidation Number Method

Steps:

Assign oxidation numbers to all elements.

Identify oxidation and reduction changes.
Balance the number of electrons lost and gained.

Balance atoms and charges.

Ion–Electron Method

Write oxidation and reduction half-reactions separately.

Balance atoms (except H and O).

Balance O using H₂O.

Balance H using H⁺ (or OH⁻ in alkaline medium).

Balance charges using electrons.

Add half-reactions, canceling electrons.

Applications of Redox Reactions

Biological Systems

Respiration: Glucose oxidized to CO₂ and H₂O, releasing energy.

Extraction of metals (e.g., Fe from Fe₂O₃ by reduction with CO).

Environmental Applications

Common Misconceptions

Oxidation is not only “addition of oxygen”; it may involve electron loss or increase in oxidation number.
Reduction is not only “removal of oxygen”; it may involve electron gain or decrease in oxidation number.
Oxidation and reduction always occur together; one cannot happen without the other.

The oxidizing agent is reduced and the reducing agent is oxidized.

WEEK 10: WATER AND SOLUTION – PROPERTIES, TYPES OF SOLUTIONS, SOLUBILITY

WATER

Water is the most abundant compound on Earth and is often described as the universal solvent because of its ability to dissolve a wide variety of substances. It exists naturally in three states: solid (ice), liquid (water), and gas (water vapour).
Importance of Water

Biological importance:

Acts as a transport medium for nutrients and waste products in plants and animals.

Maintains body temperature through sweating and evaporation.
Participates in photosynthesis:

6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

Industrial importance:

Used as a raw material in the manufacture of chemicals (e.g., H₂, O₂, H₂SO₄).

Acts as a coolant in power plants and vehicles.

Used as a cleaning and dissolving agent.

Domestic importance:

Drinking, cooking, washing, sanitation.

Physical Properties of Water

Pure water is a colourless, odourless, and tasteless liquid.

Boiling point: 100°C at 1 atm pressure.

Freezing point: 0°C at 1 atm pressure.

Density: 1 g/cm³ at 4°C.

Water expands upon freezing, making ice less dense (ice floats on water).

Water has a high specific heat capacity, meaning it can absorb or release large amounts of heat without a large change in temperature. This moderates climate.
Water has high surface tension due to hydrogen bonding, allowing small insects to walk on its surface.
Electrical conductivity: Pure water is a poor conductor of electricity, but natural water conducts electricity due to dissolved ions.

Chemical Properties of Water
Reaction with metals:

Active metals such as sodium, potassium, and calcium react with cold water to form hydroxides and release hydrogen gas.
2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂↑

Less reactive metals (e.g., Mg, Zn) react only with steam.

Mg + H₂O (steam) → MgO + H₂↑

Reaction with non-metals:

Chlorine dissolves in water to form a mixture of hydrochloric acid and hypochlorous acid:
Cl₂ + H₂O → HCl + HOCl

Hydrolysis: Some salts undergo hydrolysis in water.

Example: NH₄Cl + H₂O → NH₄OH + HCl

Electrolysis:

Water decomposes when an electric current is passed through acidified water:

2H₂O → 2H₂↑ + O₂↑

SOLUTION

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances in which one substance (solute) is uniformly dispersed within another (solvent).
Solute: Substance being dissolved (lesser amount).

Solvent: Substance that dissolves the solute (greater amount).

Examples:

Salt solution: NaCl (solute) + Water (solvent).
Sugar solution: Sugar (solute) + Water (solvent).

Types of Solutions

According to Physical State of Solvent:

Solid solutions: Brass (Cu + Zn), Bronze (Cu + Sn).

Liquid solutions: Vinegar (CH₃COOH in H₂O), sugar solution.

Gaseous solutions: Air (mixture of gases).

According to Solubility Level:

Unsaturated solution: More solute can dissolve at a given temperature.

Saturated solution: Contains the maximum amount of solute at a given temperature; any additional solute remains undissolved.
Supersaturated solution: Contains more solute than normal; prepared by heating a saturated solution and cooling gently without disturbance.
According to Electrical Conductivity:

Electrolytic solutions: Conduct electricity (e.g., NaCl solution, HCl solution).

Non-electrolytic solutions: Do not conduct electricity (e.g., sugar in water, ethanol in water).

Properties of Solutions

They are homogeneous mixtures with uniform composition.

Particles are molecular-sized and cannot be seen with the naked eye.

They are transparent and do not scatter light (no Tyndall effect).

Solute cannot be separated by filtration but by evaporation or distillation.
Concentration varies — solutions may be dilute or concentrated.

SOLUBILITY

Definition:
The solubility of a substance is the maximum amount of solute that can dissolve in 100 g of solvent at a given temperature to form a saturated solution.
Unit: g solute / 100 g solvent (at a specified temperature).

Factors Affecting Solubility

Nature of solute and solvent:

Polar solutes dissolve in polar solvents (e.g., NaCl in H₂O).

Non-polar solutes dissolve in non-polar solvents (e.g., iodine in Cl₄).

Temperature:

For most solids, solubility increases with temperature (e.g., KNO₃).

For gases, solubility decreases with increase in temperature.

Pressure:

Has little effect on solids and liquids.

For gases, solubility increases with pressure (Henry’s Law). Example: CO₂ in soft drinks.
Particle size:

Smaller particles dissolve faster due to larger surface area.

Agitation:

Stirring increases the rate of dissolution.

Solubility Curves

A solubility curve shows how the solubility of a substance varies with temperature.

It can be used to:

Determine the solubility of a salt at a given temperature.

Predict the temperature at which a solution becomes saturated.

Calculate the amount of solute that crystallizes when a hot saturated solution is cooled.
Worked Example 1:
The solubility of KNO₃ at 40°C is 64 g per 100 g of water. How much KNO₃ will crystallize if a saturated solution containing 64 g at 40°C is cooled to 20°C where solubility is 32 g per 100 g of water?
Solution:
Amount crystallized = 64 g – 32 g = 32 g

Applications of Solubility

Crystallization: Used in purification of substances such as sugar and salts.

Drug formulation: Determines the maximum dose of drugs soluble in blood plasma.

Food industry: Explains dissolving of salt and sugar in water-based foods and drinks.

Environmental science: Solubility of oxygen in water is vital for aquatic life.

Industrial processes: Fertilizer production, beverage carbonation, soap and detergent manufacturing.

WEEK 1: INTRODUCTION TO ORGANIC CHEMISTRY

Characteristics of Organic Compounds

MEANING OF ORGANIC CHEMISTRY

Organic Chemistry is the branch of Chemistry that deals with the study of carbon compounds, particularly those containing carbon–hydrogen bonds (hydrocarbons) and their derivatives.
The term organic originally came from the belief that compounds from living organisms could only be formed by a “vital force.” This was disproved in 1828 when Friedrich Wöhler accidentally synthesized urea (an organic compound found in urine) from ammonium cyanate
(an inorganic salt). This historic event marked the birth of modern Organic Chemistry, proving that organic compounds can be synthesized artificially.
Today, Organic Chemistry is not limited to compounds from living organisms; it covers all carbon compounds, except a few simple ones like carbon dioxide, carbonates, bicarbonates, and carbides (which are studied under Inorganic Chemistry).

IMPORTANCE OF ORGANIC CHEMISTRY

Organic Chemistry is crucial because it influences every aspect of human life. Its importance can be grouped as follows:
In Everyday Life

The food we eat (carbohydrates, proteins, lipids) is organic.

The medicines we take (antibiotics, painkillers, vitamins, vaccines) are organic.

Clothing materials such as cotton, wool, nylon, polyester, and rayon are organic.

Fuels (petrol, diesel, kerosene, cooking gas, coal) are organic.

In Industry

Petrochemical industries rely on hydrocarbons for fuels and raw materials.

Pharmaceutical industries depend on organic synthesis for drug production.

Plastic, paint, rubber, and cosmetic industries are based on organic compounds.

In Agriculture

Fertilizers, pesticides, herbicides, and growth hormones contain organic compounds.

In Nature

Life itself is built around carbon chemistry. DNA, RNA, enzymes, and hormones are organic molecules that control heredity and metabolic processes.
GENERAL CHARACTERISTICS OF ORGANIC COMPOUNDS

Organic compounds have unique properties due to the nature of carbon atoms and the types of bonds they form. The main characteristics include:
Presence of Carbon and Hydrogen

Organic compounds always contain carbon, usually bonded with hydrogen.

Carbon’s ability to catenate (form long chains, branched chains, and rings with itself) makes the variety of organic compounds almost limitless.
For example:

Methane (CH₄) → simplest hydrocarbon.

Hexane (C₆H₁₄) → long chain.

Benzene (C₆H₆) → ring compound.

Tetravalency of Carbon

Carbon has an atomic number of 6, with an electronic configuration of 2, 4.

It has 4 valence electrons and needs 4 more to complete its octet.

Therefore, it always forms four covalent bonds with other atoms, allowing for stable molecules.
Example: Methane, CH₄.

Covalent Bonding

The atoms in organic compounds are linked mainly by covalent bonds (shared electron pairs).
This makes organic compounds generally less soluble in water and have lower melting/boiling points compared to ionic salts.
Combustibility

Most organic compounds are combustible.
They burn in oxygen to produce carbon dioxide and water, often with much heat energy.

Example: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + heat.

Hydrocarbons with higher carbon content burn with a sooty flame due to incomplete combustion.
Solubility

Organic compounds are generally insoluble in water (polar solvent) but soluble in organic solvents such as benzene, chloroform, ethanol, and ether.
Reason: Organic compounds are mostly non-polar or weakly polar, and solubility depends on similarity of polarity (“like dissolves like”).
Thermal Instability

Many organic compounds decompose easily when heated strongly.

For instance, sugar (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁) chars on strong heating to give carbon and water vapour.

Slow Reaction Rate

Organic reactions are usually slow and often require special conditions such as heat, pressure, or catalysts (e.g., platinum, nickel).
Example: Hydrogenation of ethene to ethane requires nickel catalyst.

Formation of Isomers

Organic compounds exhibit isomerism – the existence of two or more compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural or spatial arrangements.
Example: C₄H₁₀ → Butane and 2-methylpropane (isobutane).

This greatly increases the number of possible organic compounds.

Large Number of Compounds

Over 90% of known chemical compounds are organic. This is due to:

Catenation – ability of carbon to bond with itself extensively.
Tetravalency – carbon forms up to 4 bonds.

Multiple bonding – carbon forms single, double, and triple bonds.

Isomerism – different arrangements of the same atoms.

Low Melting and Boiling Points

Most organic compounds are liquids or gases at room temperature due to weak intermolecular forces (Van der Waals forces).
Solids like glucose and urea decompose before melting.

Distinctive Odours

Many organic compounds have characteristic odours.

Esters smell fruity.

Amines have a fishy smell.

Aldehydes and ketones have sharp odours.

WEEK 2: ALKANES, ALKENES, AND ALKYNES

Structure, Properties, and Examples

INTRODUCTION TO HYDROCARBONS

Definition: Hydrocarbons are organic compounds made up of only carbon (C) and
hydrogen (H) atoms.

Classification:

Alkanes (Saturated hydrocarbons): All bonds are single covalent bonds (σ- bonds).
Alkenes (Unsaturated hydrocarbons): Contain at least one double bond (C=C)
consisting of one sigma (σ) and one pi (π) bond.

Alkynes (Unsaturated hydrocarbons): Contain at least one triple bond (C≡C)
consisting of one sigma (σ) and two pi (π) bonds.

Homologous Series:
A family of organic compounds with:

The same general formula.

A difference of a –CH₂– group between successive members.

Similar chemical properties.

Gradual change in physical properties.

Alkanes (Saturated Hydrocarbons) General Formula: CnH2n+2C_nH_{2n+2} Bonding: Only single bonds (σ-bonds).
Examples:

Methane (CH₄)

Ethane (C₂H₆)

Propane (C₃H₈)

Butane (C₄H₁₀)

Isomerism:

From butane (C₄H₁₀) upwards, alkanes show structural isomerism.

n-butane: CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃

iso-butane: CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₃
Physical Properties:

State: C₁–C₄ are gases, C₅–C₁₇ are liquids, C₁₈ and above are waxy solids.

Melting/boiling points: Increase with molecular size due to stronger van der Waals forces.
Polarity: Non-polar, insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents.

Chemical Properties:

Alkanes are chemically inert due to strong C–C and C–H bonds.

Combustion:

Complete: CH4+2O2→CO2+2H2OCH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

Incomplete: Produces CO or C (soot).

Substitution reaction:

In the presence of UV light or heat, halogens replace hydrogen:
CH4+Cl2→CH3Cl+HClCH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl

Further substitution yields CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃, and CCl₄.

General Formula: CnH2nC_nH_{2n} Bonding: At least one double bond (σ + π). Examples:
Ethene (C₂H₄): H₂C=CH₂

Geometrical isomerism (cis/trans):

cis-but-2-ene (CH₃ groups on same side).

trans-but-2-ene (CH₃ groups on opposite sides).

Physical Properties:

Similar to alkanes but slightly more polarizable due to π-electrons.

Lower members are gases; middle members are liquids; higher ones are waxy solids.

WEEK 3: ISOMERISM – STRUCTURAL AND GEOMETRIC

MEANING OF ISOMERISM

Isomerism is a very important concept in organic chemistry. It is the phenomenon where two or more compounds have the same molecular formula (same number and type of atoms) but are arranged differently, leading to different structural formulas and often different physical and chemical properties.
Compounds that show this relationship are called isomers.

Isomerism explains why organic chemistry has such a vast number of compounds, even though they are made mainly of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and halogens.
Example:

Butane (C₄H₁₀) has two isomers:

n-Butane → CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃

2-Methylpropane → CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₃

Both have the same molecular formula (C₄H₁₀) but different structures and properties.

TYPES OF ISOMERISM

Isomerism is broadly divided into:

Structural Isomerism – when isomers differ in how atoms are bonded together.

Stereoisomerism – when isomers differ in the spatial arrangement of atoms (same connectivity but different 3D orientation).
In SS1, we focus on structural isomerism and one type of stereoisomerism: geometrical isomerism.

STRUCTURAL ISOMERISM

Structural isomerism occurs when compounds have the same molecular formula but different bonding arrangements of atoms.
Types of Structural Isomerism

Chain Isomerism (Skeletal Isomerism):

Occurs when compounds differ in the arrangement of the carbon skeleton (straight chain vs branched chain).
Example:

n-Butane: CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃

2-Methylpropane: CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₃
→ Both are C₄H₁₀ but have different structures and boiling points.

Position Isomerism:

Occurs when a functional group or substituent is attached at different positions on the carbon chain.
Example:

Butan-1-ol: CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂OH (OH at position 1)

Butan-2-ol: CH₃–CH₂–CHOH–CH₃ (OH at position 2).

→ Both are alcohols with formula C₄H₁₀O but have different properties.

Functional Group Isomerism:

Occurs when compounds have the same molecular formula but belong to different homologous series (different functional groups).
Example:

Ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH) – an alcohol.

Dimethyl ether (CH₃OCH₃) – an ether.

→ Both are C₂H₆O but very different in chemistry.

Tautomeric (Dynamic) Isomerism:

A special type where two isomers exist in equilibrium and interconvert easily.

Most common is keto-enol tautomerism.

Example:

Keto form: CH₃–CO–CH₂–CH₃ (Butan-2-one).
Enol form: CH₂=CH–CH(OH)–CH₃ (But-2-en-ol).

Metamerism:

Occurs in compounds with a divalent functional group (like –O–, –S–, or –CO– NH–) where alkyl groups on either side are different.
Example:

Diethyl ether (CH₃CH₂–O–CH₂CH₃).

Methyl propyl ether (CH₃–O–CH₂CH₂CH₃).

GEOMETRICAL ISOMERISM (Cis-Trans Isomerism)

Geometrical isomerism is a form of stereoisomerism. It occurs when there is restricted rotation around a double bond or within a ring structure, leading to different spatial arrangements of atoms.
Features of Geometrical Isomerism

Found mainly in alkenes and cyclic compounds.

Due to the rigidity of the C=C double bond, groups attached cannot rotate freely.

Isomers are named as cis (same side) and trans (opposite side).

Examples

But-2-ene (C₄H₈):

Cis-but-2-ene: CH₃ groups on the same side of the double bond.

Trans-but-2-ene: CH₃ groups on opposite sides of the double bond.

1,2-Dichloroethene (C₂H₂Cl₂):

Cis form: Both Cl atoms on the same side.
Trans form: Cl atoms on opposite sides.

PROPERTIES OF ISOMERS

Structural isomers:

May differ in boiling and melting points.

Different solubilities and densities.

Petroleum is a naturally occurring, viscous, dark brown or black, flammable liquid found in underground geological formations, often trapped in porous rocks beneath impermeable rock layers.

Meaning of Chemical Reactions

A chemical reaction is a process in which substances (reactants) interact to form new substances (products) with different chemical compositions and properties.
In a chemical reaction:

Bonds in reactants are broken.

New bonds are formed in products.
Example:

Hydrogen and oxygen (reactants) form water (product).

Characteristics of Chemical Reactions:

Energy change → reactions may release or absorb energy (exothermic or endothermic).

Observable changes → gas evolution, colour change, heat/light, precipitate formation.

Mass is conserved → Law of Conservation of Mass states: “Matter is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.”
Irreversibility → many reactions cannot be easily reversed.

Everyday Examples of Chemical Reactions:

Cooking of food (chemical change in starch and proteins).

Burning of fuel (combustion reaction).

Rusting of iron (slow chemical reaction).

Photosynthesis in plants (sunlight-driven chemical process).

Types of Chemical Reactions

We shall study three common types: Combination, Decomposition, and Displacement.

Combination (Synthesis) Reactions

Definition: Two or more simple substances react chemically to form a single, more complex product.
General Form:

Features:

Exothermic (releases heat/light).

Only one product is formed.

Common in combustion and industrial synthesis.

Everyday Applications:

Combustion of fuels for energy.

Photosynthesis in plants (6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + O₂).

Cement industry (lime reacting with water).

Decomposition Reactions

Definition: A single compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances.

General Form:

Features:

Requires energy input (endothermic).

Produces two or more products.

Used in laboratory preparation of gases (e.g., O₂ from KClO₃).

Everyday Applications:

Photosynthesis (photolytic decomposition of water in chloroplasts).

Rechargeable batteries (electrolysis processes).

Decomposition of food → spoilage.

Displacement Reactions

Definition: A more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its compound.

Explanation:

In (1), zinc is more reactive than copper, so zinc displaces copper.

In (3), chlorine is more reactive than bromine, so it displaces bromine.

Features:

Guided by the reactivity series of metals and halogens.

More reactive element replaces a less reactive one.

Used in extraction of metals (e.g., Fe from Fe₂O₃ using C).

Everyday Applications:

Galvanization (zinc protects iron by reacting first).

Recovery of metals from solutions.

Halogen displacement reactions in water purification.

WEEK 8: ENERGY CHANGES IN REACTIONS

Endothermic and exothermic

INTRODUCTION

All chemical reactions involve changes in energy. Energy can exist in various forms such as:

Heat (thermal energy)

Light

Sound

Electrical energy
During a reaction, energy can either be released to the surroundings or absorbed from the surroundings. The direction of energy flow determines whether a reaction is exothermic or endothermic.
Understanding energy changes is crucial because:

It helps predict whether a reaction will occur spontaneously.

It explains temperature changes in the surroundings.

It underpins the use of chemical energy in industries, biology, and everyday life.

DEFINITIONS

Exothermic Reaction: A chemical reaction that releases heat energy (or other forms of energy) to the surroundings. The temperature of the surroundings increases.
Example: Combustion of fuels – CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + heat.

Endothermic Reaction: A chemical reaction that absorbs heat energy (or other forms of energy) from the surroundings. The temperature of the surroundings decreases.
Example: Thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate – CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (heat absorbed).

PRINCIPLES OF ENERGY CHANGES IN CHEMICAL REACTIONS

Law of Conservation of Energy: Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only be
transferred from one form to another.

In chemical reactions, energy is transferred between the reacting system and its surroundings.
Enthalpy (ΔH):

Enthalpy is the total heat content of a system at constant pressure.

ΔH < 0: Exothermic reaction (energy released).
ΔH > 0: Endothermic reaction (energy absorbed).

Activation Energy (Ea):

Even exothermic reactions require a minimum energy to initiate the reaction.

This is called the activation energy, necessary to break bonds in reactants before new bonds can form in products.

ENERGY PROFILE

Exothermic Reaction Description:
Reactants have higher energy than products.

Energy is released as the reaction progresses.

ΔH is negative.

Example: Combustion of methane – CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + heat.

Endothermic Reaction Description:
Reactants have lower energy than products.
Energy is absorbed from surroundings.

ΔH is positive.

Example: Thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate – CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂.

CHARACTERISTICS OF EXOTHERMIC REACTIONS

Heat energy is released to the surroundings.

Surroundings experience a temperature increase.

Enthalpy change (ΔH) is negative.

Products are more stable than reactants.

Occurs spontaneously if sufficient activation energy is provided.

Examples:

Combustion of fuels (CH₄, C₃H₈, petrol)

Respiration (C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy)

Neutralization reactions (HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O + heat)

Freezing of water (exothermic phase change)

CHARACTERISTICS OF ENDOTHERMIC REACTIONS

Heat energy is absorbed from the surroundings.

Surroundings experience a temperature decrease.

Enthalpy change (ΔH) is positive.

Products are less stable than reactants.

Often non-spontaneous without continuous energy supply.

Examples:
Photosynthesis: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + sunlight → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

Thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

Dissolution of ammonium chloride in water

Electrolysis of water: 2H₂O + energy → 2H₂ + O₂

WEEK 9: RATES OF CHEMICAL REACTIONS – FACTORS AFFECTING RATE

Concept of Rate of Reaction

Definition: The rate of a chemical reaction is the measure of how fast reactants are converted into products in a chemical process.
It tells us how much reactant is used up or how much product is formed in a given time.
Mathematically:

Ways of Measuring Rate of Reaction

Gas evolution: Using gas syringe or inverted burette (e.g., Mg + HCl).

Mass change: Place reaction vessel on a balance and record loss in mass (e.g., CaCO₃ + HCl → CO₂).
Colour change: Observe using colorimeter or naked eye (e.g., iodine clock reaction).

Turbidity/Precipitate formation: Measure time for a mark to disappear through cloudy solution (Na₂S₂O₃ + HCl → S).

Factors Affecting Rate of Reaction

Nature of Reactants

Bond type and bond strength affect rate.

Ionic reactions (in solution) are faster than covalent reactions (involving bond breaking).

Example:

Neutralization: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O (very fast).

Reaction between methane and chlorine (CH₄ + Cl₂) requires UV light (slow without).
Concentration of Reactants

Higher concentration = more particles per unit volume = greater collision frequency = faster reaction.
Example:

2M HCl reacts faster with Mg ribbon than 0.5M HCl.

Graphical Representation: Steeper slope of volume vs time curve for higher concentration.
Temperature

Higher temperature increases kinetic energy of particles.

More particles have energy ≥ activation energy (Eₐ).

Increases both frequency and effectiveness of collisions.

Rule: Rate approximately doubles for every 10°C rise in temperature.

Example: Reaction between Na₂S₂O₃ and HCl is much faster at 60°C than 25°C.

Pressure (for gaseous reactions)

Increasing pressure reduces gas volume → more molecules per unit volume → more collisions.
Example: Haber process:

works best at high pressure.

Surface Area of Reactants

Reaction occurs at surfaces. Finely divided solids expose more area for collision.
Example:

Powdered CaCO₃ reacts faster with HCl than marble chips.

Sawdust burns faster than logs.

Presence of a Catalyst

Definition: A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of reaction without being consumed or permanently changed.
Mechanism: Provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy.

Examples:

MnO₂ catalyzes decomposition of H₂O₂ → O₂.

Fe catalyzes Haber process (NH₃ synthesis).

Ni catalyzes hydrogenation of vegetable oils.

Collision Theory of Chemical Reactions

For reaction to occur:

Particles must collide.

Collisions must be effective (with correct orientation and sufficient energy).

The energy required to initiate reaction is called activation energy (Eₐ).

Energy Profile Diagram:

Without catalyst: High activation energy barrier.

With catalyst: Lower activation energy, faster reaction.

Experimental Demonstrations

Effect of Concentration: Magnesium + HCl with varying molarity; measure H₂ evolution.
Effect of Temperature: Na₂S₂O₃ + HCl at different temperatures; time disappearance of cross mark.
Effect of Catalyst: Decomposition of H₂O₂ with MnO₂; rapid oxygen evolution.

Effect of Surface Area: CaCO₃ powder vs marble chips in HCl.

Industrial Relevance of Rate of Reaction

Haber Process: Ammonia production (Fe catalyst, 200 atm, 450°C).

Contact Process: Sulphuric acid production (V₂O₅ catalyst, 450°C).

Hydrogenation of oils: Manufacture of margarine (Ni catalyst).

Polymerization: Catalysts control rate and yield of plastics.

WEEK 10: REVERSIBLE REACTIONS AND CHEMICAL EQUILIBRIUM – LE CHATELIER’S PRINCIPLE

REVERSIBLE AND IRREVERSIBLE REACTIONS

Irreversible Reactions

Irreversible reactions proceed only in one direction, from reactants to products, and cannot revert under normal conditions.
Once completed, the reactants are fully converted to products.

Reversible Reactions

Reversible reactions can proceed in both forward and backward directions under the same conditions.
Represented with a double arrow (⇌).

CHEMICAL EQUILIBRIUM

When a reversible reaction is carried out in a closed system, the reaction proceeds in both directions until a dynamic equilibrium is reached.
At equilibrium:

Rate of forward reaction = Rate of backward reaction

Concentrations of reactants and products remain constant

The system is dynamic: molecules continue to react, but overall composition does not change
Example:

Initially, formation of NH₃ is fast, but as NH₃ accumulates, the backward decomposition becomes significant. Eventually, the rates equalize.

FACTORS AFFECTING CHEMICAL EQUILIBRIUM

Le Chatelier’s Principle:

If a system at equilibrium is disturbed by a change in concentration, temperature, or pressure, the system shifts to counteract the change and restore equilibrium.

Effect of Concentration

Increasing the concentration of reactants shifts equilibrium toward products.

Increasing the concentration of products shifts equilibrium toward reactants.

Example:

Adding more H₂ shifts equilibrium → right (more NH₃).

Adding NH₃ shifts equilibrium → left (more N₂ + H₂).

Effect of Pressure (gaseous reactions)

Increasing pressure favors the side with fewer moles of gas.

Decreasing pressure favors the side with more moles of gas.

Example:

Left: 4 moles of gas; Right: 2 moles

Increasing pressure → equilibrium shifts right (more NH₃).

Decreasing pressure → equilibrium shifts left (more N₂ + H₂).

Effect of Temperature

For exothermic reactions (release heat):

Increasing temperature → equilibrium shifts backward (toward reactants).

Decreasing temperature → equilibrium shifts forward (toward products).

For endothermic reactions (absorb heat):

Increasing temperature → equilibrium shifts forward (toward products).

Decreasing temperature → equilibrium shifts backward (toward reactants).

Example:

Forward reaction exothermic.

Increase temperature → less NH₃ formed.

Decrease temperature → more NH₃ formed.

Effect of Catalyst

A catalyst speeds up both forward and backward reactions equally.

It allows the system to reach equilibrium faster but does not change the position of equilibrium.
EQUILIBRIUM CONSTANT (Kc)

At equilibrium, the ratio of product concentrations to reactant concentrations, raised to the power of their coefficients, is constant.

Example:
For the reaction:

A large Kc → products favored A small Kc → reactants favored

INDUSTRIAL APPLICATIONS

Haber Process (NH₃ production):

N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ + Heat

High pressure (200 atm) favors fewer gas molecules → more NH₃

Moderate temperature (450 °C) compromises yield & rate
Iron catalyst with promoters increases rate

Contact Process (SO₃ production):

2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ ΔH = -197 kJ/mol

High pressure & low temperature favors forward reaction

Vanadium(V) oxide catalyst speeds up reaction

Food & Biological Systems:

Dissolved gases in blood (O₂, CO₂) obey equilibrium principles.

Le Chatelier’s principle explains gas exchange in lungs.

WEEK 11: METALS AND NON-METALS – PROPERTIES AND USES

Introduction

Metals and non-metals are two broad categories of elements found on the Periodic Table.
They are classified based on their physical and chemical properties.

Metals are generally found on the left and center of the Periodic Table, while non- metals are found on the right side.
Understanding their properties is crucial because they form the building blocks of materials used in industry, agriculture, construction, medicine, and technology.

Metals

Metals are elements that readily lose electrons to form positive ions (cations) and exhibit
metallic bonding.

General Physical Properties of Metals:

Malleability: Can be hammered or rolled into sheets (e.g., aluminium foil).

Ductility: Can be drawn into wires (e.g., copper wires).

Good Conductors of Heat and Electricity: Due to free movement of delocalized electrons.
High Melting and Boiling Points: Most metals, except mercury, are solid at room temperature.
High Density: Metals like iron, copper, and gold are heavy.

Metallic Lustre: Shiny appearance when polished.

Sonority: Produce ringing sounds when struck.

Strength: Many metals (iron, steel) are strong and durable.

Tensile Strength: Ability to resist breaking under tension.

Alloy Formation: Metals can be mixed to form alloys (e.g., brass, bronze, steel).

General Chemical Properties of Metals:

Electropositivity: Metals lose electrons easily to form positive ions.

Reaction with Oxygen: Metals form basic oxides, e.g., 4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O.

Reaction with Water: Metals like sodium react vigorously; some, like calcium, react slowly; metals like gold are unreactive.
Reaction with Acids: Most metals (except Cu, Hg, Ag, Au, Pt) react with dilute acids to form salts and hydrogen gas.
Reaction with Chlorine: Metals readily form chlorides, e.g., 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl.

Form Ionic Compounds: Metals form ionic bonds by donating electrons.

Non-Metals

Non-metals are elements that tend to gain or share electrons to form negative ions (anions) or covalent compounds.
General Physical Properties of Non-Metals:

Poor Conductors: Generally insulators (except graphite).

Low Melting and Boiling Points: Many are gases or brittle solids at room temperature.

Non-Malleable and Non-Ductile: They shatter when hammered.

Low Density: Non-metals like sulfur and phosphorus are light.

No Metallic Lustre: Usually dull in appearance.

Brittle: Break easily when struck.

Existence in Different States: Some non-metals are gases (oxygen), liquids (bromine), or solids (sulfur).
Allotropes: Some non-metals exist in different physical forms, e.g., carbon (diamond, graphite), oxygen (O₂, O₃).
General Chemical Properties of Non-Metals:

Electronegativity: Tend to gain or share electrons.

Acidic Oxides: Non-metals form acidic oxides, e.g., CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃.

Reaction with Hydrogen: Forms covalent compounds (HCl, CH₄).

Reaction with Metals: Form ionic compounds (e.g., NaCl).
Reactivity Trend: Increases across a period (left to right on the Periodic Table).

Metalloids

Metalloids are elements with properties of both metals and non-metals.

Examples: Boron, Silicon, Arsenic, Antimony, Tellurium.

They are often semiconductors (used in electronics).
