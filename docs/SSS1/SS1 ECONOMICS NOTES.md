WEEK 1: MEANING OF ECONOMICS AND RELATED CONCEPTS MEANING OF ECONOMICS
The English word economics is derived from the ancient Greek word oikonomia—meaning the management of a family or a household. It is thus clear that the subject economics was first studied in ancient Greece.

What was the study of household management to Greek philosophers like Aristotle (384-322 BC) was the “study of wealth” to the mercantilists in Europe between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries. Economics, as a study of wealth, received great support from the Father of Economics, Adam Smith, in the late eighteenth century.

Since then, the subject has evolved significantly, and the Greek or Smithian definition no longer suffices. Over time, the focus of economics has shifted, leading to different definitions. These definitions can be grouped into three categories:

Smith’s Wealth Definition
Marshall’s Welfare Definition
Robbins’ Scarcity Definition

ADAM SMITH’S WEALTH DEFINITION

The formal definition of economics can be traced back to Adam Smith (1723-90), a great Scottish economist. Following the mercantilist tradition, Smith regarded economics as a science of wealth, studying the processes of production, consumption, and accumulation of wealth.

His emphasis on wealth as the subject matter of economics is evident in his book An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations (commonly known as Wealth of Nations), published in 1776.
According to Smith:
“The great object of the Political Economy of every country is to increase the riches and power of that country.”

Unlike the mercantilists, he did not believe wealth lay in the accumulation of precious metals like gold and silver. Instead, he defined wealth as goods and services that command value in exchange.

Key Points:

Economics is concerned with the generation and distribution of wealth.
The market economy operates through the "invisible hand" mechanism (price system).
Smith viewed economics as the science of wealth.

MARSHALL’S WELFARE DEFINITION

Alfred Marshall, in his book Principles of Economics (1890), shifted focus from wealth to
human welfare. He defined economics as:
“A study of men as they live and move and think in the ordinary business of life.”

Marshall emphasized that economics is:

A study of wealth on one side.
A study of human welfare on the other (more important) side.

Key Points:

Wealth is a means to an end (human welfare), not an end in itself.
Economics examines individual and social actions related to material well-being.

ROBBINS’ SCARCITY DEFINITION
The most widely accepted definition was given by Lord Robbins in his 1932 book An Essay on the Nature and Significance of Economic Science. He argued that economics should focus on scarcity and choice:

“Economics is the science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses.”

Key Propositions:

Unlimited Wants – Human desires are endless; luxuries become necessities.
Scarce Resources – Means to satisfy wants are limited relative to demand.
Alternative Uses – Resources can be allocated in different ways, leading to opportunity cost.

IMPLICATIONS:

Economics studies scarcity-induced choice problems.
Applies to all societies (Robinson Crusoe, feudal Europe, capitalist America, and communist Russia).
Makes economics a positive science (fact-based rather than value-based).

NATURE OF ECONOMICS

Economics is a social science that studies human behaviour in decision-making.
It is not just about money but about choices (e.g., career, spending, policy).
Every decision involves trade-offs (costs and benefits).

SCOPE OF ECONOMICS

Economics covers a broad range of topics, including:

Microeconomics – Studies individual and firm behaviour (demand, supply, production).
Macroeconomics – Examines economy-wide issues (GDP, inflation, unemployment).
International Economics – Focuses on trade, exchange rates, and global institutions.
Development Economics – Analyzes economic growth and poverty reduction.
Environmental Economics – Studies sustainable resource use and policy impacts.

IMPORTANCE OF ECONOMICS

Helps societies allocate scarce resources efficiently.
Guides businesses, policymakers, and consumers in decision-making.
Supports economic growth, inequality reduction, and sustainable development.
Essential for international trade and finance.
Useful for personal financial management.

METHODOLOGY OF ECONOMICS

Uses theoretical models (assumptions, logic) and empirical methods (data analysis).
Incorporates insights from psychology, sociology, and political science.

WEEK 3: CONCEPTS OF DEMAND AND SUPPLY

Demand

Meaning of demand: Demand is an economic concept that relates to a consumer's desire to purchase goods and services and willingness to pay a specific price for them. An increase in the price of a good or service tends to decrease the quantity demanded. It is also referred to as the quantity of a commodity that a consumer is willing to buy at a given price and at a given period of time.

Price system: In economics, a price system is a system through which the valuations of any forms of property (tangible or intangible) are determined. All societies use price systems in the allocation and exchange of resources as a consequence of scarcity. Even in a barter system with no money, price systems are still utilized in the determination of exchange ratios (relative valuations) between the properties being exchanged. A price system may be either a regulated price system (such as a fixed price system) where prices are administered by an authority, or it may be a free price system (such as a market system) where prices are left to float "freely" as determined by supply and demand without the intervention of an authority. A mixed price system involves a combination of both regulated and free price systems.

First law of demand: The law of demand states that, all things being equal, when the price of a product goes up, the quantity demanded will go down – and when the price of a product goes down, the quantity demanded will go up. The law of demand explains consumer choice behavior when the price changes. In the market, assuming other factors affecting demand remain constant, when the price of a good rises, it leads to a fall in the demand for that good. This is the natural consumer choice behavior. This happens because a consumer hesitates to spend more on the good for fear of going out of cash.

Demand schedule: In economics, a demand schedule is a table that shows the quantity demanded of a good or service at different price levels. It is divided into two, namely: individual and market demand schedule.

Factors influencing demand

Numerous factors can influence demand, including:

Consumer preferences and tastes: Changes in consumer preferences and tastes can lead to fluctuations in demand for specific products or services.
Income levels: As consumers' incomes increase, their ability and willingness to purchase goods and services generally rise, leading to higher demand.
Price of substitutes and complements: The demand for a good or service can be influenced by the prices of related goods, such as substitutes (products that can replace the good in question) and complements (products that are consumed together with the good).
Expectations: If consumers expect future changes in price, income, or product availability, they may adjust their current demand accordingly.
Population and demographics: An increase in population or shifts in demographic trends can influence the overall demand for goods and services.

Law of supply: The law of supply states that, all things being equal, an increase in the price of goods or services results in an increase in the quantity that suppliers make available to the market. Existing suppliers increase production of higher-priced goods to maximize profits, while new suppliers may also enter the market.

Factors influencing supply:
Price of the good:
As stated in accordance with the law of supply, the supply of a certain good will increase proportionately to an increase in price.
Cost of production:
The supply of a product and the cost of production are adversely related to each other. For companies, if the cost of production increases, the supply of products would shrink so as to save resources. For example, due to the high wage rate of labor, poor natural conditions such as crop failure, as well as the increase in raw material prices, taxes, and transportation costs, the cost of production is raised.
Government taxation policies:
With the role to regulate and protect the industry, the government has a great influence on the supply of a product. The lower the tax, the higher the supply of that product. On the other hand, if strict regulations are imposed and excise duty is added, the product’s supply would fall.
Transportation condition:
The supply chain relies on the efficient management of assets and logistics to get raw materials, parts, and finished products from one place to another. Transport is always a constraint to the supply of products, as the products may not be available on time due to poor transport facilities.
Natural conditions:
It implies that weather conditions have a direct impact on the availability of particular goods. When the monsoon arrives on schedule, for example, the quantity of agricultural goods increases. During droughts, however, the availability of these goods declines. Some crops are climate-sensitive, and their growth is solely dependent on weather conditions.

WEEK 4: THEORY OF PRODUCTION

Meaning of Production:
Production is the process by which different inputs, including capital, labor, and land, are used to create outputs in the form of products or services to satisfy human wants. Production can also be defined as the various economic activities aimed at the creation of goods and services and the distribution of these to the final consumers for the satisfaction of human wants.

Types of Production
Production is basically grouped into two major categories: direct and indirect production.

Direct Production:
Direct production is the type of production in which an individual produces goods and services only for family use or consumption. The goods and services produced are not for sale; rather, they are to be consumed by the family. Family labour is usually employed as the size is very small.

Indirect Production:
Indirect production is the type of production in which goods and services are produced in large quantities/scale mainly for sale or exchange for other needs. This type of production involves the use of modern machines/equipment and skilled labour to produce surplus goods and services that the producer would exchange for money, which he will use to satisfy other wants.

Indirect production is sub-divided into three major groups, namely: primary, secondary, and tertiary production.

Primary Production:
Primary production is carried out by ‘extractive’ industries like agriculture, forestry, fishing, mining, and oil extraction. These industries are engaged in such activities as extracting the gifts of nature from the earth’s surface, from beneath the earth’s surface, and from the oceans.

Secondary Production:
This includes production in the manufacturing industry, i.e., turning out semi-finished and
finished goods from raw materials and intermediate goods—conversion of flour into bread or iron ore into finished steel. They are generally described as manufacturing and construction industries, such as the manufacture of cars, furnishing, clothing, and chemicals, as well as engineering and building.

Tertiary Production:
Industries in the tertiary sector produce all those services which enable the finished goods to be put in the hands of consumers. In fact, these services are supplied to the firms in all types of industry and directly to consumers. Examples include distributive trade, banking, insurance, transport, and communications. Government services, such as law, administration, education, health, and defence, are also included.

Land:
Land refers to natural resources such as land, energy, etc., that are used in the process of production and are counted under the category ‘land’. Unlike other factors of production, the supply of land is limited. The reward for land is rent.

Features or Characteristics of Capital
Capital is durable, capital is man-made, capital exists in different forms, capital is subject to depreciation, capital ensures large scale production etc.

Entrepreneur: An entrepreneur can be defined as the factor of production that co-ordinates and organises other factors of production (Land, Labour and Capital) in order to produce goods and services. The entrepreneur bears the risks by investing his capital in setting up the business and takes decisions of the business with the aim of obtaining maximum profit. The reward for entrepreneur is profit.

Features or Characteristics of Entrepreneur:
An entrepreneur is a decision maker, he is a risk bearer, he controls other factors of production etc.

Division of labour and specialization
Meaning: According to Adam Smith, division of labour involves assessing the production process and allocating tasks to individuals to enhance productivity. He gives the example of a pin that goes through the production process and notes that division of labour leads to productivity.

What does division of labour mean in economics?
Division of labour is the assigning of different tasks to different people, which increases productivity and ultimately profits. This leads to economic and monetary gains.

What is an example of division of labour?
A construction manager needs to construct a new building. The building needs to have electricity, running water, a solid foundation, and an attractive, functional interior, among other things. He realizes that in order to accomplish this, he needs masons, plumbers, carpenters, electricians, painters, and interior designers. How important is division of labour?
Division of labour is important, as it increases productivity in organizations; ensures that market demand is met with high-quality products made by skilled professionals; allows for job growth as individuals become experts in their respective areas; and leads to monetary gain and economic growth.

Specialization: Specialization is the process wherein a company or individual decides to focus their labour on a specific type of production. In layman’s terms, specialization means focusing on a specific job. When an individual specializes, they limit their focus to one specific activity. When a company specializes, it focuses on a narrow range of goods or services.

Types of Specialization:
Specialization by process: This is the type of specialization in which the production process is divided into different operations or stages, and each worker or firm or country concentrates on only one operation or stage.
Specialization by product: This is the type of specialization in which a producer (individual or
a firm or government) concentrates on the production of a particular commodity.
Specialization by sex: This is the type of specialization in which certain occupations are exclusively either for males or females as dictated by customs, traditions or by law.
Geographical or territorial specialization: This is the type of specialization in which certain geographical regions or territories specialise in the production of a particular commodity. This is made possible as a result of the type of climate and natural resources available in the area.

Economies of Scale or Scale of Production What Are Economies of Scale?
When more units of a good or service can be produced on a larger scale, yet with (on average) fewer input costs, economies of scale are said to be achieved.

Alternatively, this means that as a company grows and production units increase, a company will have a better chance to decrease its costs. According to this theory, economic growth may be achieved when economies of scale are realized.

Understanding Economies of Scale

Economist Adam Smith identified the division of labour and specialization as the two key means to achieving a larger return on production. Through these two techniques, employees would not only be able to concentrate on a specific task but, with time, improve the skills necessary to perform their jobs. The tasks could then be performed better and faster. Hence, through such efficiency, time and money could be saved while production levels increased.

Just like there are economies of scale, diseconomies of scale also exist. This occurs when production is less than in proportion to inputs. What this means is that there are inefficiencies within the firm or industry, resulting in rising average costs.

Types of Economies of Scale

There are two major types of economies of scale, namely:

Internal economies (economies of large scale) and internal diseconomies.
External economies (economies of small scale) and external diseconomies.

External Economies of Scale

Economist Alfred Marshall made a distinction between internal and external economies of scale.
When a company reduces costs and increases production, internal economies of scale
have been achieved.
External economies of scale occur outside of a firm, within an industry.

Thus, when an industry's scope of operations expands due to outside developments, external economies of scale might result. For example, the creation of a better transportation network might result in a subsequent decrease in cost for a company as well as its entire industry. When external economies of scale occur, all firms within the industry benefit.

External economies of scale are achieved from the concentration or localisation of industries in a particular area. In other words, these are the benefits a firm enjoys from an increase in its output and a decrease in cost as a result of the assistance it derives from other firms within the same location. External economies are mostly derived from industrial estates where there are many firms operating in the same location.

Diseconomies of Scale

As mentioned before, diseconomies may also occur. They could stem from inefficient managerial or labour policies, over-hiring, or deteriorating transportation networks (external diseconomies of scale).

External diseconomies refer to the disadvantages a firm experiences when the activities of one or more industries increase the cost of production or output of that firm within the same location.
Internal diseconomies of scale involve either technical constraints on the production process that the firm uses, or organizational issues and problems that increase costs or waste resources without any change to the physical production process.

Internal diseconomies are the disadvantages which a firm undergoes as a result of expansion, resulting in less efficiency and an increase in the cost per unit of output, often due to managerial problems.
Advantages of Large-Scale Production

The following are the merits of large-scale production:

Internal Economies: Internal economies arise within the firm because of the expansion of the size of a particular firm.
External Economies: External economies arise with the expansion of the industry. These are generally the result of large-scale production and are associated with the advantages of localisation.
Division of Labour: Large-scale production is always associated with more and more division of labour. With the division of labour, per worker output increases. Hence, per unit labour cost is reduced in large-scale production.
Low Cost of Production: Large-scale production provides many types of economies. Suppose there are two different factories, each producing 500 units of a commodity. For these two factories, there must be two managers. But if the scale of production is enlarged and in one factory we start producing 1000 units of the same commodity, the work can be supervised by one manager. In this way, in large-scale production, the salary of one manager is saved. So, the cost of production is reduced.
Ancillary Industries: With the development of large-scale production, many small industries arise that use its by-products or supply inputs to it. For example, when the production of steel is increased, many other auxiliary industries develop. The development of auxiliary industries contributes to the industrialisation of the area and the industry itself.

Disadvantages of Large Scale Production
The following are the demerits of large scale production:

Danger of Over-Production: The large scale organisation results in over-production at times, so demand cannot be properly estimated. At last, prices fall and depression sets in.
Less Supervision: A large scale producer cannot pay full attention to every detail in various departments. Costs often rise on account of the dishonesty of workers.
Unequal Distribution of Wealth: All wealth and incomes of the country get concentrated in the pockets of big producers due to large scale production. There is unequal distribution of wealth and resources on account of the large scale production. The rich become richer and the poor become poorer.
Dependence on Foreign Markets: A large producer has generally to depend on foreign markets. The foreign markets may be cut off by wars, etc. This makes the business risky.
Individual Tastes Ignored: The individual tastes and interests stand completely ignored in large scale production. Goods of uniform quality are turned out irrespective of the requirements of the individual customers. Individual tastes are not, therefore, satisfied. This results in the loss of customers to other competitors.

Definition of Economic System
An economic system is a means by which societies or governments organize and distribute available resources, services, and goods across a geographic region or country. Economic systems regulate the factors of production, including land, capital, labor, and physical resources. It is an arrangement specifying how production and consumption are controlled in a society.
An economic system, or economic order, is a system of production, resource allocation, and distribution of goods and services within a society. An economic system is a way a society produces and distributes goods and services. It involves how things are made, who gets to make them, how they are distributed, and how people get access to them. It’s like a set of rules that everyone in society follows when it comes to money and trade.

Types of Economic System
There are basically three types of economic system namely:

Capitalism or Free Market Economy

Capitalism (Free Market Economy)
Capitalism or free market economy is an economic system wherein individuals own and manage property in accordance with their desires, and free market forces establish prices in a way that fits society’s best interests. It is characterised by private ownership of means of production. In this case, private individuals play a greater role than government in taking decisions on what to produce, how to produce, for whom to produce, and the distribution of what has been produced. Examples of countries which practice capitalism include the United States of America, Japan, Australia, Singapore, Switzerland, and France.

Profit maximization at all cost
It increases crime rate because of efforts to acquire wealth at all cost
It leads to economic inequality as only a few individuals have full control of the means of production

Socialism is an economic system or condition of society in which the means of production are owned and controlled by the state (the government). Examples of countries which operate mixed economy are Britain, Mexico, and Peru. In Africa: Nigeria, Egypt, Tanzania, Kenya, etc.

Economic Problems
Definition of Economic Problems
The basic economic problems refer to the problems people encounter in society while attempting to satisfy their numerous wants with the limited resources available to them. These basic economic problems of society include what to produce, how to produce, for whom to produce, and how efficiently the resources are being utilised.

What to Produce?
Since resources are limited and human wants are unlimited, societies must decide what goods and services to produce and in what quantities, as it is practically impossible to produce all human wants or needs. This involves making choices about which products or services are most needed or desired by the population.

Factors Determining What to Produce
Cost of production: If cost of production is low, it would enable the producer to produce goods and services to make profit
Consumers’ income must be taken into consideration before the producer decides on what to produce
Market demand for a particular set of goods and services must also be considered before production
Availability of resources: when resources for production are available and affordable, the producer will be encouraged to produce goods and services, and vice versa
Consumer needs must also be taken into consideration before deciding on what to produce

How to Produce?
As soon as what to produce is established, the problem of how to produce is related to the production techniques employed in producing the decided goods and services.

Factors Determining How to Produce
Technique of production
Technological advancement
Relative cost of factors of production
For Whom to Produce?
All goods and services produced must get to the final consumers. Production is incomplete until the goods and services reach the final consumers.

Factors Determining For Whom to Produce

Level of income: The higher the level of income of the consumers, the more they are able to buy goods and services produced, and vice versa
Satisfaction of wants: All goods and services produced either by individuals, firms, or government must satisfy the needs or wants of society
Type of economic system: In capitalist and mixed economies, who gets what depends on the prices of the various products and the amount available to each individual. In a socialist economy, the state normally introduces a quota system in the distribution of goods and services among the people

How Efficiently are Resources Being Utilised?
Generally, if the output achieved with a given set of resources is high relative to the input of resources used, then resource utilization can be considered efficient. Since the resources available in society are limited relative to demand, it becomes necessary to ensure that these limited resources are efficiently used to produce the desired goods and services.

Factors Determining Efficient Use of Resources
Quality of labour: The use of skilled labour contributes to efficient and effective use and allocation of resources in production, leading to increased quantity and quality of output
Techniques of production: The use of machines and equipment may produce more goods, save time, and reduce wastage

WEEK 8: FIRM, FACTORY, INDUSTRY AND BUSINESS ORGANIZATION

Firm
A firm is a business organisation such as a corporation that produces and sells goods and services with the aim of generating revenue and making a profit. In economics, firms are organizations that produce goods and services. They are typically owned and operated by individuals or groups of individuals, and are motivated by the desire to make a profit.

Firm vs. Company
Although they appear synonymous and are often used interchangeably, there is a difference between a firm and a company. A company can be any trade or business in which goods or services are sold to produce income. Further, it encompasses all business structures, such as a sole proprietorship, partnership, and corporation. On the other hand, a firm typically excludes the sole proprietorship business; it generally refers to a for-profit business managed by two or more partners providing professional services, such as a law firm. In some cases, a firm can be a corporation.

Plant or Factory
A plant or factory is a place where a firm carries out its productive activities. In the study of economics, a plant is an integrated workplace, usually all in one location. A plant generally consists of the physical capital, like the building and the equipment at a particular location that is used for the production of goods. A plant is also called a factory. Examples are paper mill, steel mill, automobile plant, chemical plant, textile plant, cement plant, etc.

Industry
In macroeconomics, an industry is a branch of an economy that produces a closely related set of raw materials, goods, or services. For example, one might refer to the wood industry, the insurance industry, or the banking industry, etc. An industry is the combination of a number of firms producing broadly similar commodities or services for the same market.

Business Organisations (Meaning)
Business organisation can be defined as an enterprise set up by an individual or group of individuals, government or its agencies for the main purpose of making profit and providing
goods and services for the satisfaction of human wants. All business organisations, be it small or large, irrespective of the ownership and structure, have one thing in common – the provision of goods and services to meet the numerous needs of the people.

Types of Business Organisations
There are two major groups of business organisations: private and public enterprises.

(a) Private Enterprises
Private enterprises are the enterprises owned and managed by private individuals. This type of business organisation is usually classified as private sector enterprises. Examples of such private enterprises include sole proprietorship and partnership. The main objective of private enterprises is to maximize profits.

Characteristics of Private Enterprise
Private individuals provide the capital: Private individuals that own the enterprises are responsible for raising capital for the establishment of the enterprise.
Ownership belongs to private individuals: Private individuals, e.g. sole proprietorship or partnership, are the ones that own the business enterprises.
Objective of business is to make profits: The purpose of setting up the business enterprise is to make profits for the owners.
Accountability is to the owners: The transactions of the business enterprises are usually accounted for and submitted to the owners.
Owners bear the risk of the business: In the event of business failure or liquidation, the owners will have to bear the entire risks associated with such failures.

Public Enterprises
Public enterprises are the types of business organisations which are owned, controlled, and managed by the government. These business organisations are owned by either the federal, state, or local government, and their major objective is to provide social services to the people. They are associated with such names as authority, corporation, boards, and commissions. Examples of public enterprises include public corporations and companies owned by government such as the National Electric Power Authority (N.E.P.A), Nigerian Ports Authority (N.P.A), Nigerian

Ownership belongs to the government
Objective is to provide social services

Board of directors manage the business

Private enterprises are owned by individuals, while public enterprises are owned by government.

WEEK 1: THE NATURE AND STRUCTURE OF NIGERIAN ECONOMY

General Overview of Nigerian Economy
Nigeria has a mixed economy structured around capitalism. In a mixed economy, private businesses may receive government intervention, and some sectors may be regulated or entirely controlled by the government. The name of the game is still fundamentally capitalist in nature: private businesses seek revenue in order to generate personal wealth.

Nigeria is home to over 200 million people, making it one of the most populous countries in the world. This allows it to have a proportionately large labour force, which helps sustain numerous different economic sectors.

Nigeria's economy increasingly revolves around importing and exporting. Today, one of Nigeria's largest exports is petroleum. Nigeria is the largest exporter of petroleum in all of Africa. Most of Nigeria's petroleum is purchased by India and the United States. Around 90% to 95% of Nigeria's export profits come from petroleum.

Nature and Structure of Industry in Nigeria
Industries in Nigeria can be grouped into three, namely: primary, secondary, and tertiary industries.

Primary Industry: Deals with the extraction of natural resources. Forestry, agriculture, fishing, quarrying, mining, and mineral exploitation are all part of a country's economy. It is thus named because it serves as the foundation for all other items.
Secondary Industry: Defined as the industry that deals with the raw materials effectively collected by the primary industry, converting them into finished products. These finished goods are in turn sold by the tertiary industries in the consumer market. In an economy, the industrial sector is dominated by the production and manufacturing of finished products. The secondary industry sector includes steel production, automobile manufacturing, and telecommunications, among others. This is the key sector that has the potential to transform world economies.
Tertiary Industry: The services sector of an economy, encompassing medical providers, educators, financial services, haircuts, and personal trainers, among many others. The service sector is now the largest sector of the global economy in terms of value-added and is especially important in more advanced economies.

Additionally, Nigeria exports numerous agricultural products, including rubber and bananas. Palm oil, which has played an ever-important role in Nigeria's history, remains a significant export as well.

Economic Activities of the Six Geo-Political Zones

Nigeria has six geopolitical zones, namely: the North-West, North-East, North-Central, South-West, South-East, and South-South.

The North-West Geo-Political Zone
The states in this zone include Sokoto, Zamfara, Kebbi, Katsina, Jigawa, Kano, and Kaduna. The economic activities of this zone are mainly farming, fishing, and livestock rearing, especially cattle, sheep, and goats. They are also involved in industrial activities like mining (e.g., limestone in Sokoto).

The North-East Geo-Political Zone
This zone is located in the northern part of the country and consists of Yobe, Borno, Bauchi, Gombe, Adamawa, and Taraba. The major economic activities include farming, fishing, and livestock production, especially cattle, sheep, and goats. They are also involved in minor mining activities. This zone is the least endowed with mineral resources.

The North-Central Geo-Political Zone
This zone represents the Middle Belt of Nigeria and comprises Benue, Kogi, Nasarawa, Niger, and Plateau states. The Federal Capital Territory (FCT), Abuja, is also located in this zone.
The main activities include farming, fishing, hunting, trading, weaving, blacksmithing, dyeing, typing, and mat-making. Farming and fishing dominate due to the fertile nature of the soil and the presence of the Niger and Benue Rivers. Mining is also significant, with tin and columbite in Jos, gold and iron ore in Kogi, and limestone in Benue. Hydroelectric power generation is also notable in this zone, e.g., Kainji Dam.

The South-West Geo-Political Zone
This zone is made up of the six Yoruba-speaking states: Lagos, Ogun, Oyo, Ondo, Ekiti, and Osun. It is endowed with both agricultural and commercial activities.

The region engages in farming, particularly tree crops like cocoa, rubber, kola, and oil palm, as well as livestock activities such as poultry and piggery. Commercial activities are also important, with limestone in Ogun as a key resource.

The South-East Geo-Political Zone
This zone is made up of the five Igbo-speaking states: Anambra, Imo, Enugu, Abia, and Ebonyi.

The main economic activities are farming, with crops such as palm oil, rubber, kola, yam, cassava, and rice. The zone is also involved in mining activities (coal, petroleum, limestone). Additionally, the South-East is noted for heavy trading and local manufacturing, particularly in Abia and Anambra.

The South-South Geo-Political Zone
This zone comprises the six oil-producing states of the Niger Delta: Edo, Delta, Rivers, Bayelsa, Cross River, and Akwa Ibom.

The major economic activity is crude oil (petroleum) production, along with limestone in Edo and iron ore in Delta. Crop farming is also significant, with cocoa, oil palm, kola, and rubber.
Fishing thrives due to the Niger Delta location. Other important activities include trading, banking, and seaport operations.

ECONOMIC POTENTIALS OF THE NIGERIAN ECONOMY

The Nigerian economy is one of the largest in Africa. Since the late 1960s, it has been based primarily on the petroleum industry. A series of world oil price increases from 1973 produced rapid economic growth in transportation, construction, manufacturing, and government services.

Roughly between one-fifth to one-half of all Nigerians obtain a living from agricultural production. Most are small-scale subsistence farmers who produce only a little surplus for sale and who derive additional income from one or more cash crops and from the sale of local crafts.

Because petroleum dominated the economy, a great influx of rural people moved into larger urban centres. Agricultural production stagnated to such an extent that cash crops such as palm oil, peanuts (groundnuts), and cotton were no longer significant export commodities. By about 1975, Nigeria was forced to import basic commodities such as rice and cassava for domestic consumption.

This system worked well as long as revenues from petroleum remained constant. However, since the late 1970s, the agricultural sector has faced continuing crisis due to fluctuating world oil prices and the country’s rapid population growth. Although much of the population remained engaged in farming, too little food was produced, making costly imports necessary. Various governments (mostly military) responded by banning agricultural imports and briefly focusing on agricultural and indigenization plans.

Because the soil is not entirely suited to mechanized farming, the hoe and machete continue to be the dominant farm implements. The shortage of farmland in some areas and limited access to land in others restrict the size of farms cultivated per family. Environmental degradation, poor storage facilities, weak transport systems, and a lack of investment capital also contribute to low productivity and agricultural stagnation. With rapid population growth and accelerating urbanization, the food deficit continues to worsen despite government interventions.
Root crops—particularly yam and cassava—are the main food crops in the south, while grains and legumes—such as sorghum, millet, cowpeas, and maize (corn)—are staple crops in the north. Rice is also an important domestic crop.

Tree crops—notably oil palm, cocoa, and rubber—are the principal industrial crops of the south, while peanuts (groundnuts) and cotton are the main industrial crops of the north. Small-scale farmers dominate the production of both food and industrial crops. Cocoa beans, from the cacao tree, remain Nigeria’s major agricultural export, though production of other industrial crops has declined due to the overall stagnation in agriculture.

In 1982, in the first major step taken to halt the decline in industrial crop production, the government disbanded the produce marketing boards, which paid prices set by the government. Many farmers have since been motivated to cultivate tree crops, and the federal and state governments have established plantations of oil palm, rubber, and cocoa. Programs to alleviate the food shortage have featured the direct purchase and distribution of foodstuffs by government agencies and the production by government parastatals of various staples on large commercial farms. The Operation Feed the Nation program of 1976–80 sought to increase local food production and thereby reduce imports. Citizens were encouraged to cultivate any empty plot of land, with urban dwellers being encouraged to garden undeveloped building plots.

The raising of sheep, pigs, and goats was underdeveloped at the beginning of the 21st century. The cattle-herding Fulani are still the main beef producers, although some of the cattle under the care of these nomads belong to settled farmers and city dwellers. However, the level of meat consumption in Nigeria, as in most African countries, does not approach that of the West.

Fishing has assumed greater importance as a food source following the loss of thousands of head of livestock during the recurring drought in the Sahel since the early 1970s. The domestic catch supplies more than half of the fish demand. Lake Chad and the southern coastal waters are the main sources of fish, but large quantities are caught every year in pools in seasonal rivers of the northern states.
Resource extraction is the most important sector of the economy. The most economically valuable minerals are crude oil, natural gas, coal, tin, and columbite (an iron-bearing mineral that accompanies tin). Petroleum, first discovered in 1956, is the most important source of government revenue and foreign exchange.

Nigeria possesses significant reserves of coal, but these deposits are being developed gradually. Coal is used by the railroad, by traditional metal industries, and by power plants to generate electricity. Coal mining, initially concentrated around the city of Enugu and its environs, began in 1915.

WEEK 6: MONEY

Money
Money is any object that is generally accepted as payment for goods and services and repayment of debts in a given country or socio-economic context. Money is any item or medium of exchange that symbolizes perceived value. As a result, it is accepted by people for the payment of goods and services, as well as the repayment of loans. Money is a medium of exchange that functions as legal tender, the official currency, in the form of banknotes, coins, etc., issued by a government or other authority.

Trade by barter
Barter is the direct exchange of goods or services—without an intervening medium of exchange or money—either according to established rates of exchange or by bargaining. It is considered the oldest form of commerce.

Barter is an act of trading goods or services between two or more parties without the use of money—or a monetary medium, such as a credit card. In essence, bartering involves the provision of one good or service by one party in return for another good or service from another party.

Examples of barter trade include exchanging a science textbook for a history book, or a plumber exchanging plumbing services for the dental services of a dentist, etc.

Benefits or advantages of trade by barter:
The advantages of barter system are:
Bartering presents a practical solution for srmounting financial constraints. In circumstances of limited cash flow or economic downturns, bartering empowers individuals and businesses to acquire necessary goods and services without relying exclusively on monetary means.
Natural resources will not be over exploited.
Bartering promotes optimal resource utilization. It facilitates the utilization of excess or underutilized goods and services, preventing resource wastage.
Power will not be concentrated in some circles.
There won't be problems of balance of payments crisis, foreign exchange crisis.
Bartering fosters environmental sustainability by reducing waste and consumption. It provides an alternative to the prevalent "buy-and-discard" culture inherent in modern consumerism.

Problems or disadvantages of trade by barter:

There is no common measure of value. Lack of Common Measure of Value: One of the disadvantages of the barter system is having no common unit to measure the worth of goods and services. Hence, the problem arises with the proportion of goods to be exchanged.
No standard unit of account.
Problem of double coincidence of wants. This involves looking for someone who is in need of what you have and at the same time has what you need.
Difficulty in storage of goods. The barter system, unlike money, does not encourage storage of wealth. It is difficult to store wealth or value, especially where perishable goods like yam and fresh tomatoes are involved.
Inability to make deferred payments.

Brief Origin/Historical Development of Money
Money originated as a result of the problems of trade by barter. In the olden days, different commodities served as money in different countries of the world, such commodities as cattle, cowries, shells, tobacco, salt, and beads.

Later, precious metals like silver and gold were used. The use of paper money originated from the use of ‘receipts’ issued by Goldsmiths in London in exchange for deposits of precious metals. The receipts became banknotes, and the Goldsmiths became the bankers.

In recent times, people started accepting inconvertible paper money as a medium of exchange. For anything to serve as money, it must enjoy people’s confidence.
Functions of Money
Medium of exchange: Money's most important function is as a medium of exchange to facilitate transactions. Without money, all transactions would have to be conducted by barter, which involves direct exchange of one good or service for another. The difficulty with a barter system is that in order to obtain a particular good or service from a supplier, one has to possess a good or service of equal value, which the supplier also desires. In other words, in a barter system, exchange can take place only if there is a double coincidence of wants between two transacting parties. The likelihood of a double coincidence of wants, however, is small and makes the exchange of goods and services rather difficult.

Store of value: In order to be a medium of exchange, money must hold its value over time; that is, it must be a store of value. If money could not be stored for some period of time and still remain valuable in exchange, it would not solve the double coincidence of wants problem and therefore would not be adopted as a medium of exchange.

Unit of account: Money also functions as a unit of account, providing a common measure of the value of goods and services being exchanged. Knowing the value or price of a good, in terms of money, enables both the supplier and the purchaser of the good to make decisions about how much of the good to supply and how much of the good to purchase.

As a measure of value: The value of goods and services is expressed by prices; therefore, money is used as a yardstick to measure and compare the worth of goods and services as well as occupations.

Standard for deferred payment: Since money can be stored, it can be accumulated to pay debts that are fixed in terms of money. Money can serve as a medium by which business transactions on credit can be settled in the future. The use of money makes it possible for payments to be deferred from the present to some future date.

Characteristics or Qualities of Money
General acceptability: An important quality of money is its acceptance. Good money requires
acceptance by all without any hesitation. Since the law declares money as the legal tender, it has an inherent quality of general acceptability.

Portability: Apart from its acceptance, good money also requires portability. If people can carry or transfer money from one place to another, then it is good money.

Durability: Acceptance and portability aside, the material used to make money must last for a long time without losing its value. For example, ice and fruits are not good money since they lose their value quickly with the passage of time. After all, ice melts and fruits perish. Therefore, durability is an essential quality of good money.

Divisibility: Talking about the qualities of good money, it is important to remember the divisibility of money. If someone wants to buy a smaller unit of a commodity, then divisibility of money can make it possible. For example, cows cannot function as good money. This is because you cannot divide a cow without making it lose its value.

Homogeneity: Look at two ₦100 naira notes. They look and feel identical, right? They also have the same value, size, colour, and quality. This is an important quality of good money – homogeneity. If money is not homogeneous, then transactions will become uncertain as people would be unsure of what they are receiving.

Cognizability: The ability to recognize money is critically important. Today, we can look at a currency note and tell its value. If money is not cognizable, then people can find it difficult to determine if they are dealing with money or some inferior asset.

Stability: Of all the qualities of good money, stability is probably the most essential one. The value of money cannot change for a long period of time and must remain stable. If the value of money keeps changing, then it will fail to function as a measure of value and as a standard of deferred payment.

Relative scarcity: Money must be relatively scarce; that is, it must not be too plentiful so as not to lose its value.
Types or Forms of Money

Bank notes: These are slips of paper or currency issued by the central bank. This type of money (paper) originated from the receipts issued by goldsmiths to those who deposited precious metals, e.g., gold, with them for safekeeping. Bank notes are in denominations and are portable, e.g., ₦5, ₦10, ₦20, ₦50, ₦100, ₦200, ₦500, and ₦1000.

Commodity money: Commodity money might be defined as a specific product that people widely use to exchange for several other commodities. Commodity money is tangible, which means individuals can see and hold it. However, users recognize it because of its inherent quality. For example, gold, diamond, cattle, and beads.

Representative money: Representative money is also known as near or quasi-money assets. Representative money is a certificate or token that can be exchanged for the underlying commodity. For example, instead of carrying the gold commodity money with you, the gold might have been kept in a bank vault, and you might carry a paper certificate that represents—or was "backed"—by the gold in the vault. It was understood that the certificate could be redeemed for gold at any time. Also, the certificate was easier and safer to carry than the actual gold. Over time, people grew to trust the paper certificates as much as the gold. Representative money led to the use of fiat money—the type used in modern economies today.

Fiat money: Fiat money is any money the government has declared to be legal tender but is not backed by reserves. Its value is derived from its official recognition as a medium of exchange from government decree. Unlike commodity and representative money, fiat money is not backed by other commodities such as silver or gold, but its creditworthiness comes from the government recognizing it as money.

Fiduciary money/note: Fiduciary money, or currency, refers to banknotes and coins in circulation in the economy. This is the liquidity available to economic actors to carry out transactions.

Deposit money: This is a form of money kept in the accounts of the bank. Any money saved in the bank will be credited to the account of the depositor. The money in the deposit can be
transferred through the use of a cheque. A cheque is just an order to pay somebody and should not be considered as money.

Token money: Token money refers to notes or coins for which the value written on them is greater than the value of the materials they are made from.

These funds can be acquired through various financial instruments, broadly categorized into equity financing, debt financing, hybrid instruments, and alternative financing methods. Each instrument has distinct features, advantages, and disadvantages, making it suitable for different business needs. This essay explores these instruments in detail, providing insights into their mechanisms and applications in real-world business scenarios.

Equity Financing

Equity financing involves raising capital by selling ownership stakes in a company. Investors who purchase equity become shareholders and are entitled to a portion of the company’s profits.

Shares (Stocks)
Shares represent units of ownership in a company and are issued in the stock market. There are two main types:

Ordinary Shares (Common Stock)
Ordinary shares grant shareholders voting rights, allowing them to participate in major company decisions, such as electing the board of directors. Dividends for ordinary shareholders are not fixed and are paid only if the company declares a profit. However, in the event of liquidation, ordinary shareholders are the last to be paid, making this a high-risk investment.

Advantages:
No repayment obligation, reducing financial pressure on the company.
Attracts long-term investors who believe in the company’s growth.

Disadvantages:
Dilution of ownership, as issuing more shares reduces the control of existing shareholders.
Dividend payments are not tax-deductible, unlike interest on loans.
Preference Shares (Preferred Stock)
Preference shares provide shareholders with a fixed dividend, paid before ordinary shareholders. However, they usually do not carry voting rights. In case of liquidation, preference shareholders are paid before ordinary shareholders but after debt holders.

Advantages:
Stable income for investors through fixed dividends.
Less risky than ordinary shares due to priority in dividend payments.

Disadvantages:
Higher cost for the company compared to debt, as dividends must be paid even in low-profit periods.
Limited capital appreciation, as preference shares do not benefit significantly from company growth.

Retained Earnings
Retained earnings refer to profits that a company reinvests into its operations rather than distributing them as dividends. This internal source of financing is crucial for business growth without incurring debt or diluting ownership.

Advantages:
No interest or repayment obligations.
Maintains full ownership and control.

Disadvantages:
Limited by the company’s profitability.
Shareholders may prefer dividends over reinvestment, leading to dissatisfaction.

Debt Financing

Debt financing involves borrowing funds that must be repaid with interest over a specified period. Unlike equity, lenders do not gain ownership in the company.
Loans
Loans can be short-term or long-term, depending on the business’s needs.

Short-term Loans (Overdrafts, Trade Credit)

Overdrafts: Allow businesses to withdraw more money than they have in their accounts, up to a pre-approved limit. Interest is charged only on the amount used.
Trade Credit: Suppliers permit businesses to purchase goods and pay for them at a later date, usually within 30 to 90 days.

Advantages:
Provides flexibility in managing cash flow.
No collateral is required for trade credit.

Disadvantages:
High interest rates for overdrafts.
Late payments can damage supplier relationships.

Long-term Loans (Term Loans, Mortgages)

Term Loans: Repaid over several years with fixed or variable interest rates, often used for business expansion.
Mortgages: Secured loans where property is used as collateral.

Advantages:
Fixed repayment schedules aid financial planning.
Interest payments are tax-deductible.

Disadvantages:
Strict repayment terms; defaulting can lead to asset seizure.
Requires a strong credit history for approval.
Bonds (Debentures)
Bonds are long-term debt securities where investors lend money to a company in exchange for periodic interest payments and the return of the principal at maturity.

Advantages:
Lower interest rates compared to bank loans.
Can be traded in financial markets, providing liquidity.

Disadvantages:
Legal obligation to repay, even in financial distress.
Requires a good credit rating to attract investors.

Hybrid Instruments

Hybrid instruments combine features of both equity and debt financing.

Convertible Debentures
These are bonds that can be converted into company shares after a specified period.

Advantages:
Lower interest rates due to the conversion option.
Flexibility for businesses to avoid immediate equity dilution.

Disadvantages:

Future dilution of ownership if converted.

Alternative Financing Methods

Leasing
Leasing allows businesses to use assets without purchasing them outright. Payments are made periodically, and the lessor retains ownership.
Advantages:
No large upfront capital required.
Access to modern equipment without long-term commitment.

Disadvantages:
Total lease payments may exceed the asset’s purchase price.
No ownership benefits, such as asset appreciation.

Venture Capital & Angel Investors

Venture Capital: Funds provided by firms to startups with high growth potential in exchange for equity.
Angel Investors: Wealthy individuals who invest personal funds in early-stage businesses.

Advantages:
Provides mentorship and industry connections.
No repayment required if the business fails.

Disadvantages:
Loss of control as investors may influence business decisions.
High expectations for returns on investment.

Crowdfunding
Crowdfunding involves raising small amounts of money from a large number of people, typically via online platforms.

Advantages:
Access to capital without traditional lenders.
Market validation through public interest.

Disadvantages:
High competition for investor attention.
Potential loss of intellectual property if ideas are publicly shared.
Conclusion

The choice of financing instrument depends on factors such as cost, control, repayment terms, and business stage. Equity financing avoids debt but dilutes ownership, while debt financing ensures control but increases repayment risks. Hybrid and alternative methods offer flexible solutions for businesses with unique needs. Understanding these instruments enables entrepreneurs to make informed financial decisions that align with their business goals.

WEEK 10: PRODUCTION POSSIBILITY CURVE (PPC)

The production possibility curve (PPC), also known as the Production Possibility Boundary (PPB), refers to a graph or a curve showing the possible combinations of different commodities that can be produced in a given economy, taking into consideration the prevailing level of technology, if all the available productive resources are efficiently utilised.

In other words, PPC is a graphical representation of all possible combinations of two or more types of commodities which a society can produce with a given quantity of resources.

Note that the idea behind the production possibility curve is that, in order to produce a particular commodity, the production of another commodity has to be sacrificed. PPC has a downward slope from left to right, indicating that there is an opportunity cost of producing more of one type of commodity. The cost is, however, measured in terms of quantity forgone of the other types of commodities.

Relationship between PPC and Opportunity Cost

Concept of Total, Average, and Marginal Productivity
Total Productivity (TP):
Total Productivity (TP), also known as total output or total product, refers to the total quantity of a commodity produced with all the units of factors of production employed.
Mathematically:
TP = AP × Labour

Example:
If 60 men were employed in a farm and they produce an average of 30 tonnes of rice per person, calculate the total product.

Solution:
TP = AP × Labour TP = 30 tonnes × 60 TP = 1,800 tonnes

Average Productivity (AP):
Average Productivity (AP), also known as average output, is the output per unit of the variable factors of production like labour or capital employed.
Mathematically:
AP = Total Product ÷ No. of Labour (or Capital) Employed

Example:
If 4,000 tonnes of maize were harvested by 80 men in a farm on a daily basis, calculate the average output.

Solution:
AP = Total Output ÷ No. of Labour AP = 4,000 ÷ 80
AP = 50 tonnes/person
Marginal Productivity (MP):
Marginal Productivity (MP), also known as marginal output, refers to the additional output achieved by employing an additional unit of the variable factors of production. MP can be negative if the employment of an additional variable factor results in a decrease in total output.
Mathematically:
MP = Change in TP ÷ Change in Variable Factor

Example:
4,080 tonnes of cassava were harvested from the same farm as a result of adding one more man to the 80 men. Calculate the marginal product.

Solution:
MP = (4,080 – 4,000) ÷ (81 – 80)
MP = 80 ÷ 1
MP = 80

WEEK 1: COST CONCEPTS

Introduction

In economics, cost refers to the expenditure incurred in the production of goods and services. It is a fundamental concept that influences business decisions, pricing strategies, and resource allocation. Understanding cost structures helps firms optimize production, maximize profits, and maintain competitiveness. This lesson will provide an in-depth analysis of cost concepts, their classifications, and real-world applications.

Types of Costs in Economics

Fixed Costs (FC) – Overhead Costs

Fixed costs are expenses that do not change with the level of production. They must be paid even if output is zero.

Examples:
Rent for factory space
Salaries of permanent staff
Insurance premiums
Depreciation of machinery

Key Characteristics:

Time-bound: Fixed in the short run but may change in the long run (e.g., a company relocating to a cheaper facility).
Unavoidable: Must be paid regardless of business performance.

Variable Costs (VC) – Direct Costs

Variable costs fluctuate with the level of production. They increase as output rises and decrease when production falls.
Examples:
Raw materials (e.g., flour for a bakery)
Wages of temporary workers
Electricity for machinery
Packaging costs

Key Characteristics:
Directly linked to production: No production means no variable costs.
Can be controlled in the short run (e.g., reducing raw material purchases).

Capitalism / Free Market / Laissez-faire Economy

It is an economic system in which all or most means of production are owned and controlled by private individuals, and government control is at a minimum. Countries that practice capitalism include the USA, Italy, Japan, Australia, Germany, France, etc.

Features of Capitalism

Private Ownership of Property: The private sector owns and controls the means of production with minimal participation from the state/government.
Production and Consumption Regulated by the Price System: That is, by market forces.
Existence of Competition: Due to private ownership, it leads to competition or rivalry between and among firms.
Maximization of Profit: The sole aim of production is to make profit.

Labour as a Commodity: Labour is regarded as a marketable commodity.

Joint Decision Making: A greater number of people take collective decisions on what to produce, how to produce, and for whom to produce.
State Ownership: The state owns the means/factors of production.

Non-market Economy: The means of production are not purchased in the market, and not all goods produced are meant for sale.
Equitable Distribution of Income: Income is distributed equally among citizens.
Optimum Utilization / Full Employment of Factors of Production: Ensures efficient use of resources to produce desired goods.

Equitable Distribution of Income: Individuals do not own the means of production and therefore cannot control excess wealth at the expense of the majority.
Absence of exploitation.

Choice of Production: Consumers have a wide choice as to what is produced.
Prevention of private monopoly.
Equitable distribution of resources.
Economic Security: Employment and jobs for citizens are guaranteed and secured.
Increased employment opportunities.
Disadvantages of Socialism

Absence of alternative choices. Consumers have to take what is produced since there is no alternative choice of goods.
Creates room for laziness, since government provides everything for the people.
Reduces individual initiatives, because people depend on government for whatever they need.
Leads to monopoly, as the state provides all essential goods and services.
Difficulty in estimating wants, since people’s needs change over time and differ across individuals.
May give rise to dictatorship.
Absence of competition.

Mixed Economy

This is an economic system in which the means of production and distribution are owned and controlled by both private individuals and the state/public/government. Countries operating this system include Nigeria, Britain, Egypt, Kenya, Tanzania, Mexico, etc.

Features of Mixed Economy

Freedom of Choice: Consumers, workers, and property owners have freedom of choice.
Economic Freedom: There is freedom of production, distribution, and consumption.
Joint Decision: Decisions on what to produce and how to distribute are taken by both private and public sectors.
Fair Competition: Due to the presence of both private and state ownership.
Government Role: The government monopolizes the provision and ownership of essential resources.

Advantages of Mixed Economy

Ensures equitable distribution of incomes.
Ensures and promotes job security and employment.
Combines the good qualities of capitalism and socialism.
Ensures economic development.
Provides freedom of choice from a wide range of options.
Encourages private initiatives.

Disadvantages of Mixed Economy

Wealth is not distributed equally, creating a wide gap between the rich and the poor.
Profit maximization is often more paramount than the welfare of citizens.
Lack of efficiency due to state involvement.
High rate of corruption and mismanagement of resources.
Labour is usually exploited heavily.

WEEK 4 - LABOUR MARKET

LABOUR
Labour is often defined as the physical or mental effort exerted by human beings in the production of goods and services. In neoclassical economics, labour is a broader concept that incorporates all human activity that adds value to a product or service. This includes not only physical and mental effort but also the use of tools, machines, and other equipment. It also covers the time spent on planning, organizing, and supervising production.

In certain economic models, labour is assumed to be a homogeneous input. This means that all workers are assumed to have the same skills, abilities, and productivity. However, labour is not homogeneous in real life. Workers differ in their skills, abilities, experience, and motivation. These differences can lead to different amounts of output per hour of work, even when all other inputs are the same.

MARKET
Market refers to a means by which the exchange of goods and services takes place as a result of buyers and sellers being in contact with one another, either directly or through mediating agents or institutions. Markets, in the most literal and immediate sense, are places in which things are bought and sold.

In the modern industrial system, however, the market is not a place; it has expanded to include the whole geographical area in which sellers compete with each other for customers. Alfred Marshall, whose Principles of Economics (first published in 1890) was for long an authority for English-speaking economists, based his definition of the market on that of the French economist
A. Cournot.

Economists understand by the term "Market," not any particular marketplace in which things are bought and sold, but the whole of any region in which buyers and sellers are in such free intercourse with one another that the prices of the same goods tend to equality easily and quickly.

LABOUR MARKET
The term labour market refers to the supply of and demand for labour services. It is a market
where workers compete for jobs and employers compete for workers. The supply and demand for labour are determined by a series of factors, including the actual amount of work to be done, the number of workers available or able to do the work, and the level of wages that workers are willing to accept.

THE CONCEPT OF LABOUR FORCE
The labour force, or workforce, is the total number of people within a given population who are currently employed plus the number of people who are unemployed and actively seeking work. They are normally found within the age bracket of 18 to 65 years.

To be a member of the labour force one must be of working age (18–65 years), be able-bodied (i.e., not handicapped either mentally or physically), and must be willing to work.

PERSONS THAT ARE NOT MEMBERS OF THE LABOUR FORCE
A person is outside the labour force, according to the International Labour Organisation definition, if he or she is not part of the labour force, meaning he or she is neither employed nor unemployed.

The set of people outside the labour force is also called the "inactive population" and can include pre-school children, school children, students, pensioners, the elderly (above 65 years), and housewives or housemen, for example, provided that they are not working at all and not available or looking for work either. Some of these may still be of working age.

FACTORS AFFECTING THE SIZE OF LABOUR FORCE

Age – The size of the labor force is influenced by the age distribution of the population. As the population ages, the labor force may shrink, as more people retire and fewer people enter the workforce.
Sex – The labor force can also be influenced by gender differences, as certain industries or occupations may be more male- or female-dominated.
Occupation – The size of the labor force can be affected by the types of jobs available in a particular area or industry. For example, if there are more job opportunities in a certain field, it may attract more people to that industry and increase the size of the labor force.
Education – Education levels can also impact the size of the labor force, as people with higher levels of education may be more likely to enter certain professions or industries.
Migration – The rate of migration can also affect the size of the labour force. If the rate at which the working population leaves a country is higher than the rate at which people come in, it will lead to a reduction in the supply of labour.

EFFICIENCY OF LABOUR

Efficiency of labour can be defined as the ability of labour to increase output without increasing the level of input or the quantity of labour. Efficiency of labour refers to the ability of an employee to complete one task given within the time and without any wastage of resources.

Efficiency of labour implies the quality and quantity of goods and services which can be produced within a given time and under certain conditions. In other words, the productive capacity of a worker is termed as efficiency of labour.

FACTORS AFFECTING EFFICIENCY OF LABOUR:
The efficiency of labour depends on the following factors:

Climatic factors: Climatic differences also affect the efficiency of labour. Working under extreme climatic conditions is always more difficult than working in the temperate zones.

Geographical differences: Locational differences also sometimes play a significant role. A person who has been born and brought up in the plains will find it hard to display much efficiency if he is forced to work at high-altitude hilly areas.
Mental abilities: Mental abilities are also important. General training helps a worker in assimilating new skills and technical knowledge. Moral qualities also play a role in this connection. A worker is likely to be more efficient the greater his sense of discipline, self-respect, self-sufficiency, punctuality, etc.

Favourable working conditions: A healthy and conducive work environment increases the level of efficiency. The facilities enjoyed by the worker determine labour efficiency to a significant extent. Promotion: Frequent promotions of workers in any organisation can lead to an increase in the efficiency of labour.

Education and training: An educated worker understands their job better than those who are not educated. Therefore, his or her output will be good.

Personal qualities: Qualities like mental fitness, intelligence, soberness, resourcefulness, being a team player, and being responsible are building blocks of efficient labour.

WEEK 6 - ELEMENTARY TREATMENT OF UTILITY THEORY

Elementary Treatment of Utility Theory
Utility is the amount of satisfaction an individual or consumer derives from the consumption of a commodity or service at a particular time. A commodity or service possesses utility if the commodity is able to satisfy the consumer at that particular time. Utility is a relative term, meaning that what gives satisfaction to an individual may not give satisfaction to another person.

Types of Utility

The three types of utility are listed below:

Time utility
Form utility
Place utility

Time Utility
This is the ability of a commodity or service to satisfy a consumer’s wants at a particular time. This means that a commodity or service does not satisfy wants all the time. It has to do with the storage of goods until the time they are needed.

Form Utility
This is the transformation of a commodity from one form to another for the commodity to satisfy consumer wants. The change during the product manufacturing process increases its utility. For instance, flour does not satisfy the wants of consumers until it is changed to bread, cake, chin- chin, etc.

Place Utility
Place utility involves moving commodities from one location where utility is less to another place where utility or demand is high. For instance, cocoa is produced in West Africa and transported to Europe where it satisfies industrial needs. Yam can be moved from farm to market where it would be bought.
Concept of Marginal Utility, Total Utility and Average Utility

Total Utility (TU)
Total utility is the total amount of satisfaction a consumer derives from the consumption of a particular commodity at a point in time. The amount of satisfaction received from the consumption of goods does not increase at an equal rate to the quantity of goods supplied. The consumer has the saturation point in the consumption of a commodity at a given time.

The teacher further illustrates TU using a curve. The formula for calculating total utility is:
TU = AU × Q

Marginal Utility (MU)
Marginal utility means the additional or extra satisfaction a consumer derives from the consumption of an additional unit of output. Marginal utility is the change in the total utility as a result of consumption of an additional unit of a commodity. MU is illustrated in a diagram for better understanding.

The formula for calculating MU is:
MU = Change in Total Utility ÷ Change in Quantity

Average Utility (AU)
Average utility is the amount of satisfaction a consumer derives from the consumption of a unit of commodity. Average utility is obtained by dividing the total utility by the number of commodities consumed.

That is:
AU = TU ÷ Q

Relationship between Total and Marginal Utility

TU and MU increase up to a point, after which further increase in TU leads to a decrease in MU.
MU is the difference between two consecutive TU.
When TU is decreasing, MU is negative.
The summation of all MU is equal to TU.

The Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility

The law of diminishing marginal utility states that as a consumer consumes more and more of a particular commodity, utility (satisfaction) will increase up to a certain point and then start decreasing as a result of continuous consumption of the same commodity.

For instance, as a thirsty child keeps drinking water, the satisfaction keeps increasing up to a point and then starts decreasing as more water is consumed.

The concept of origin is the smallest (minimum) quantity of a commodity which must be consumed before the commodity can yield any satisfaction to the consumer. Consumer Surplus
Consumer surplus is the difference between the amount a consumer budgeted to pay for a commodity based on the expected level of satisfaction and the amount he actually paid to have the commodity.

That is, the difference between the expected or budgeted amount of a commodity and the actual amount of the commodity.

An indifference curve is the curve which shows the possible combinations of two commodities, with each combination yielding the same level of satisfaction to the consumer. It does not matter to the consumer which combination he gets. If he gets combination X instead of combination Y, he will not feel any better off.

Changes in Equilibrium Price

Changes in demand and supply lead to a change in the equilibrium price. The market equilibrium price can be affected in four ways:

A decrease in demand
An increase in supply

Increase in Demand

If the demand for a commodity increases while its supply remains constant, there will be an excess demand over supply.
This will lead to an increase in the equilibrium price of the commodity as well as an increase in the equilibrium quantity.
This is illustrated in a diagram.

Decrease in Demand

If the demand for a commodity decreases while its supply remains constant, there will be an excess of supply over demand.
This results in a decrease in the equilibrium price and quantity of the commodity. This is typically illustrated in a diagram for better understanding.

Increase in Supply
If the supply increases while demand remains constant, there will be an excess of supply over demand.
This will bring about a decrease in equilibrium price of the commodity and an increase in the equilibrium quantity.
This is further illustrated in a graph.

Decrease in Supply

A decrease in supply without any change in demand will lead to an excess of demand over supply, making the equilibrium price increase and the equilibrium quantity decrease.
This is also illustrated in a graph and further explained.

Laws of Demand and Supply

The five laws of demand and supply are listed below:

The lower the price, the higher the quantity demanded, and the higher the price, the lower the quantity demanded.
The higher the price, the higher the quantity supplied, and the lower the price, the lower the quantity supplied.
The price at which quantity demanded is equal to quantity supplied is known as equilibrium price.
An increase in quantity demanded will cause an increase in both equilibrium price and the quantity supplied, while a decrease in demand will cause both equilibrium price and the quantity supplied to fall.
An increase in the supply of a commodity will cause the equilibrium price to fall and quantity demanded to rise, while a decrease in supply will cause equilibrium price to rise and quantity demanded to fall.

Types of Demand

The four types of demand are as follows:
Derived demand
Joint or complementary demand
Competitive demand
Composite demand

Derived Demand

This type of demand occurs as a result of demand for other commodities.
The demand for one commodity makes the demand for another commodity necessary.
For instance, demand for road construction brings about the demand for labour. Flour and sugar are demanded because there is a demand for bread.

Joint or Complementary Demand

This type of demand occurs when two commodities that are related to each other are demanded at the same time.
Examples include bread and butter, tea and milk, car and petrol, etc.

Competitive Demand

When two commodities are fairly close substitutes for each other, they are in competitive demand.
In other words, they serve the same purpose.
An increase in the demand for one commodity will result in a decrease in the demand for the other.
For instance, Omo and Elephant detergent, Klin and Ariel, butter and margarine, etc.

Composite Demand

Demand is said to be composite when a commodity is required to serve two or more purposes. For instance, sugar is used in the home for beverages as well as in industries for making pastries and confectionery.
If the industrial demand for sugar increases, it will affect the quantity of sugar demanded in the home.

Types of Supply

The three types of supply are listed below:

Joint or complementary supply
Competitive supply
Composite supply

Joint or Complementary Supply

This occurs when two or more commodities are produced and supplied from one source.
An increase in the production and supply of one will automatically bring about an increase in the production and supply of the other commodities that are produced from the same source.
For instance, an increase in the production and supply of petrol can also lead to an increase in the supply of kerosene.

Competitive Supply

This type of supply occurs when many commodities are supplied to satisfy a particular want. It is the supply of two or more commodities that serve as substitutes for one another.
Examples: Ariel and Klin, butter and margarine, fish and meat, Close-up and Macleans, etc.

Composite Supply

This type of supply occurs when a certain commodity can serve two or more purposes.
The supply of the commodity for one purpose will affect the supply of the commodity for another purpose.
For instance, the supply of crude oil for the production of petrol will greatly affect the production of kerosene.

WEEK 9 - MARKET STRUCTURE

In economics, the term Market can be defined as any arrangement, system, or organization whereby buyers and sellers of goods and services are brought into contact with one another for the purpose of transacting business.

Types of Market

Markets could be classified on the basis of the type of commodities bought and sold, or on the basis of the channel of movement, or on the basis of prices.

Markets based on types of commodities include labour market, consumer goods market, capital market, and money market.
Markets according to prices are: perfect and imperfect markets.

TYPES OF MARKET ACCORDING TO PRICES

PERFECT MARKET
A perfect market is a market structure in which prices are determined by the forces of demand and supply. Demand and supply operate without interference by the government or its agencies.

Perfect competition exists if the following features or conditions are present:

Features of a Perfect Market

Homogeneous commodity: The commodities must be identical, i.e. the same size, shape, and colour. Trademarks or branding must not be used to differentiate a particular product. For example, tea should be written as tea and not Top Tea or Lipton.
There is a large number of buyers and sellers, none of whom has control over the ruling price.
All buyers must have perfect knowledge of all the market transactions.
There must be free entry and exit for all buyers and sellers.
The cost of moving goods is assumed to be zero.
All the firms have identical cost structures. They incur the same costs in producing a commodity.

A perfect market does not exist in reality but is a theoretical concept. Most markets in existence are imperfect in nature.

IMPERFECT MARKET
An imperfect market may be defined as the market in which prices of goods and services can easily be influenced by the sellers or buyers. Imperfect market is also called imperfect competition.

Features of an Imperfect Market
The goods are heterogeneous. The goods sold are not identical and are altered by the use of trademarks and branding.
There are few buyers and sellers, therefore prices can be influenced.
There is no free flow of information in the market.
Preferential treatment exists in an imperfect market. The producer could sell at a lower price to some and at higher prices to others.
There is no free entry into and exit from the market.
Transport costs are incurred in moving goods and factors of production.

Types of Imperfect Market

Monopolistic Competition: This is a market situation where there are many sellers or producers selling or producing non-homogeneous commodities.
Oligopoly: This is a market situation where there are few producers or sellers of the same commodity.
Duopoly: This is a market situation in which there are only two sellers or producers of a commodity.
Monopsony: This is the type of market in which there is only one buyer for a product e.g. commodity boards.
Oligopsony: This is a market where there are few buyers and many sellers of a commodity.

MONOPOLY

Monopoly may be defined as a market situation where there is only one producer or supplier of a particular good or service that has no substitute. The monopolist has the power to influence the price of goods in his favour.

The goods sold by a monopolist are normally differentiated.

Causes of Monopoly
Act of Parliament: This is a legal instrument by the government conferring special monopoly on some organization to produce or supply certain goods or services e.g. public corporation.
Patent Law: This law confers on a firm special privilege to protect its new invention.
Natural Cause: Nature does not distribute resources evenly; as a result, there are some areas that have resources which other areas do not have e.g. crude oil in Niger Delta.
Merging of Producers: The merging of producers will make them stronger to be able to eliminate other competitors in the business.
Protection of Public Interest: Deliberate effort to protect public interest by the government may confer certain monopoly on some firms e.g. power holding company.
Level of Technology: When a firm develops a high level of technology which makes goods cheaper, this may force other competitors out of production.

Advantages of Monopoly

Overproduction is reduced: The monopolist estimates demand more accurately and tries to produce the quantity required by the consumers.
Duplication of resources is avoided.
There may be greater efficiency and full utilization of productive resources.
Production costs and prices may be reduced: Since he is the only producer, he is likely to produce on a larger scale which would lead to lower cost per unit.
Research and invention may be stimulated.

Disadvantages of Monopoly

Exploitation of consumers: This is made possible since he is the only seller of the commodity.
It leads to hoarding: The desire for supernormal profit by the monopolist may lead to restriction in output and hoarding.
Decline in efficiency: Due to lack of competition the monopolist may become less innovative and enterprising.
Restriction of consumers’ choice: The monopolist is the only seller available so there are no other options of sellers or similar commodities available.
Exertion of political interest: Large monopolies could pressurize the government to pass laws to protect their interests.

Control of Monopoly

Provision of substitute products: Monopolies exist because their products have no substitute.
Privatisation: Private individuals should be encouraged to take over government corporations and agencies in order to eliminate monopoly.
Stoppage of issuance of patent law: The stoppage will encourage more people to compete with the inventor.
Discouraging merging of firms.
Reduction of tariffs.

Price and Quantity Determination Under Perfect Competition

In a perfect market, also known as a competitive market or perfect competition, the buyers or sellers cannot influence the prices of goods and services. The perfect competitor produces the most profitable output where his marginal cost equals marginal revenue. Any production beyond this point will lead to lower profits because the marginal cost of production will be higher than the marginal revenue.
