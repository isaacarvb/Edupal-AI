WEEK 4: CURRENT ACCOUNT IN PARTNERSHIP ACCOUNTING

Introduction

In partnership accounting, each partner contributes capital to the business and may also engage in other financial interactions such as earning salaries, withdrawing funds (drawings), or receiving interest on capital. These regular, often recurring transactions between the partners and the partnership firm are not recorded directly in the capital account when the fixed capital method is adopted. Instead, they are documented in a separate ledger account known as the Current Account.

The Current Account plays a vital role in reflecting the dynamic financial relationship between a partner and the partnership, outside of the initial capital invested.

Purpose of the Current Account

The primary purpose of maintaining a Current Account in a partnership firm is to:

Track Routine or Regular Partner Transactions: These include the partner’s drawings (withdrawals for personal use), salaries paid to partners for their work in the business, interest on capital, interest on drawings, and the partner’s share of profit or loss.
Separate Operational Transactions from Capital Investment: In partnerships that adopt the fixed capital method, the capital account remains unchanged unless there is a clear addition or withdrawal of capital. All routine financial exchanges between the partner and the firm are managed through the current account.
Measure Each Partner’s Entitlement or Obligation: At any given point, the current account shows whether a partner is owed money by the business (credit balance) or owes money to the business (debit balance).

Items Included in the Current Account

The current account reflects both income and expenses related to the partner during the accounting period.
Credit Entries (Increases in Partner’s Entitlement):

These entries reflect amounts owed to the partner and are recorded on the credit side:

Interest on Capital: A reward for investing capital in the firm.
Partner’s Salary or Commission: As compensation for managing or contributing services to the partnership.
Share of Net Profit: The portion of business profits due to the partner, based on the agreed profit-sharing ratio.

Debit Entries (Reductions in Partner’s Entitlement):

These entries reflect amounts owed by the partner to the firm and are recorded on the debit side:

Drawings: Withdrawals made by the partner for personal use.
Interest on Drawings: A charge on the partner for early withdrawals from the business fund.
Share of Loss (if any): Deducted if the firm incurs a loss and it is to be borne by the partner.

Format of a Partner’s Current Account

The current account is maintained in a T-account format with two sides:

The debit side reduces the partner’s entitlement, while the credit side increases it. At the end of the period, the closing balance of the current account is carried forward and added to the capital account on the balance sheet.

Relationship Between Capital Account and Current Account

In the fixed capital method, the capital account shows only the original capital introduced by the partner and any additional capital introduced or withdrawn over time. It remains largely unchanged throughout the accounting period.

All other financial transactions involving the partner (except capital contributions or withdrawals) are recorded in the current account. This system provides clarity and neat separation between long-term investments (capital) and operational transactions (current account).

Conversely, in the fluctuating capital method, both capital contributions and day-to-day transactions are recorded in a single capital account. There is no need for a separate current account under this method.

WEEK 6: ADMISSION OF A NEW PARTNER AND GOODWILL ACCOUNT

Introduction

A partnership is a dynamic business form that allows for flexibility in its composition. As the business grows, it may become necessary to admit a new partner to bring in additional capital, fresh ideas, or special skills. However, the admission of a new partner impacts various aspects of the partnership, including the valuation of assets, distribution of profits, and the recognition of goodwill.

Reasons for Admitting a New Partner

There are several business and strategic reasons for bringing a new partner into an existing partnership:

To raise additional capital for expansion or to meet business obligations.
To introduce new expertise, skills, or business connections that can enhance operations or productivity.
To share the workload and responsibilities in managing the business.
To replace a retiring or deceased partner.
To improve creditworthiness or attract new clients or markets.

Admitting a new partner often results in changes in profit-sharing ratios, revaluation of assets and liabilities, and recognition of goodwill.

Revaluation of Assets and Liabilities

Before admitting a new partner, the existing partners may decide to revalue the business's assets and liabilities to reflect their true (fair market) value. This ensures that:

Existing partners gain or bear the benefit or loss from the revaluation.
The incoming partner does not benefit from previous undervaluation of assets or suffer from overstatements of liabilities.
The difference between the old and new valuations is recorded in a Revaluation Account (also called Profit or Loss on Revaluation).

WEEK 8: RECEIPTS AND PAYMENTS ACCOUNT / INCOME AND EXPENDITURE ACCOUNT

Introduction

Non-profit organisations such as schools, clubs, associations, and religious bodies do not aim to make profits like businesses. Instead, their objective is to provide services to their members or the public. Because of this, they prepare Receipts and Payments Accounts and Income and Expenditure Accounts—not Trading, Profit and Loss Accounts.

These two accounts serve similar roles in nonprofit accounting as the Cash Book and Income Statement do in commercial enterprises, but they have distinct purposes, formats, and accounting bases.

Receipts and Payments Account

A Receipts and Payments Account is a summary of all cash and bank transactions of a non- profit organisation during a particular period, usually a financial year.

It is prepared on a cash basis, meaning it records all actual cash received and paid, regardless of the period to which they relate.

Features:

Similar to a cash book (cash + bank column).
Includes both capital and revenue items.
Records both current and previous/future period transactions.
No distinction between recurring and non-recurring items.
Does not show surplus or deficit—just cash movement.

Income and Expenditure Account

An Income and Expenditure Account is similar to the Profit and Loss Account of a business. It is prepared to determine the surplus (excess of income over expenditure) or deficit of a non-profit organisation for a specific period.

It is prepared on an accrual basis, meaning only incomes earned and expenses incurred during the year are recorded—regardless of whether cash was received or paid.

Features:

Records only revenue items (no capital receipts/payments).
Includes only income and expenses relating to the current year.
Adjustments are made for prepaid and accrued incomes and expenses.
Aims to calculate surplus or deficit, not profit or loss.
Does not include opening or closing balances.

WEEK 9: SINGLE ENTRY (INCOMPLETE RECORDS)

Introduction

Most well-established businesses maintain a complete set of double-entry records, where every transaction affects two accounts — a debit and a credit. However, some small businesses or individuals may not maintain full double-entry records due to lack of knowledge, resources, or deliberate choice. These are referred to as maintaining Single Entry or Incomplete Records.

This lesson introduces students to the nature of single entry, its limitations, and how accountants convert it into usable double-entry information.

Meaning of Single Entry (Incomplete Records)

Single entry refers to an incomplete method of bookkeeping where only partial records of financial transactions are maintained. Typically, the business records:

Cash and bank transactions
Personal accounts of debtors and creditors But omits:
Complete ledger accounts (e.g., capital, expenses, income)
Nominal accounts and real accounts
Systematic balancing and posting

This makes it difficult to prepare accurate final accounts directly.

Features of Single Entry / Incomplete Records

No double-entry principle is applied consistently.
Only personal accounts (debtors and creditors) and cash/bank transactions may be maintained.
Real and nominal accounts (like assets, liabilities, expenses, and incomes) are not recorded in full.
Trial Balance cannot be prepared.
Final accounts (e.g., Trading and Profit & Loss) cannot be prepared without adjustments.

Reasons Why Some Businesses Use Single Entry

Lack of accounting knowledge
Small scale of operation
Intention to reduce accounting costs
Informal business arrangements
Lack of legal obligations for detailed reporting

Limitations of Single Entry

Inability to detect errors or fraud easily
Cannot prepare a reliable trial balance
Difficult to assess actual financial position
Inadequate for planning and decision-making
Not acceptable under accounting standards or for tax purposes

Conversion of Single Entry into Double Entry

To obtain useful financial information from single entry, accountants must reconstruct missing data by:

Using the Statement of Affairs Method

This is used to determine the opening and closing capital positions in order to calculate the profit or loss for the period.

Formula:

Profit/Loss = Closing Capital – Opening Capital + Drawings – Additional Capital Introduced

Reconstructing Missing Accounts
Using available information to prepare:

Total Debtors Account
Total Creditors Account
Cash/Bank Book
Statement of Affairs (Net Worth Statement)

Example – Statement of Affairs Method Given:
Opening Capital: ₦100,000
Closing Capital: ₦130,000
Drawings during the year: ₦15,000
Additional capital introduced: ₦5,000 Required: Calculate the profit or loss for the year. Solution:
Profit = Closing Capital – Opening Capital + Drawings – Additional Capital Profit = ₦130,000 – ₦100,000 + ₦15,000 – ₦5,000 = ₦40,000

So, the profit for the year is ₦40,000.

WEEK 1: FORMATION OF A COMPANY

Introduction

A company is a legal entity formed to carry out business with the aim of making profits. It has its own legal identity, separate from its owners. Before a company can begin operations, it must go through a series of legal and procedural steps known as the formation of a company.

This lesson introduces students to the stages involved in company formation, the documents required, and the relevant legal processes that ensure its incorporation and legitimacy.

Definition of a Company

A company is an artificial person, created by law, having a separate legal identity from its owners (called shareholders), and having perpetual succession. It can own property, sue and be sued in its own name.

Meaning of Formation of a Company

Formation of a company refers to the process of registering a company with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) in Nigeria (or relevant authority in other countries), and taking necessary steps to give it legal status and allow it to begin operations.

This process involves several stages and legal documents, which are essential to comply with statutory regulations.

Stages in the Formation of a Company

There are generally four main stages involved in forming a company:

Promotion

This is the first stage where the idea of forming a company is conceived.
The promoters carry out the preliminary work, such as:
Identifying the business opportunity
Arranging for capital
Engaging professionals (lawyers, accountants)
Drafting formation documents

Incorporation (Registration)

The company is registered with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) in Nigeria.
Key documents to be submitted include:
Memorandum of Association (MoA)
Articles of Association (AoA)
Form CAC 1.1 (Application form)
Statement of share capital and return of allotment
Payment of prescribed fees

Once approved, the company is issued a Certificate of Incorporation, making it a legal entity.

Capital Subscription

Applies to public companies.
Involves the company issuing a prospectus to the public to subscribe for shares.
The aim is to raise the required minimum capital before commencing operations.

Commencement of Business

After successful incorporation and capital subscription (for public companies), the company applies for a Certificate of Commencement of Business.
For private companies, this is not mandatory as they can commence business immediately after incorporation.

Key Documents Used in Formation

Memorandum of Association (MoA)

This defines the company’s external relationships and includes:
Name of the company
Registered office address
Objectives of the company
Liability of members
Share capital structure

Articles of Association (AoA)

This defines the company’s internal regulations, including:

WEEK 6: ISSUE OF SHARES
Introduction

In corporate finance, a share represents a unit of ownership in a company. Companies often need to raise funds to start operations, expand their activities, or finance special projects. One common method is through the issue of shares to investors. This process allows a company to obtain capital without incurring debt, in exchange for giving investors a stake in the business.
Shares are issued by limited liability companies and are usually purchased by members of the public or existing shareholders. The process and method of issuing shares are governed by the Companies and Allied Matters Act (CAMA) in Nigeria and similar company laws elsewhere.

Meaning of Share Issue

The issue of shares refers to the process by which a company allocates new shares to individuals or entities in exchange for payment. The money received from the sale of shares becomes part of the company’s capital, which is used to fund its activities.
Shares can be issued for:

Cash – where investors pay money in exchange for shares.

Non-cash consideration – where shares are issued in exchange for assets or services.

Types of Shares

Ordinary Shares – Carry voting rights and dividends are paid depending on profits.

Preference Shares – Have preferential rights to dividends and repayment in case of liquidation.
Deferred Shares – Dividends are paid after all other classes of shares have been paid.

Redeemable Shares – Shares that the company can buy back in the future.
Methods of Issuing Shares

The main methods of issuing shares include:

Offer for Subscription (Public Issue)

The company invites the general public to apply for shares via a prospectus.

Applications are submitted along with application money.

The company allots shares after reviewing applications.

This is common for companies seeking large-scale funding.

Offer for Sale

The company sells all its shares to an issuing house (e.g., a stockbroker or investment bank).
The issuing house then sells the shares to the public.

This method reduces administrative burden on the company.

Right Issue

Existing shareholders are given the right to purchase more shares in proportion to their existing holdings.
Usually offered at a price lower than the market price.

Used to raise additional capital while giving preference to current investors.

Private Placement

Shares are sold to selected individuals or institutions, not to the general public.

Usually quicker and involves fewer regulatory processes.

Bonus Issue (Scrip Issue)

Existing shareholders are given free shares in proportion to their existing holdings.

No new money is raised; it is a capitalization of reserves.

Steps in Issuing Shares for Cash

Authorisation – The Company’s Articles of Association must permit share issuance.

Prospectus or Offer Document – Published if the shares are offered to the public.

Application – Investors submit applications with the application money.

Allotment – The Company decides how many shares each applicant will receive.

Calls on Shares – The company may request further payments in instalments (e.g., on application, allotment, first call, final call).

If the number of shares subscribed is less than the minimum subscription required by law, the issue is considered unsuccessful and money is refunded.

WEEK 8: LOAN CAPITAL

Introduction

To fund large-scale projects, meet operational needs, or expand into new markets, companies require long-term finance. While issuing shares is one option, another common method is to raise money through loan capital.

Loan capital represents borrowed funds that must be repaid with interest over a specified period. It is a vital part of a company’s capital structure and differs from equity financing in many ways.

This lesson will explore the meaning, types, features, advantages, disadvantages, and accounting treatment of loan capital.

Meaning of Loan Capital

Loan capital refers to money borrowed by a company from external sources (such as individuals, banks, or financial institutions) which must be repaid at an agreed date with interest.

Unlike share capital, loan capital does not give the lender ownership rights in the company.

Loan capital is often used for long-term financing, and it usually comes in the form of
debentures, bonds, loans, or mortgages. Features of Loan Capital
Debt Obligation: Must be repaid by the company regardless of profit or loss.
Interest Payment: Fixed or variable interest is payable at agreed intervals.
No Ownership Rights: Lenders are not part owners of the company.
Priority in Repayment: In the event of liquidation, lenders are paid before shareholders.
Fixed Term: Usually repayable over a long-term period (e.g., 5, 10, or 15 years).
Security/Collateral: May be secured (backed by company assets) or unsecured.

Types of Loan Capital
Debentures

A written acknowledgment of debt issued by the company under its seal.
Can be secured or unsecured.
May be redeemable or irredeemable.
Interest is paid regardless of profits.

Bonds

Similar to debentures but more common in international markets.
Can be government or corporate bonds.

Term Loans

Loans from banks or financial institutions for a specified period.
Repaid in installments or at maturity.

Mortgages

Long-term loans secured on real estate (land or buildings).

The capital market is a part of the financial system that deals with long-term borrowing and lending of funds, typically beyond one year. It serves as a platform for the issuance and trading of securities like shares and bonds. Entities that participate in the capital market include:

Government institutions
Investment banks

There are several ways organisations and governments can raise capital in the capital market. These include:

This method involves offering new shares to existing shareholders in proportion to their current holdings.

Introduction

The capital market provides opportunities for companies and governments to raise long-term funds for expansion, investment, and infrastructure development. This lesson discusses the essential conditions companies must satisfy to be eligible for participation in the capital market, particularly for listing on a stock exchange like the Nigerian Exchange Group (NGX).

Meaning of Capital Market Access

Accessing the capital market means that a company has met the necessary conditions to raise capital through the sale of shares, bonds, or other securities to the public or institutional investors through the stock exchange or over-the-counter (OTC) platforms.

This process is not automatic — it requires transparency, legal compliance, and financial readiness.

Regulatory Authorities Involved

Before accessing the capital market, a company must be approved by relevant regulatory bodies including:

Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) – Overall regulator of the capital market in Nigeria.
Nigerian Exchange Group (NGX) – Oversees the listing and trading of securities.
Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) – Registers companies and ensures compliance with company law.

Key Requirements for Accessing the Capital Market
Legal Incorporation

The company must be registered with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) as a public limited company (Plc).
It must have a valid Certificate of Incorporation.
The Memorandum and Articles of Association (MoA and AoA) must contain provisions authorizing the issuance of shares to the public.

Minimum Share Capital

The company must meet the minimum paid-up capital required by the SEC and NGX.
For main board listing in Nigeria, this is often ₦300 million or more, depending on sector and category.

Financial Performance

The company must demonstrate profitable operations over a specified period (usually 2–3 years).
It must prepare and present audited financial statements, signed by licensed professionals.

Corporate Governance Standards

The company must have a functioning Board of Directors, including independent directors.
It must comply with the Nigerian Code of Corporate Governance, ensuring transparency, accountability, and fairness.

Disclosure and Documentation

The company must submit the following documents to SEC and NGX:

Audited financial statements (3 years minimum)
Application form and prospectus or information memorandum
Details of board members and top management
Capital structure and shareholding details
Compliance with tax obligations

Appointment of Professional Advisers

Companies must engage authorized capital market operators such as:

Issuing houses (to manage the offer)
Stockbrokers (to interface with the exchange)
Registrars (to manage shareholder records)
Auditors, solicitors, and financial advisers

Prospectus Preparation

A prospectus is a legal document that describes the investment offering to the public.
It must contain full disclosures about the company’s history, financials, risks, and governance.
Must be vetted and approved by the SEC.

SEC and Exchange Approval

The offer must be registered and approved by the SEC.
The NGX also conducts a review of the company’s documentation before approving listing.

Ongoing Compliance Requirements (Post-Access)

Once listed, companies must continue to meet certain obligations:

Regular submission of financial reports (quarterly, annual)
Public disclosure of material information (e.g., board changes, mergers)
Annual General Meetings (AGMs)
Dividend declarations and transparency in operations

Fair and transparent operations
Financial system stability

Protect Investors: Safeguard public and institutional investors from exploitation.

WEEK 8: HIRE PURCHASE AND INSTALLMENT PAYMENT ACCOUNT

Introduction

In today’s economy, many individuals and businesses acquire goods without paying the full price upfront. Two common methods of deferred payment are hire purchase and installment payment. These methods allow buyers to possess goods immediately while paying over time.
However, the ownership structure, interest calculation, and accounting treatment differ between the two.

This lesson explains the meaning, features, differences, and accounting procedures of hire purchase and installment payment systems.

Meaning of Hire Purchase

Hire Purchase is a system of buying goods where the buyer takes possession immediately but becomes the legal owner only after the final payment is made.

Payments are made in installments.
Interest is usually charged.
If the buyer defaults, the seller has the right to repossess the goods.

Legal Title passes only after the last installment is paid.

Features of Hire Purchase

Ownership is retained by the seller until full payment.
The buyer enjoys use of the asset while paying.
Buyer can return the goods before full payment.
Interest is charged on unpaid balance.
Regulated by Hire Purchase Act in many jurisdictions.

Meaning of Installment Payment
Installment Payment (also known as credit sale) is a system where the buyer takes ownership immediately but pays for the goods in agreed installments.

The legal title is transferred immediately.
The seller cannot repossess the goods if the buyer defaults.
The seller can only sue for payment.

Ownership passes at the point of sale, even though payment is made over time.

Features of Installment Payment

Immediate transfer of ownership to buyer.
Full use and risk of asset lie with the buyer.
Goods cannot be returned unless defective.
Interest may be charged.
Default leads to legal action, not repossession.
