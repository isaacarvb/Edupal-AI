GRAMMAR: NOMINALIZATION OF ADJECTIVES AND VERBS
INTRODUCTION

Nominalisation is a sophisticated grammatical tool used to transform other parts of speech— primarily verbs and adjectives—into nouns or noun phrases. This process elevates your writing from simple descriptions of actions and qualities to discussions of abstract concepts and entities. It is a hallmark of formal, academic, and professional writing.
Without Nominalisation: "The manager decided quickly. This was efficient." (Focus on actions).
With Nominalisation: "The manager's quick decision demonstrated efficiency." (Focus on concepts/things).

DETAILED CONTENT: THE NOMINALIZATION PROCESS

There are two primary methods: suffix addition and conversion.

Nominalization by Suffix Addition This is the most common method. We attach a suffix to the root word, which often changes the spelling.

ORAL ENGLISH: CONSONANT SOUNDS (VOICED & VOICELESS)
INTRODUCTION

In phonetics, a crucial distinction between consonant sounds is voicing. This refers to whether the vocal cords vibrate when the sound is produced.
Voiceless Sounds: The vocal cords are apart and do not vibrate. The sound is produced by air only (like a whisper). Place your fingers on your throat; you will feel no vibration.
Voiced Sounds: The vocal cords are close together and vibrate. You will feel a distinct buzzing sensation in your throat.
Practical Test: Say "ssssss" (like a snake). You feel nothing. Now say "zzzzzz" (like a bee). You feel a vibration. /s/ is voiceless; /z/ is voiced.
DETAILED CONTENT: THE CONSONANT PAIRS

Many English consonants form pairs where the mouth position is identical; the only difference is voicing.

SUMMARY WRITING: INTRODUCTION TO SUMMARY
INTRODUCTION

A summary is a concise, objective, and fluent restatement of the main ideas of a text, written in your own words. It demonstrates your comprehension by stripping away supporting details, examples, and repetitions, leaving only the essential core of the author's message. It is not a commentary or a critique.
DETAILED CONTENT: FEATURES AND RULES

Features of a Good Summary:

Brevity: Significantly shorter than the original.
Clarity: Easy to understand and grammatically correct.
Objectivity: Contains only the author's ideas, not your opinions.
Completeness: Includes all the main points relevant to the question.
Paraphrasing: Expressed predominantly in your own words.

The Do's and Don'ts of Summary Writing:

ORAL ENGLISH: CONSONANT CLUSTERS
INTRODUCTION

A consonant cluster (also known as a consonant blend) is a sequence of two or more consonant sounds that appear together in a syllable without any vowel sounds between them. Mastering these clusters is crucial for clear, fluent, and natural-sounding English pronunciation. A common error for learners is epenthesis—the unintentional insertion of a vowel sound (typically the schwa /ə/) within a cluster, which breaks the word unnaturally.
Correct: film (/fɪlm/)
Incorrect (with epenthesis): fi-lim (/'fɪl.ɪm/)
DETAILED CONTENT: TYPES OF CLUSTERS

Two-Consonant Clusters These are the most common and form the basis for more complex clusters.
Initial Clusters (Start of a word):

/s/ + consonant: stay, sky, slow, swim, snow
/l/ blends: blow, clean, fly, glass, play
/r/ blends: bring, cry, dry, free, grow, pray, tree
Final Clusters (End of a word):

-st: last, past, test
-nd: hand, send, find
-sk: task, desk, ask
-mp: jump, lamp, camp
-ft: left, gift, soft
-ld: gold, hold, child
Three-Consonant Clusters These require careful articulation to avoid skipping or adding sounds.
Initial Clusters: These almost always begin with /s/.

/spr/: spring, spray, sprout
/str/: street, strong, strike
/skr/: script, scream, scrub
/spl/: splash, split, spleen
Final Clusters: Often formed by adding inflectional endings like -s or -ed.

-nts: tents, wants, pants
-mps: jumps, lamps, pumps
-sks: tasks, desks, masks
-pts: scripts, adapts, excerpts
-lps: helps, gulps, pulps

Four (and Five) Consonant Clusters These are complex and typically occur at the end of words. The key is to articulate each consonant cleanly and quickly without inserting a vowel.
-fths: twelfths (/twɛlfθs/), fifths (/fɪfθs/)
-ngths: strengths (/strɛŋθs/), lengths (/lɛŋθs/)
-ksts: texts (/tɛksts/), next (/nɛkst/)
-mpst: glimpsed (/ɡlɪmpst/)
-mpts: prompts (/prɒmpts/), attempts (/ə'tɛmpts/)

WRITING: EXPOSITORY ESSAY
INTRODUCTION

An expository essay is a formal piece of writing that aims to explain, describe, or inform the reader about a topic. Its primary purpose is to present a balanced, objective analysis based on facts. Unlike an argumentative essay, it does not seek to persuade the reader to adopt a particular opinion, but rather to "expose" information clearly and logically.
DETAILED CONTENT: STRUCTURE OF AN EXPOSITORY ESSAY

A well-structured essay is built like a house: a strong foundation (introduction), supporting walls (body paragraphs), and a solid roof (conclusion).
1. The Introduction (1 paragraph):

Hook: Begin with a compelling statement, a rhetorical question, or a striking fact to engage the reader.
Background: Provide brief context or define key terms related to the topic.
Thesis Statement: This is the cornerstone of your essay. It is a single, clear sentence that states the main point or central argument you will explain.
2. The Body (Typically 3 paragraphs):

Each paragraph should develop one distinct main point that supports your thesis.
Topic Sentence: The first sentence of each body paragraph, which states the main idea of that paragraph.
Supporting Details: Use facts, statistics, examples, and logical explanations to elaborate on your topic sentence.
Concluding Sentence (Optional but recommended): Summarises the paragraph and may provide a transition to the next point.
Use Transitional Words: Words like Furthermore, Moreover, In addition, Consequently, For instance ensure a smooth flow between ideas and paragraphs.
3. The Conclusion (1 paragraph):
Restate Thesis: Rephrase your thesis statement in new words, reflecting the explanation you have provided.
Summarise Main Points: Briefly recap the key arguments from your body paragraphs.
Final Thought: Offer a broader implication, a final insight, or a recommendation based on the information presented. Avoid introducing new ideas.
ROBUST WORKED EXAMPLE (ESSAY OUTLINE)
Topic: "The Three Major Causes of Road Accidents in Nigeria"

Thesis Statement: "The high rate of road accidents in Nigeria is primarily attributable to human error, poor vehicle maintenance, and inadequate road infrastructure."
Body Paragraph 1:

Topic Sentence: The predominant cause of road accidents is human error, often exacerbated by reckless behaviour.
Supporting Details: Discuss speeding, dangerous overtaking, driving under the influence of alcohol/drugs, and distraction from mobile phones. Provide a hypothetical or general example.
Body Paragraph 2:

Topic Sentence: Secondly, the poor mechanical condition of many vehicles on the road presents a significant hazard.
Supporting Details: Explain issues like bald tyres, faulty brakes, and non- functional lights. Link this to a lack of rigorous vehicle inspection regimes.
Body Paragraph 3:

Topic Sentence: Furthermore, the deplorable state of road infrastructure across the country contributes substantially to accidents.
Supporting Details: Describe problems such as potholes, lack of proper signage, non-functional streetlights, and eroded roads.
Conclusion:
Restate that road accidents are a complex issue stemming from the triad of driver behaviour, vehicle condition, and road quality.
Summarise the three points.
Final thought: A call for a multi-pronged approach involving stricter law enforcement, public education, and government investment in infrastructure is essential to curb this menace.

STRUCTURE: NOUNS AND NOUN PHRASES
INTRODUCTION

A Noun is a fundamental building block of language, naming a person, place, thing, or idea. A Noun Phrase is a group of words built around a noun that functions as a single unit within a sentence. Understanding the complexity and function of noun phrases is essential for analysing sophisticated writing and constructing detailed, grammatically correct sentences.
Simple Noun: The cat slept.
Noun Phrase: The exceptionally fluffy ginger cat from next door slept.
DETAILED CONTENT

Types of Nouns (Review and Expansion)

Proper: Specific, capitalised names (e.g., Chinua, Abuja, Christmas).
Common: General names for people, places, things (e.g., artist, city, holiday).
Abstract: Names for ideas, qualities, or states that cannot be physically touched (e.g., freedom, bravery, justice).
Collective: Names for groups of people, animals, or things (e.g., team, flock, fleet).
Concrete: Names for things that can be perceived by the senses (e.g., rain, sand, perfume).
Countable: Have singular and plural forms (e.g., book/books, child/children).
Uncountable: Cannot be counted and have no plural form (e.g., advice, furniture, information).
Noun Phrases (NP) - The Core Concept A noun phrase (NP) consists of a Headword (the main noun) and all the words that modify it (describe, define, or quantify it).
Structure: (Determiner) + (Pre-modifiers) + (Headword Noun) + (Post-modifiers)
Determiner: a, an, the, this, that, my, some, five
Pre-modifiers: Adjectives, other nouns (e.g., university student)
Headword Noun: The core of the phrase.
Post-modifiers: Prepositional phrases, relative clauses, participle clauses.

Illustrative Examples:

a idea (Determiner + Headword)
a brilliant idea (Determiner + Pre-modifier + Headword)
a brilliant idea for a new project (Determiner + Pre-modifier + Headword + Post-modifier)
the tall, intelligent boy who won the prize (Determiner + Pre-modifiers + Headword + Post-modifier)
Functions of Nouns/Noun Phrases in a Sentence A noun/noun phrase can perform several key roles:
Subject of the Verb (S): The 'doer' of the action.

[The diligent student] passed the exam.
Object of the Verb (O): The 'receiver' of the action.

He bought [a new bicycle].
Subject Complement (C): Renames or describes the subject after a linking verb (e.g., is, are, was, were, seems, becomes).
Mr. Bello is [a dedicated teacher].
Object Complement (C): Renames or describes the direct object.

They elected him [their chairman].
Object of a Preposition (OP): Follows a preposition (e.g., in, on, at, with, for, about).

She put the book on [the wooden table].

Noun Phrase vs. Noun Clause - A Critical Distinction This is a higher-level grammatical concept crucial for SS2.
Noun Phrase: A group of words without its own subject and verb, functioning as a noun.
I know [the answer].
I know [the correct answer to the question].
Noun Clause: A group of words with its own subject and verb, functioning as a single noun within a larger sentence. It is introduced by words like that, what, why, how, whether, whoever.
I know [what you did last summer]. (Subject: you, Verb: did)
[Why he resigned] remains a mystery. (The entire clause is the Subject of the sentence)
The truth is [that she never arrived]. (The clause is the Subject Complement)

Transcription is the systematic representation of spoken language in written form, using the symbols of the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA). It allows us to record pronunciation accurately, independent of spelling. Word Stress is the emphasis placed on a specific syllable within a word, making it louder, longer, and higher in pitch. Correct stress is often more important for being understood than perfect individual sounds.

/e/ as in bed, pen
/æ/ as in cat, bad
/ɑ:/ as in car, father
/ɒ/ as in hot, clock

SUMMARY WRITING: READING TO SUMMARIZE
INTRODUCTION

This week focuses on the active reading process that must occur before you write a single word of your summary. A high-quality summary is the direct result of meticulous reading, analysis, and extraction of information. The skill lies not in writing quickly, but in reading intelligently.
DETAILED CONTENT: STEPS AND RULES

Step 1: Interrogate the Question.

Before reading the passage, read the summary question with detective-like scrutiny.
Identify the key command: "Summarise the causes," "List the effects," "State the
solutions."
This creates a mental filter. You will now read the passage looking only for causes, effects, or solutions, ignoring everything else.
Step 2: First Reading (For Gist).

Read the passage through once, at a steady pace.
Goal: Understand the general topic, the author's main argument, and the overall structure. What is the passage basically about?
Step 3: Second Reading (For Extraction).

Read the passage again, slowly and actively, with a pen in hand.
Underline or highlight only the sentences or phrases that directly answer the summary question.
Ignore: Examples, illustrations, statistical data (unless central to the main point), rhetorical questions, repetitions, and the author's personal anecdotes.
Create a Skeletal Outline: Make a numbered list or bullet points of the key ideas you have underlined. This is your raw material.
Step 4: The Art of Paraphrasing.

Look at your outline. Your task is now to express these ideas in your own words.
Techniques:
Use synonyms (e.g., important → crucial, significant).
Change the sentence structure (e.g., from active to passive voice: "The storm damaged the crops" → "The crops were damaged by the storm").
Change the word class (e.g., nominalisation: "The company decided to invest" → "The company's decision was to invest").
Original: "The pervasive and unrelenting nature of unemployment among the youth has led to a disturbing level of restiveness and discontent."
Paraphrase: "Widespread youth unemployment is a major cause of social unrest."

Step 5: Writing and Connecting.

Transform your list of paraphrased points into fluent, connected prose.
Use a range of discourse markers or connectives to show the relationship between your points:
Adding: Furthermore, Moreover, In addition.
Contrasting: However, On the other hand, Nevertheless.
Showing cause/effect: Consequently, As a result, Therefore.
Sequencing: Firstly, Subsequently, Finally.
Strictly obey the specified number of sentences or word limit.

COMPREHENSION/LISTENING: LISTENING SKILLS
INTRODUCTION

Listening is the Cinderella skill of language learning—often overlooked in favour of its more glamorous sisters, speaking and writing, yet it is the most frequently used. Crucially, hearing is passive; listening is active. It is a complex process of receiving, constructing meaning from, and responding to spoken language.
DETAILED CONTENT: THE LISTENING PROCESS

Effective listening is a five-stage process:

Hearing: The physiological process of sound waves striking the eardrum and being transmitted to the brain.
Attending: The psychological process of filtering out other sounds and focusing your attention on the specific message.
Understanding: Assigning meaning to the sounds. This involves decoding vocabulary, grammar, and linking the information to your existing knowledge.
Responding: Providing feedback to the speaker. This can be verbal (asking a question) or non-verbal (nodding, maintaining eye contact).
Remembering: Storing the information for later recall. This is the stage where note- taking is crucial.
Barriers to Effective Listening:

Physical Barriers: External noise, a poor phone connection, the speaker talking too fast or too quietly.
Psychological Barriers: Your own biases, prejudices, or emotional state (e.g., anger, anxiety) that distort the message.
Mental Barriers: Internal distractions like daydreaming, boredom, or mentally rehearsing your reply instead of focusing on what is being said (rebuttal tendency).

WRITING: ARGUMENTATIVE ESSAY
INTRODUCTION

An argumentative essay is a formal piece of writing that requires you to investigate a topic, collect and evaluate evidence, and establish a position on the subject in a concise manner. Its primary purpose is persuasion. Unlike an expository essay which is neutral, an argumentative essay is fundamentally opinionated—it takes a firm stand and defends it with logic and evidence.
DETAILED CONTENT: STRUCTURE OF AN ARGUMENTATIVE ESSAY

The structure is similar to an expository essay but with one critical addition: the counter- argument.
1. The Introduction (1 paragraph):

Hook: A compelling opening to grab the reader's interest (a startling statistic, a provocative question, a relevant quote).
Background: Briefly outline the context and the debate surrounding the topic.
Thesis Statement (Your Contention): This is your central argument, your "stance." It must be clear, debatable, and forceful. (e.g., "This essay will argue that the government should impose a sugar tax to combat the national obesity crisis.")
2. The Body (3 paragraphs is a strong model):

Body Paragraph 1: Your First Point.
Topic Sentence: State your first main reason supporting your thesis.
Support: Provide evidence: facts, statistics, examples, logical reasoning. Explain how this evidence supports your point.
Body Paragraph 2: Your Second Point.
Topic Sentence: Introduce your second, stronger reason (use a transition:
Furthermore, Moreover).
Support: Provide new evidence and elaboration.
Body Paragraph 3: The Counter-Argument and Rebuttal.
This is what distinguishes an argumentative essay.
Counter-Argument: Acknowledge the opposing viewpoint. Show the reader you understand the other side of the debate. (e.g., "Opponents of a sugar tax argue that it is a regressive measure that unfairly targets the poor.")
Rebuttal (Your Refutation): Immediately and decisively dismantle the counter-argument. Explain why it is weak, flawed, or less important than your points. (e.g., "However, the long-term health costs of obesity, which disproportionately affect the poor, are far more regressive. The tax revenue could also be used to subsidise healthy food options in low- income areas.")
3. The Conclusion (1 paragraph):

Restate Thesis: Rephrase your main argument in a new, powerful way.
Summarise Main Points: Briefly recap your key arguments and your successful rebuttal.
Clincher / Call to Action: Leave the reader with a strong final impression—a warning, a prediction, or a recommendation for action.
ROBUST WORKED EXAMPLE (ESSAY OUTLINE)
Topic: "Should single-use plastics be banned in Nigeria?"

Thesis Statement (Arguing FOR the ban): "The environmental devastation caused by single-use plastics is so severe that a government ban is not only justified but imperative for the health of our ecosystems and citizens."
Body 1 (Environmental Impact):
Topic Sentence: Firstly, single-use plastics are a primary cause of environmental pollution, clogging our drains, littering our landscapes, and harming wildlife.
Support: Describe scenes of plastic waste in cities and natural areas. Mention animals ingesting or becoming entangled in plastic.
Body 2 (Health and Economic Cost):

Topic Sentence: Moreover, this pollution poses a direct threat to public health and burdens the economy.
Support: Explain how clogged drains lead to flooding and mosquito breeding. Mention the cost of cleaning up plastic waste for municipalities.
Body 3 (Counter-Argument/Rebuttal):

Topic Sentence: Critics of a ban contend that it would lead to job losses in the plastic manufacturing industry and inconvenience consumers.
Rebuttal: However, a phased ban would allow the economy to adapt, creating new jobs in sustainable alternatives like paper, glass, and reusable cloth. The temporary inconvenience is a small price to pay for a cleaner, healthier Nigeria.
Conclusion:

Restate that the environmental and health costs of inaction far outweigh the transitional challenges of a ban.
Summarise the points.
Final thought: "By embracing a ban on single-use plastics, Nigeria can take a decisive step towards a more sustainable and prosperous future."

STRUCTURE: PLURAL FORMS OF NOUNS
INTRODUCTION

The ability to correctly form plurals is a fundamental aspect of English grammar. While the basic rule of adding '-s' or '-es' applies to most nouns, English has retained a significant number of irregular plurals from its Germanic, Latin, and Greek roots. Mastering these forms is essential for grammatical accuracy in both writing and speaking, especially in formal and academic contexts.
DETAILED CONTENT: PLURAL FORMATION RULES

Regular Plurals

Add -s: boy → boys, car → cars, book → books
Add -es (for nouns ending in -s, -ss, -x, -ch, -sh, -z):
bus → buses, box → boxes, church → churches
wish → wishes, quiz → quizzes
Nouns ending in -o:
o Add -es: potato → potatoes, tomato → tomatoes, hero → heroes
Add -s: photo → photos, piano → pianos, kilo → kilos
Nouns ending in -y:
After a consonant: Change y to ies.
baby → babies, city → cities, lady → ladies
After a vowel: Just add -s.
boy → boys, day → days, key → keys
Nouns ending in -f / -fe: Change f/fe to ves.
leaf → leaves, wife → wives, knife → knives, shelf → shelves
Exceptions: chief → chiefs, roof → roofs, belief → beliefs, proof → proofs

Irregular Plurals These must be memorised as they do not follow standard rules.
Vowel Change:
man → men, woman → women, foot → feet
tooth → teeth, goose → geese, mouse → mice
-en Plurals:
child → children, ox → oxen
Zero Plurals (No Change):
species, series, means, crossroads
Animals: sheep, deer, fish (though fishes can refer to multiple species)
Foreign Plurals (Latin & Greek): Common in academic and scientific English.
-us → -i: cactus → cacti, syllabus → syllabi, radius → radii
-a → -ae: formula → formulae, larva → larvae, vertebra → vertebrae
-um → -a: datum → data, medium → media, bacterium → bacteria, curriculum
→ curricula
-is → -es: crisis → crises, analysis → analyses, thesis → theses, oasis → oases
-on → -a: criterion → criteria, phenomenon → phenomena

Compound Nouns The plural is typically formed by pluralising the most significant word (the headword).
Pluralise the main noun:
mother-in-law → mothers-in-law
passer-by → passers-by
court-martial → courts-martial
attorney general → attorneys general
No clear main noun: Add -s at the end.
check-in → check-ins
forget-me-not → forget-me-nots
grown-up → grown-ups

ORAL ENGLISH: EMPHATIC STRESS
INTRODUCTION

Emphatic Stress (also known as contrastive or sentence stress) involves placing special emphasis on a particular word within a sentence to convey a specific meaning, often to highlight a contrast or to correct a misunderstanding. The same sentence can have multiple meanings depending on which word is stressed. This is a key element of spoken communication that conveys nuance and subtext.
DETAILED CONTENT: MEANING AND FOCUS

The word you choose to stress becomes the focal point of the sentence's meaning, usually contrasting with an unstated or previously stated idea.
Neutral Sentence Stress: In a neutral statement, stress typically falls on the last content word (noun, verb, adjective, adverb).
"My brother bought a new CAR." (Neutral statement of fact).
Emphatic Stress: Shifting the stress changes the implied meaning.

"MY brother bought a new car." (Meaning: My brother, not your brother, did it.)
"My BROTHER bought a new car." (Meaning: My brother, not my sister or
father, did it.)
"My brother BOUGHT a new car." (Meaning: He bought it, he didn't steal or
borrow it.)
"My brother bought a NEW car." (Meaning: A new car, not a second-hand one.)
"My brother bought a new CAR." (Meaning: A car, not a motorbike or van.)
ROBUST WORKED EXAMPLE
Implied Question: "Did the tall student answer the easy question?"
Response Sentence: "No, the tall student answered the DIFFICULT question."
The stress must be on DIFFICULT to contrast with the implied "easy."

SUMMARY WRITING: SUMMARIZING ARGUMENTS

DETAILED CONTENT: STEPS
Identify the Contention (Thesis): This is the author's central argument or main claim. What is the one key message they want you to believe? Look for it in the introduction and conclusion.

Synthesise and Paraphrase: Combine the contention, reasons, and rebuttal into a concise summary, using your own words and adhering strictly to the word or sentence limit.

WRITING: REPORT WRITING (SCIENTIFIC REPORT)
INTRODUCTION

A scientific report is a formal document that describes the process, progress, and results of a scientific investigation. Its purpose is to communicate information objectively, clearly, and concisely so that others can understand the experiment and potentially replicate it. The style is formal, objective, and is typically written in the past tense and the passive voice to emphasise the action rather than the person performing it.
DETAILED CONTENT: STRUCTURE
Title: Clear, specific, and descriptive.

Example: "An Experiment to Determine the Effect of Light on the Growth of Bean Seedlings."
Aim/Objective: A single, concise statement of what you set out to discover.

Example: "To investigate whether bean seedlings grow taller in direct sunlight or in complete darkness."
Apparatus/Materials: A list of all equipment and materials used.

Example: "Two identical bean seedlings, two plant pots, soil, water, a measuring ruler, a sunny windowsill, a dark cupboard."
Method/Procedure: A step-by-step, numbered account of exactly what you did, written in the past tense. It should be detailed enough for replication.
Example: "1. Two bean seedlings were planted in identical pots with the same amount of soil. 2. Plant A was placed on a sunny windowsill. 3. Plant B was placed in a dark cupboard. 4. Both plants were watered with 50ml of water daily.
The height of each plant was measured and recorded every 48 hours for two weeks."
Results/Observation: A presentation of the data collected, without interpretation. Use tables, graphs, and written observations.
Example: A table showing "Day," "Height of Plant A (cm)," and "Height of Plant B (cm)."
Discussion/Analysis: The most important section. Here, you interpret the results. What do the results mean? Why did you get these results? Were there any anomalies?
Example: "Plant A, exposed to sunlight, grew tall and green. Plant B, in darkness, grew tall but was yellow and spindly. This suggests that light is necessary for photosynthesis, which produces the green pigment (chlorophyll) and supports healthy, sturdy growth."
Conclusion: A brief summary that directly answers the Aim.

Example: "The experiment showed that while bean seedlings will grow in darkness, they require direct sunlight for healthy development and the production of chlorophyll."

ORAL ENGLISH: REVISION OF CONSONANT SOUNDS (VOICED VS. VOICELESS)
INTRODUCTION Oral English is the bedrock of effective communication. It's not just about the words you say, but how you say them. This week, we return to the very building blocks of English pronunciation: the consonant sounds. A consonant is a speech sound made by partially or completely obstructing the airflow from your lungs. Mastering the distinction between voiced and voiceless consonants is crucial for clear, accurate speech and for understanding the phonetic patterns of the language.
DETAILED CONTENT: THE MECHANICS OF VOICING

The primary classification of consonants is by voicing. This refers to whether your vocal cords vibrate when you produce the sound.
Voiceless Consonants: These are produced with the vocal cords held apart, allowing air to pass through without vibration. They often sound like a puff of air, a hiss, or a whisper.
Voiced Consonants: These are produced when your vocal cords are held close together and vibrate as air passes through. You can feel this vibration if you touch your throat.
How to Test for Voicing: A Practical Demonstration Place your fingertips lightly on your throat, on your larynx (your "Adam's apple").
Say the sound "Ssssss" (like a snake). You should feel no vibration. This is the voiceless sound /s/.
Now, say the sound "Zzzzzz" (like a bee). You should feel a clear vibration or buzz. This is the voiced sound /z/.
The only difference between /s/ and /z/ is the vibration of your vocal cords. This small difference changes the meaning of words, as we see in minimal pairs.
Consonant Pairs (Voiceless vs. Voiced) Many English consonants form pairs where the mouth position is identical; the only difference is voicing.

STRUCTURE: THE POSSESSIVE APOSTROPHE
INTRODUCTION The apostrophe (’) is a small but powerful punctuation mark, often misused. Its two primary functions are to show contraction (e.g., do not → don't) and possession (e.g., the car of my sister → my sister's car). This lesson focuses exclusively on its role in indicating ownership.
DETAILED CONTENT: THE RULES OF POSSESSION

Rule 1: Singular Nouns (not ending in 's')

Add an apostrophe + s ('s).
The cat's tail, the woman's car, a student's pen, Mr Bako's house.
Rule 2: Plural Nouns ending in 's'

Add only an apostrophe (') after the existing 's'.
The cats' tails (the tails belonging to multiple cats).
The students' pens (the pens belonging to multiple students).
The Joneses' house (the house belonging to the Jones family).
Rule 3: Plural Nouns NOT ending in 's'

Treat them like singular nouns: add an apostrophe + s ('s).
The children's toys, the men's room, the sheep's field.
Rule 4: Singular Nouns ending in 's' (e.g., names like James)

Both methods are accepted, but modern British English typically prefers adding 's.
Preferred Method (add 's): James's book, the boss's office, the witness's
testimony.
Older Method (add '): James' book. This is less common now and should be avoided in your exams for consistency.
Rule 5: Compound Nouns

Add the 's to the very last word of the compound.
My mother-in-law's car, the Attorney General's office.
Rule 6: Joint vs. Separate Possession
Joint Possession (one thing shared): Add 's to the last name only.
John and Mary's house (They own one house together).
Separate Possession (individual things): Add 's to both names.
John's and Mary's houses (John has his own house, and Mary has her own house).
THE APOSTROPHE IN CONTRACTIONS (A CRITICAL DISTINCTION) The apostrophe replaces missing letters in a contraction. This is not possession.
it is → it's
you are → you're
they are → they're
who is → who's
do not → don't

The Golden Rule: Its vs. It's This is the most common error in all of English.

it's (with an apostrophe) is only ever a contraction for "it is" or "it has".
Example: It's a very hot day today. (It is a hot day).
its (with no apostrophe) is a possessive pronoun, showing ownership.
Example: The company is famous for its customer service. (The service belongs to it).

COMPREHENSION: READING TO GRASP MAIN POINTS AND TOPIC SENTENCES
INTRODUCTION Reading comprehension transcends merely understanding individual words; it is about deciphering the author's central message. Every well-written paragraph is built around one main point, which is often explicitly stated in a topic sentence.
DETAILED CONTENT

Main Point/Idea: The central, controlling idea of a paragraph or passage. It is the core concept that all other information supports.
Supporting Details: These are the facts, examples, reasons, statistics, and anecdotes that elaborate on, explain, or prove the main point.
Topic Sentence: The sentence within a paragraph that most clearly and concisely states the main idea. While often the first sentence, it can sometimes be found in the middle or at the end of a paragraph.
How to Identify the Main Point/Topic Sentence:

Read the entire paragraph carefully.
Ask yourself: "What is the single most important idea the author wants me to understand from this paragraph?"
Look for a sentence that is broad enough to encompass all other sentences but specific enough to be meaningful.
Test your choice: Do all the other sentences in the paragraph serve to explain, exemplify, or develop this one sentence? If yes, you have likely found the topic sentence.
ROBUST WORKED EXAMPLE

It consolidates memories, processing the day's events and storing important information. Furthermore, during sleep, the brain clears out toxic by-products that accumulate during waking hours. This 'cleaning' process is vital for long-term neurological health. Without adequate sleep, a person's ability to learn, focus, and make decisions is severely impaired."
Analysis:

What is the paragraph about? The active and vital role of sleep for brain function.
Sentence 1: "Sleep is far more important than previously thought." (A good, general introduction).
Sentence 2: "While the body rests, the brain is hard at work." (This is more specific and makes a clear claim).
Sentences 3-6: These sentences provide the evidence for Sentence 2. They explain how the brain is working (memory consolidation, toxin clearance) and the consequences of it not working (impaired function).
Conclusion: The best topic sentence is the second one: "While the body rests, the brain is hard at work." It makes a definitive statement that the rest of the paragraph proves.

STRUCTURE: ESSENTIAL PUNCTUATION MARKS

INTRODUCTION Punctuation marks are the traffic signals of language. They guide the reader through your writing, indicating when to pause, stop, or change direction. Correct punctuation is not a mere formality; it is essential for clarity, meaning, and rhythm. This week, we focus on five key marks that add sophistication to your writing.
DETAILED CONTENT: FUNCTION AND APPLICATION

1. The Question Mark (?)

Use: Placed at the end of a direct question (an interrogative sentence).
Examples: What is your name? Are you leaving already?
Crucial Distinction: Do not use a question mark for indirect questions, which are statements about a question being asked.
Incorrect: He asked me what my name was?
Correct: He asked me what my name was.
2. The Exclamation Mark (!)

Use: Placed at the end of a sentence or phrase to convey strong emotion (surprise, shock, joy, anger) or to give a forceful command.
Examples: What a fantastic goal! Stop! I can't believe we won!
Note on Formality: Use exclamation marks very sparingly in formal writing (essays, reports). Overuse can make your writing seem immature or overly emotional.
3. The Colon (:) - The Introducer

Use 1 (Introducing a List): A colon follows a complete, independent clause to introduce a list, explanation, or example.
Correct: You will need the following ingredients: flour, eggs, milk, and sugar. ("You will need the following ingredients" is a complete sentence).
Incorrect: The ingredients you need are: flour, eggs, milk, and sugar. ("The ingredients you need are" is not a complete sentence).
Use 2 (Introducing an Explanation or Quote): It signals that what follows explains or illustrates what came before.
Explanation: He had one overriding passion: football.
Quote: The headteacher made an announcement: "All students must return their books by Friday."
4. The Semi-Colon (;) - The Connector

Use 1 (Joining Related Independent Clauses): It joins two closely related sentences without a conjunction (like 'and' or 'but'). The ideas must be intimately connected.
Example: The rain fell relentlessly; the streets began to flood.
Use 2 (Separating Complex List Items): Used to separate items in a list when the items themselves contain commas. This prevents confusion.
Example: The conference was attended by delegates from London, England; Paris, France; Tokyo, Japan; and Abuja, Nigeria.
5. The Hyphen (-) - The Linker

Use 1 (Compound Modifiers): Used to join two or more words that act as a single adjective before a noun.
With Hyphen (before noun): He is a well-behaved student. It was a last- minute decision.
Without Hyphen (after noun): The student is well behaved. The decision was made at the last minute.
Use 2 (With Certain Prefixes): Often used with prefixes like ex-, self-, and all-.
Examples: ex-president, self-confidence, all-inclusive
Use 3 (Writing Numbers): Used in fractions and compound numbers from twenty-one to ninety-nine.
Examples: two-thirds, forty-five, sixty-eight

ORAL ENGLISH: INTONATION FOR EXPRESSING SURPRISE AND DISBELIEF

INTRODUCTION Intonation is the melody of speech—the rise and fall of your voice that adds emotional meaning to your words. While we use standard patterns for statements and questions, we can modify these to convey strong emotions like surprise, shock, or disbelief. Mastering these patterns makes your spoken English more expressive and natural.
DETAILED CONTENT: THE PATTERNS OF SURPRISE

The High-Rising Tone (↗) - The "Really?!" Pattern This is the most common and instinctive way to express surprise. You take a statement and turn it into a question of disbelief by making your voice rise sharply at the end.
Standard Statement (Falling Tone): "He passed his \exam." (A simple fact.)
Surprise/Disbelief (High-Rising Tone): "He passed his ↗exam?!" (Meaning: "I can't believe it! Are you serious?")
Other Examples:
"You're moving to /London?" (I had no idea!)
"She sold the /car?" (That was her prized possession!)
The Fall-Rise Tone (V) - The "I Doubt It" Pattern This tone is more complex. The voice starts at a normal pitch, falls, and then rises again on the key word. It expresses doubt, hesitation, or scepticism rather than outright shock.
Example: "He Vpassed his exam." (This implies: "Well, that's what he says, but I'm not sure I believe him.")
ROBUST WORKED EXAMPLES & PRACTICE

Let's use the statement: "The headmaster is dancing."

Neutral Statement (Falling): "The headmaster is \dancing." (I'm informing you.)
Yes/No Question (Rising): "Is the headmaster /dancing?" (I'm asking for confirmation.)
Surprise/Disbelief (High-Rising): "The headmaster is ↗dancing?!" (I'm shocked! The headmaster never dances!)
Pronunciation Practice: Say the word "Really?" in two different ways:

(a) As a simple question: "Rea\lly?" (Falling tone - you just want information).
(b) To express total shock: "↗Really?!" (High-rising tone - your pitch should go much higher).

STRUCTURE: SENTENCE TYPES (BY FUNCTION)

INTRODUCTION We can categorise sentences based on what they are designed to do. Is the speaker informing, commanding, questioning, or exclaiming? Understanding these four functional types helps you to vary your writing and understand the author's intent when reading.
DETAILED CONTENT: THE FOUR FUNCTIONS

1. Declarative Sentence: The Informer

Function: To make a statement, convey information, or express an idea. It is the most common sentence type.
Punctuation: Ends with a full stop (.).
Examples:
"The sun rises in the east." (Fact)
"I enjoy reading historical fiction." (Opinion)
"He asked me where I lived." (This is a statement about a question being asked, so it remains declarative.)
2. Imperative Sentence: The Commander

Function: To give a command, make a request, or offer instruction. The subject (you) is almost always implied rather than stated.
Punctuation: Ends with a full stop (.) or an exclamation mark (!) for strong commands.
Examples:
"Close the door." (Direct command)
"Please take a seat." (Polite request)
"Stop talking!" (Forceful command)
"First, mix the flour and eggs." (Instruction)
3. Interrogative Sentence: The Questioner

Function: To ask a question.
Punctuation: Ends with a question mark (?).
Examples:
"What is the time?" ('Wh-' question)
"Are you feeling better?" (Yes/No question)
"You've finished your homework, haven't you?" (Tag question)
4. Exclamatory Sentence: The Emoter

Function: To express strong emotion or feeling, such as surprise, shock, joy, or anger.
Punctuation: Ends with an exclamation mark (!).
Key Feature: Often begins with "What" or "How" but is not structured as a question.
Examples:
"What a beautiful painting!"
"How incredible that is!"
"I can't believe we won!"

ORAL ENGLISH: THE RISING TONE

INTRODUCTION Having mastered the finality of the Falling Tone, we now explore its counterpart: the Rising Tone (or "Glide-Up"). This pattern conveys incompleteness, uncertainty, and openness. It is the sound of questions, politeness, and tentative suggestions.
DETAILED CONTENT: USES OF THE RISING TONE (/)

The rising tone occurs when your voice starts at a normal pitch and "glides up" to a higher pitch on the nucleus word.
Symbol: A forward-slash (/) placed directly before the nucleus word.

Core Uses of the Rising Tone:

Use 1: Yes/No Questions (The Primary Use)

Function: Used for questions that can be answered with 'yes' or 'no'. It signals that you are awaiting a response.
Examples:
"Are you /coming?"
"Is that your /book?"
"Have you /eaten?"
Use 2: Politeness, Friendliness, and Encouragement

Function: A rising tone can soften a greeting or an offer, making it sound more welcoming.
Examples:
"Good /morning." (Sounds warmer than "Good \morning.")
"Come /in." (More inviting.)
"Can I /help you?"
Use 3: Tentativeness or Uncertainty
Function: Shows that you are not entirely sure of your statement.
Examples:
"I think it’s /blue..." (I'm not completely certain.)
"Perhaps we should /go?" (This is a suggestion, not a command.)
Use 4: Listing Items (Non-Final Items)

Function: When listing things, use a rising tone on all items except the last one, which takes a falling tone to show the list is complete.
Example: "I bought /bread, /milk, /sugar, and \eggs."

WRITING: INTRODUCTION TO CREATIVE WRITING

INTRODUCTION Creative Writing is writing that expresses ideas and thoughts in an imaginative, often symbolic, way. Its primary purpose is to entertain, evoke emotion, and tell a story, rather than simply to inform. The most common form is the short story.
DETAILED CONTENT: THE FIVE ELEMENTS OF A SHORT STORY
A compelling short story is built on five essential pillars:

1. Plot: The sequence of events—the storyline. A strong plot typically follows this arc:

Exposition: The introduction. We meet the characters and learn about the setting.
Rising Action: A conflict is introduced, and the tension builds.
Climax: The turning point of the story—the moment of highest tension and drama.
Falling Action: The events that occur after the climax, leading towards the end.
Resolution: The story's conclusion; the conflict is resolved (for better or worse).
2. Character: The 'people' (or animals, objects, etc.) in the story. The main character is the protagonist; the character or force in conflict with them is the antagonist.
3. Setting: The time and place where the story occurs. A well-described setting uses sensory details (sight, sound, smell, touch) to immerse the reader.
4. Conflict: The central problem that drives the plot. There are several main types:

Character vs. Character: The protagonist against another person.
Character vs. Nature: The protagonist against a force of nature (e.g., a storm, a wild animal).
Character vs. Society: The protagonist against the rules or norms of their society.
Character vs. Self: An internal struggle within the protagonist (e.g., fear, doubt, a moral dilemma).
5. Theme: The central idea, message, or insight about life that the story explores. It is not the plot, but the underlying meaning (e.g., "the importance of family," "the danger of greed").

COMPREHENSION: READING FOR CRITICAL EVALUATION

INTRODUCTION Moving beyond basic understanding, critical evaluation requires you to analyse and judge a text. You are assessing the author's argument, identifying potential bias, and evaluating the strength of the evidence presented. It is about becoming a discerning, rather than a passive, reader.
DETAILED CONTENT: THE CRITICAL READER'S TOOLKIT

To evaluate a text critically, ask these key questions:

1. Author's Purpose: Why was this text written?

To inform? (News article, textbook)
To persuade? (Opinion piece, advertisement, political speech)
To entertain? (Novel, comic strip)
To satirise? (Using humour to criticise)
2. Author's Tone: What is the author's attitude towards the subject?

Is it objective and neutral? (Uses facts, avoids emotion)
Is it subjective and emotional? (Uses loaded language, exclamation marks)
Is it sarcastic, angry, enthusiastic, or dismissive?
3. Author's Bias: Does the author have a preconceived opinion that affects their fairness?

Look for: Loaded language (e.g., "heroic freedom fighters" vs. "ruthless terrorists"), presenting only one side of an argument, omitting key facts, or an overtly emotional appeal.
4. Validity of the Argument: How strong is the reasoning?
Evidence: Is it based on facts, statistics, and examples, or on personal anecdotes and emotions?
Logical Fallacies: Watch for common errors in reasoning, such as:
Ad Hominem: Attacking the person instead of their argument.
Hasty Generalisation: Making a broad claim based on a small sample.
5. Reliability of the Source: Who is the author and where is it published?

Is the author an expert? Is the publication reputable? Is it a personal blog or a peer-reviewed journal?

STRUCTURE: ACTIVE AND PASSIVE VOICE

INTRODUCTION Voice is a grammatical category that describes the relationship between the action expressed by the verb and the participants (subject, object). Understanding and correctly using active and passive voice allows you to control the focus of your sentences and is essential for formal and academic writing.
DETAILED CONTENT: TRANSFORMING VOICE

Active Voice: The subject of the sentence performs the action. This is the most common, direct, and vigorous sentence structure.
Structure: [Subject] + [Verb] + [Object]
Example: "The dog [subject] chased [verb] the cat [object]."
Passive Voice: The subject of the sentence is acted upon. The focus is on the action or the recipient of the action, not the doer.
Structure: [Subject] + [a form of 'to be' + Past Participle] + [by + Agent] (The 'by' phrase is often omitted).
Example: "The cat [subject] was chased [verb: was + past participle of chase] by the dog [agent]."
Rules for Converting Active to Passive Voice:

The object of the active sentence becomes the subject of the passive sentence.
The main verb is changed to its past participle form and is preceded by the appropriate form of the verb 'to be' (am, is, are, was, were, be, being, been).
The subject of the active sentence becomes the agent, introduced by the preposition 'by', or is omitted if it is unknown or unimportant.
Tense-by-Tense Conversion Table:

Passive Sentence: "The meal is being prepared by the chef."

GRAMMAR: REVISION OF TENSES

INTRODUCTION Welcome to the Third Term. A solid grasp of tenses is the bedrock of effective communication in English. Tense does more than just indicate time; it reveals the relationship between actions, their duration, and their completeness. Mastering this allows you to convey your thoughts with precision and clarity.
DETAILED CONTENT: A COMPREHENSIVE OVERVIEW OF ENGLISH TENSES Tenses can be understood through two lenses: Time (Past, Present, Future) and Aspect (Simple, Continuous, Perfect, Perfect Continuous). The combination of these gives us our 12 primary tenses.
THE PRESENT TENSES

Simple Present:

Form: Base verb (add -s or -es for third person singular: he, she, it).
Core Uses & Examples:
Habits & Routines: "She walks to school every day."
General Truths & Facts: "Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius."
Fixed Future Schedules (Timetables): "The film starts at 9 PM."
Stative Verbs (feelings, thoughts, possession): "I believe you. He owns a car."
Present Continuous (Progressive):

Form: am/is/are + present participle (verb-ing).
Core Uses & Examples:
Actions Happening Now: "Be quiet, please. I am studying."
Temporary Situations: "He is staying with his cousin for a few weeks."
Definite Future Plans: "We are travelling to Lagos tomorrow."
Expressing Annoyance (with 'always'): "You are always interrupting
me!"
Present Perfect:

Form: has/have + past participle.
Core Uses & Examples:
Past Action with a Present Result: "I have lost my wallet." (The result: I don't have it now).
Life Experience (time not specified): "She has visited Ghana three times."
Unfinished Action (started in the past, continues now): "He has lived in London for ten years." / "She has worked here since 2020."
Present Perfect Continuous:

Form: has/have + been + present participle (verb-ing).
Core Uses & Examples:
To Emphasise Duration: Focuses on the ongoing nature of an action that started in the past and may or may not be finished. "She has been painting the room all morning." (She is probably covered in paint and the room is not finished).
Recent, Temporary Actions: "You look tired." "I have been running."

THE PAST TENSES

Simple Past:

Form: Regular: verb + -ed. Irregular: various forms (e.g., ate, went, saw).
Core Uses & Examples:
Completed Action at a Definite Time in the Past: "I finished my homework last night."
A Series of Completed Actions: "I woke up, brushed my teeth, and went
to work."
Past Continuous:

Form: was/were + present participle (verb-ing).
Core Uses & Examples:
Action in Progress at a Specific Past Moment: "At 8 PM yesterday, I
was watching a film."
Interrupted Action: "I was sleeping when the phone rang."
Two Simultaneous, Ongoing Actions: "While I was cooking, he was cleaning."
Past Perfect:

Form: had + past participle.
Core Uses & Examples:
The 'Past-in-the-Past': Describes an action that occurred before another action in the past. "When we arrived at the cinema, the film had already started." (First: film started. Second: we arrived).
Past Perfect Continuous:

Form: had + been + present participle (verb-ing).
Core Uses & Examples:
Duration Before Another Past Action: Emphasises how long an action had been happening before a second action interrupted it. "He had been waiting for an hour before she finally arrived."
THE FUTURE TENSES

Simple Future:

Form: will + base verb. (shall is sometimes used with I and we for formal offers).
Core Uses & Examples:
Predictions: "I think it will rain later."
Spontaneous Decisions: "The phone is ringing. I will get it."
Promises/Offers: "I will help you with that heavy bag."
Future Continuous:

Form: will be + present participle (verb-ing).
Core Uses & Examples:
Action in Progress at a Specific Future Time: "This time next Friday, I
will be flying to Abuja."
Asking Politely About Someone's Plans: "Will you be using the car tonight?"
Future Perfect:

Form: will have + past participle.
Core Uses & Examples:
Action Completed Before a Specific Future Time: "By 2030, I will have graduated from university and started my career."
ROBUST WORKED EXAMPLES & ILLUSTRATIONS

Illustration: Present Perfect vs. Simple Past Imagine two people looking at an empty parking space.
Person A: "Where is your car?"
Person B: "I sold it." (Simple Past: The action is complete and finished in the past. We might ask, "When did you sell it?")

GRAMMAR: SENTENCES (CONDITIONAL & COMPLEX)

INTRODUCTION This week, we delve into the logic and structure of Conditional Clauses and Complex Sentences. Conditional sentences allow us to speculate, negotiate, and imagine possibilities by stating a condition and its consequence. Mastering them is essential for expressing everything from scientific facts to heartfelt regrets. Understanding complex sentences, in turn, is key to writing with sophistication and clarity.
DETAILED CONTENT: CONDITIONAL CLAUSES Conditional sentences typically have two parts: the if-clause (the condition) and the main clause (the result). There are three primary types, plus a 'zero' conditional for completeness.
TYPE 0: GENERAL TRUTHS

Function: To express scientific facts, general truths, or habitual results that are always true.
Structure: If + Simple Present, ... Simple Present
Examples:
"If you heat ice, it melts."
"If my father has a day off, he goes fishing."
TYPE 1: LIKELY / PROBABLE CONDITION

Function: To talk about a real and possible situation in the present or future.
Structure:
IF-Clause: Simple Present Tense
MAIN-Clause: Simple Future Tense (will + base verb)
Formula: If + Simple Present, ... will + base verb
Examples:
"If it rains tomorrow, the match will be cancelled."
"If you study hard, you will pass the exam."
TYPE 2: UNLIKELY / HYPOTHETICAL CONDITION

Function: To talk about an unreal, improbable, or imaginary situation in the present or future. It often refers to a situation that is contrary to known facts.
Structure:
IF-Clause: Simple Past Tense
MAIN-Clause: Conditional (would + base verb)
Formula: If + Simple Past, ... would + base verb
Examples:
"If I won the lottery, I would buy a private jet." (The speaker does not expect to win).
"If I were you, I would apologise." (I am not you, but this is my advice).
⚠ Important Note: In formal, correct British English, we use "were" for all subjects (I, he, she, it) in the if-clause of Type 2 conditionals. This is called the subjunctive mood. While "If I was..." is common in informal speech, "If I were..." is the grammatically preferred form.
TYPE 3: IMPOSSIBLE / PAST CONDITION

Function: To talk about an unreal situation in the past; it often expresses regret or criticism about something that did not happen. The condition can no longer be fulfilled.
Structure:
IF-Clause: Past Perfect Tense (had + past participle)
MAIN-Clause: Perfect Conditional (would have + past participle)
Formula: If + Past Perfect, ... would have + past participle
Examples:
"If I had studied harder, I would have passed the examination." (But I didn't study hard, and I failed).
"If she had taken the earlier train, she would not have been late." (But she didn't take it, so she was late).
COMPLEX SENTENCES A conditional sentence is a specific type of Complex Sentence.

A Complex Sentence contains one Main (Independent) Clause and one or more
Subordinate (Dependent) Clauses.
A Main Clause can stand alone as a complete sentence because it expresses a full thought.
A Subordinate Clause cannot stand alone; it depends on the main clause to complete its meaning. It often begins with a subordinating conjunction like if, when, because, although, who, which, that.
Example Analysis:
"The match will be cancelled [Main Clause] + if it rains tomorrow [Subordinate Clause]."
"The student [Main Clause Subject] who won the prize [Subordinate Clause describing 'student'] was very happy [Main Clause Verb]."
ROBUST WORKED EXAMPLES & ILLUSTRATIONS

Illustration: The Logic of Conditionals

Type 1 (Real): "If I have enough money, I will buy a car." (This is a real future plan. I might have the money).
Type 2 (Unreal): "If I had enough money, I would buy a car." (This is a dream or wish. I currently do not have the money).
Type 3 (Past Unreal): "If I had had enough money, I would have bought a car." (This is a past regret. I didn't have the money in the past, so I didn't buy it).
Example 1 (Identifying the Type):

"If he spoke French, he would get the job in Paris."
Answer: Type 2. The use of spoke (Simple Past) and would get indicates a hypothetical present situation—he does not speak French.

"If the team	(train) harder, they	(win) the match last week."
Answer: "If the team had trained harder, they would have won the match last week." (Past regret).

WRITING: SPEECH WRITING

INTRODUCTION A speech is a formal address delivered to an audience. Its power lies in its orality; it is written to be heard, not read. This fundamental distinction shapes every aspect of its composition, from its structure to its language. A great speech can inspire, persuade, inform, and entertain.
DETAILED CONTENT: FEATURES AND TECHNIQUES OF A SPEECH

Title: Should be clear, relevant, and impactful. (e.g., "THE PERIL OF PLASTIC: A CALL TO ACTION").
Salutation (Greeting): A formal address that acknowledges and respects the audience hierarchy.
Example: "The Honourable Minister of Education, the Principal of this great institution, members of the Board of Governors, dedicated members of staff, proud parents, and my fellow students."
Introduction:
Hook the Audience: Start with a powerful quote, a surprising statistic, a rhetorical question, or a brief, relevant story.
State the Occasion: Clearly state why you are all gathered.
Present Your Thesis: Briefly state your central message or argument.
Body:
Structured Points: Organise your main ideas into 2-4 clear paragraphs.
Rhetorical Devices: Use these to engage the listener's ear and emotions:
Rhetorical Questions: "How many of you wish to see a cleaner environment?"
Repetition (Anaphora): "We must act. We must lead. We must change."
The Rule of Three: "It is a matter of honour, duty, and legacy."
Personal Anecdotes: A short, personal story makes your speech relatable.
Direct Address: Use "we," "you," and "our" to create inclusivity.
Conclusion:
Summarise: Briefly recap your main points.
Closing Thought: End with a powerful, memorable statement, a call to action, or a note of hope.
Formal Thanks: Thank the audience for their time and attention.

INTRODUCTION This register covers the entire journey of a written work, from the author's mind to the reader's hands. Understanding these terms is crucial for anyone interested in literature, journalism, or academia.

Manuscript: The original, author-prepared text, whether handwritten or typed, submitted to a publisher.

WRITING: SUMMARY (SUMMARIZING A TALK OR LECTURE)

INTRODUCTION Summarizing a live talk or lecture is a dynamic and challenging skill. Unlike a written text, you cannot re-read a speaker's words. This skill hinges on active listening and efficient note-taking, forcing you to distinguish immediately between essential ideas and supporting details.
DETAILED CONTENT: THE THREE-STEP PROCESS Step 1: Before the Lecture (Preparation)
Context is Key: If possible, find out the topic in advance. A little background knowledge makes it easier to follow and identify key terms.
Tools of the Trade: Have a dedicated notebook and pen. Avoid distractions.

Step 2: During the Lecture (Active Listening & Note-Taking)

Don't Transcribe! Your goal is to capture ideas, not every word.
Listen for Signpost Language: Speakers use verbal cues to signal important information.
Introducing the Main Idea: "My central argument is...", "The key point here is..."
Enumerating Points: "There are three primary reasons...", "First... Second... Furthermore... Finally..."
Giving Examples: "For instance...", "To illustrate...", "A case in point..." (Note the point being illustrated, not necessarily the example itself).
Emphasising: "It is crucial to remember that...", "Most importantly..."
Summarising/Concluding: "In conclusion...", "To sum up...", "Therefore..."
Develop a Personal Shorthand: Use abbreviations and symbols to write faster.
& or + = and
w/ = with
w/o = without
→ = leads to, results in
e.g. = for example
cf. = compared to
govt = government
devt = development
Focus on the Skeleton, Not the Flesh: Capture the thesis and main supporting arguments. Ignore jokes, lengthy anecdotes, and repetitive statements.
Step 3: After the Lecture (Writing the Summary)

Review Immediately: While the memory is fresh, read through your notes. Fill in any gaps and rewrite illegible words.
Identify the Core: What was the single most important message (the thesis)?
List Key Points: What were the 2-4 main ideas that supported the thesis?
Draft and Paraphrase: Write a single paragraph in your own words that combines the thesis and key points. Do not add your own opinion.
Edit for Brevity and Clarity: Ensure your summary is concise and flows logically.

VOCABULARY DEVELOPMENT: WORDS ASSOCIATED WITH INDUSTRIES

INTRODUCTION The language of industry is the language of production, from raw material to finished product. This register is vital for understanding economics, business, technology, and geography.
DETAILED CONTENT: FROM MINE TO MARKET - AN INDUSTRIAL LEXICON

Industry: The sector of an economy concerned with the production of goods and services. It can be classified as primary (extracting raw materials), secondary (manufacturing), and tertiary (services).
Raw Materials: The basic substances in their natural, unprocessed state, used as inputs for industrial production.
Examples: Crude oil, iron ore, bauxite, timber, cotton, rubber.
Manufacturing: The process of converting raw materials into finished goods on a large scale, typically in a factory.
Automation: The use of technology, control systems, and machinery (like robots) to operate processes with minimal human intervention. This increases efficiency and consistency.
Assembly Line: A manufacturing process in which parts are added to a product in a sequential manner to create a finished product much faster than with handcrafting. Pioneered by Henry Ford.
Production: The overall action of making or manufacturing from components or raw materials.
Productivity: A measure of the efficiency of production. It is the ratio of output (goods produced) to inputs (labour, materials, capital) used.
Logistics / Supply Chain: The detailed organisation and implementation of a complex operation involving the flow of goods from the point of origin (supplier) to the point of consumption (customer).
Heavy Industry: Industries that involve large-scale capital investment and typically produce large, heavy products (e.g., shipbuilding, steel production, heavy machinery).
Light Industry: Industries that are less capital-intensive and produce smaller consumer goods (e.g., textiles, food processing, electronics assembly).
Cottage Industry: A small-scale, decentralised manufacturing business often operated from a home rather than a purpose-built facility.

GRAMMAR: VERB FORMS (ACTIVE AND PASSIVE VOICE)

INTRODUCTION Voice is a grammatical category that defines the relationship between the subject of a verb and the action it expresses. Using the correct voice is not just a matter of grammar; it's a matter of style and emphasis. It determines what or who is the focus of your sentence.
Active Voice: The subject of the sentence is the doer of the action. It is direct, strong, and clear. (e.g., The chef cooked the meal).
Passive Voice: The subject of the sentence is the receiver of the action. It is used when the doer is unknown, unimportant, or when you want to emphasise the action or the recipient. (e.g., The meal was cooked by the chef).
DETAILED CONTENT: FORMING THE PASSIVE VOICE

The transformation from active to passive follows a logical sequence. The key is to use the correct form of the verb to be + the past participle of the main verb.
The Four-Step Transformation:

Identify the Subject (S), Verb (V), and Object (O) in the active sentence.
[The company (S)] [built (V)] [the house (O)].
Make the Object the new Subject.
[The house]...
Change the Verb to the form of [TO BE] + [PAST PARTICIPLE]. The tense must remain the same as in the active sentence.
built (Simple Past) → was built (was = past simple of 'to be'; built = past participle of 'build')
[The house] was built...
Make the original Subject the Agent, introduced by the preposition "by". (This is optional and often omitted if the agent is obvious or unimportant).
[The house] was built [by the company].

ORAL: STRESS PLACEMENT ON TWO-SYLLABLE WORDS

INTRODUCTION In English, we do not pronounce every syllable with the same force. Stress is the emphasis we place on a particular syllable in a word, making it louder, longer, and higher in pitch. Correct stress is not a minor detail; it is crucial for intelligibility. Misplacing stress can make a word sound unnatural or even change its meaning, especially in two-syllable words where stress often distinguishes between nouns/verbs and adjectives.
DETAILED CONTENT: THE NOUN/VERB STRESS RULE

The placement of stress in two-syllable words often follows a predictable pattern based on the word's grammatical function.
Rule 1: Nouns and Adjectives

The vast majority of two-syllable nouns and adjectives are stressed on the
FIRST syllable.
Examples:
're-cord (n.) - a disc or a documented account.
'pre-sent (n.) - a gift.
'con-flict (n.) - a serious disagreement.
'hap-py (adj.) - feeling pleasure.
'cle-ver (adj.) - quick to understand.
Rule 2: Verbs

The vast majority of two-syllable verbs are stressed on the SECOND syllable.
Examples:
re-'cord (v.) - to set down in writing or to capture sound.
pre-'sent (v.) - to give or show.
con-'flict (v.) - to be incompatible or clashing.
de-'cide (v.) - to make a choice.
be-'gin (v.) - to start.
Rule 3: Prepositions/Other

Most two-syllable prepositions are also stressed on the second syllable.
Examples:
be-'tween
a-'bove
a-'cross

COMPREHENSION: PARAPHRASING POEMS AND DRAMATIC WORKS

INTRODUCTION To paraphrase is to restate a text or passage in your own words. When applied to poetry and older dramatic works (like those of Shakespeare), paraphrasing is an essential tool for unlocking meaning. These texts often use archaic language, complex syntax, and dense figurative language that can be a barrier to understanding. Paraphrasing helps you translate this complexity into clear, modern prose.
DETAILED CONTENT: THE TECHNIQUE OF PARAPHRASING Step 1: Read for Overall Sense
Read the entire stanza or speech first to get a general sense of the topic and mood.

Step 2: Decode Line by Line

Work through the text line by line or clause by clause.
Identify and "Translate" Archaic Language:
Thou / Thee / Thy / Thine → You / Your / Yours
Art → are
Hast → have
Dost → do
Wherefore → why (This is a classic: "Wherefore art thou Romeo?" means "Why are you Romeo?" not "Where are you?").
Hath → has
Untangle Figurative Language:
Metaphor: "All the world's a stage." → Paraphrase: "Life is like a performance in a theatre."
Simile: "My love is like a red, red rose." → Paraphrase: "He is saying his love is beautiful and delicate, like a rose."
Personification: "The wind whispered through the trees." → Paraphrase: "The wind blew softly through the trees, making a whispering sound."
Step 3: Re-state in Prose

Write out the literal meaning in simple, direct sentences. Ignore the original rhyme and rhythm.
Identifying Literary Elements:

Theme: The central, universal idea or message (e.g., love, power, betrayal, the passage of time).
Mood: The atmosphere or feeling created for the reader/audience (e.g., melancholic, joyful, tense).
Imagery: Language that appeals to the senses (sight, sound, touch, taste, smell).
Dramatic Features: Dialogue (conversation between characters), Monologue (a long speech by one character), Stage Directions (instructions in the script for movement, setting, and tone).

GRAMMAR: RULES OF CONCORD (SUBJECT-VERB AGREEMENT)

INTRODUCTION Concord (or Subject-Verb Agreement) is the cornerstone of grammatical sentence construction. It is the rule that the subject of a sentence must agree with its verb in number.
A singular subject takes a singular verb.
A plural subject takes a plural verb. While the basic rule is simple, several specific situations can cause confusion.
DETAILED CONTENT: KEY RULES OF CONCORD

Basic Singular & Plural:

The boy runs. (Singular subject, verb with '-s')
The boys run. (Plural subject, verb without '-s')
Compound Subjects with "and":

Usually treated as plural.
Tola and Bola are here.
Exception: When the two nouns refer to a single entity.
Rice and beans is my favourite meal. (Seen as one dish).
Compound Subjects with "or" / "nor" (Disjunctive):

The verb agrees with the subject closest to it (Proximity Rule).
Neither the teacher nor the students are late.
Neither the students nor the teacher is late.
Indefinite Pronouns:

The following are always singular: each, every, either, neither, one, no one, everyone, everyone, someone, anyone, nobody, everybody, somebody, anybody, nothing, everything, something, anything.
Everyone has a pen.
Somebody was here.
Collective Nouns:

Words like team, committee, family, class, government, staff.
Singular: When the group acts as a single unit.
The team is winning.
Plural: When the focus is on the individual members acting separately (more common in British English).
The committee are arguing amongst themselves.
"There is/are" and "Here is/are":

The verb agrees with the subject that follows it.
There is a book on the table. (Singular)
There are many books on the table. (Plural)
Intervening Phrases:

Phrases like as well as, along with, together with, in addition to do not change the number of the subject.
The manager, as well as the assistants, is responsible. (Subject is "manager").
The assistants, as well as the manager, are responsible. (Subject is "assistants").
Titles, Names, and Quantities:

The titles of books, movies, and countries are singular even if they contain plural words.
"The Brothers Karamazov" is a long novel.
Ten thousand naira is a lot of money. (Seen as a single amount).

ORAL: VOWEL SOUNDS (/ɪ/ vs /iː/, /ʊ/ vs /uː/, /æ/ vs /ɑː/)

INTRODUCTION Vowel sounds are the building blocks of spoken English, produced with an open vocal tract. A crucial distinction in English pronunciation is between short and long vowels. The difference is not just duration but also the position of the tongue and the shape of the mouth. Mispronouncing these can lead to misunderstandings, as many form minimal pairs— words that differ only by one sound.
DETAILED CONTENT: VOWEL CONTRASTS AND MINIMAL PAIRS

1. /ɪ/ (short) vs. /iː/ (long)

/ɪ/ as in 'sit': A short, lax vowel. The tongue is relaxed and positioned in the centre-front of the mouth.
Examples: ship, slip, bin, hit, grin, sin
/iː/ as in 'seat': A long, tense vowel. The tongue is high and at the front of the mouth, with the lips slightly spread.
Examples: sheep, sleep, bean, heat, green, scene
Minimal Pair Drill:
"Don't slip on the deck of the ship." / "The sheep are sleeping."
"I feel fit." / "I have sore feet."
"Please sit here." / "This is a comfortable seat."
2. /ʊ/ (short) vs. /uː/ (long)

/ʊ/ as in 'pull': A short, lax vowel. The tongue is high and towards the back, but relaxed. Lips are slightly rounded.
Examples: pull, full, could, wood, book, look
/uː/ as in 'pool': A long, tense vowel. The tongue is high and at the back of the mouth, with the lips tightly rounded.
Examples: pool, fool, cool, shoe, blue, food
Minimal Pair Drill:
"The pull of the current was strong." / "Let's swim in the pool."
"The room was full." / "Don't be a fool."
"Look at that!" / "His name is Luke."
3. /æ/ (short) vs. /ɑː/ (long)

/æ/ as in 'cat': A short, open vowel. The jaw is open, and the tongue is low and towards the front.
Examples: man, cat, bat, hat, pack, sad
/ɑː/ as in 'cart': A long, open vowel. The jaw is open, and the tongue is low and pulled back.
Examples: mark, cart, barn, heart, park, calm
Minimal Pair Drill:
"The cat sat on the mat." / "We put the shopping in the cart."
"He was wearing a hat." / "She has a kind heart."
"We need to pack our bags." / "The soldiers began their march."

STRUCTURE: ADJUNCTS, CONJUNCTS, DISJUNCTS

INTRODUCTION Adverbials are words or phrases that provide extra information about a verb, an adjective, or another adverb. However, not all adverbials are the same. We can classify them into three types based on their function and relationship to the core sentence: Adjuncts, Conjuncts, and Disjuncts.
DETAILED CONTENT: CLASSIFYING ADVERBIALS

1. ADJUNCTS (The "Standard" Adverbials)

Function: They are integrated into the core structure of the clause. They modify the verb and provide details about the action itself.
They answer: How? When? Where? How often? Why?
Removability: If you remove an adjunct, the sentence remains grammatically complete but loses specific detail.
Examples:
Manner: He ran quickly. She spoke with confidence.
Place: She is sitting in the garden.
Time: We will leave tomorrow.
Frequency: He often visits his grandparents.
Reason: He left because he was tired.
2. CONJUNCTS (Linking Adverbs)

Function: They are not part of the core sentence meaning. They act as logical connectors between sentences or clauses, showing the relationship between ideas.
They indicate: Addition, contrast, sequence, result.
Punctuation: They are often (but not always) set off by a comma.
Examples:
Addition: Furthermore, all the money was stolen. He is rich; moreover, he is generous.
Contrast: However, he did not agree.
Result: Therefore, the project was cancelled.
Sequence: Meanwhile, the team was losing. First, boil the water. Next, add the pasta.
3. DISJUNCTS (Sentence Adverbs or Comment Adverbs)

Function: They stand outside the core sentence and express the speaker's attitude, judgment, or evaluation of the entire clause. They comment on the content or style of the sentence.
They answer: What is the speaker's view on this statement?
Punctuation: They are usually set off by a comma.
Examples:
Honesty: Frankly, I don't care. Honestly, that was a terrible idea.
Judgment: Obviously, he is lying. Clearly, more research is needed.
Evaluation: Luckily, no one was hurt. Unfortunately, the bus had already left.
Style: Briefly, the plan is to leave at dawn.

COMPREHENSION: READING FOR STRUCTURAL PATTERNS

INTRODUCTION Advanced reading involves not just understanding content but also recognising the author's craft. Identifying sentence structures helps you understand how ideas are linked, which in turn aids comprehension and improves your own writing style. The three basic sentence structures are Simple, Compound, and Complex.
DETAILED CONTENT: IDENTIFYING SENTENCE TYPES

1. Simple Sentence

Definition: Contains one independent clause (a subject and a verb that expresses a complete thought).
It can have: A compound subject or a compound verb, but only one main clause.
Examples:
"The dog barked."
"Tola and Bola studied for the exam and passed." (Compound subject & compound verb, but one clause).
2. Compound Sentence

Definition: Contains two or more independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS: For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So) or a semicolon (;).
Shows: A relationship between two equally important ideas.
Examples:
"The dog barked, and the cat ran away."
"I wanted to go to the party, but I was too tired."
"She is intelligent; she is also very hardworking."
3. Complex Sentence
Definition: Contains one independent clause and at least one dependent (subordinate) clause. A dependent clause cannot stand alone as a sentence.
Shows: A relationship where one idea is dependent on another.
Dependent clauses often start with subordinating conjunctions: because, since, although, when, if, after, before, which, that, who.
Examples:
"The dog barked when the postman arrived." (Independent: The dog barked. Dependent: when the postman arrived).
"Although it was raining, we went for a walk."
"This is the book that I recommended."

STRUCTURE: PREPOSITIONAL PHRASES

INTRODUCTION Prepositions are the words that show relationships - in time, space, or logic - between other words in a sentence. A prepositional phrase is a powerful grammatical unit that begins with a preposition and ends with a noun or pronoun (the object of the preposition). These phrases function as unified modifiers, adding crucial detail and complexity to your writing.
DETAILED CONTENT: FUNCTIONS OF PREPOSITIONAL PHRASES

A prepositional phrase can act as either an adjective or an adverb. The key to identifying its function is to ask the right question.
1. As an Adjective (An Adjectival Phrase):

Function: Modifies a noun or pronoun by answering "Which one?" or "What kind?"
Position: Almost always appears directly after the noun it modifies.
Examples:
"The house on the hill has a beautiful view." (Which house? The one on the hill).
"I appreciate a person with a sense of humour." (What kind of person? One with a sense of humour).
"The suggestion from the committee was accepted." (Which suggestion? The one from the committee).
2. As an Adverb (An Adverbial Phrase):

Function: Modifies a verb, an adjective, or another adverb by answering "Where?", "When?", "How?", or "Why?"
Position: More flexible; can appear at the beginning, middle, or end of a sentence.
Examples:
Where? "We will meet at the restaurant." (Modifies the verb 'will meet').
When? "The concert starts in the evening." (Modifies the verb 'starts').
How? "She completed the task with incredible speed." (Modifies the verb 'completed').
Why? "He went to the market for some bread." (Modifies the verb 'went').

ROBUST WORKED EXAMPLES & ILLUSTRATIONS

Illustration: Multiple Phrases in One Sentence

"The book on the shelf in the library is by my favourite author."
"on the shelf" → Adjective phrase modifying "book" (Which book?)
"in the library" → Adjective phrase modifying "shelf" (Which shelf?)
"by my favourite author" → Adjective phrase modifying "book" (What kind of book?)
Example 1 (Distinguishing Function):

"The dog slept under the table."
Question: Where did the dog sleep?
Answer: Under the table.
Function: Adverbial phrase (modifies the verb 'slept').
Example 2 (Distinguishing Function):

"The dog under the table is sleeping."
Question: Which dog is sleeping?
Answer: The one under the table.
Function: Adjectival phrase (modifies the noun 'dog').

COMPREHENSION: READING FOR CRITICAL EVALUATION

INTRODUCTION Critical evaluation moves beyond simply understanding what a text says; it involves assessing its quality, validity, and persuasiveness. A critical reader acts as a judge, questioning the author's arguments, evidence, and motives. This skill is vital for distinguishing fact from opinion, detecting bias, and forming your own well-reasoned conclusions.
DETAILED CONTENT: BECOMING A CRITICAL READER

1. Identifying Faulty Reasoning (Logical Fallacies):

Hasty Generalisation: Drawing a conclusion based on insufficient evidence.
Example: "I met two rude people from that city. Everyone from that city must be rude."
Ad Hominem (Attack on the Person): Attacking the character of the person making the argument instead of the argument itself.
Example: "We shouldn't listen to his views on climate change because he failed his science exams in school."
False Cause (Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc): Assuming that because one event happened after another, the first event caused the second.
Example: "The new mayor was elected, and then crime rates increased. Therefore, the new mayor caused the crime wave."
Bandwagon Appeal: Arguing that something is true or correct because it is popular.
Example: "Everyone is buying this brand of phone, so it must be the best."
2. Detecting Bias:

Loaded Language: Using words with strong positive or negative connotations to influence the reader emotionally.
Example: "The government's reckless scheme" vs. "The government's bold new scheme."
Omission of Relevant Information: Leaving out facts that would contradict the author's argument.
Presenting Opinion as Fact: Stating subjective beliefs as if they are objective truths without evidence.
3. Assessing Evidence:

Is the evidence relevant? Does it directly support the claim?
Is the evidence sufficient? Is there enough to make the argument convincing?
Is the evidence from a credible source? Is the source expert, unbiased, and reliable?

DETAILED CONTENT: A GLOSSARY OF GOVERNANCE

The Three Arms of Government:
Legislature: The law-making arm (e.g., National Assembly, Parliament).
Executive: The law-implementing arm (e.g., President, Governor, Ministers).
Judiciary: The law-interpreting arm (e.g., Courts, Judges).
Democracy: A system of government by the whole population, typically through elected representatives.
Autocracy/Dictatorship: A system of government by one person with absolute power.
Bill: A proposed law presented to a legislature for debate.
Act / Statute: A bill that has been passed by the legislature and become law.
Veto: The constitutional right to reject a decision or proposal made by a law-making body.
Impeachment: The process of charging a public official with misconduct, potentially leading to their removal from office.
Electorate: All the people in a country or area who are entitled to vote in an election.
Franchise / Suffrage: The right to vote in public elections.
Gerrymandering: The manipulation of electoral constituency boundaries to favour one party or class.
Bicameral Legislature: A legislature with two chambers or houses (e.g., Senate and House of Representatives).
Coalition: A temporary alliance of political parties forming a government or of states.
Ideology: A system of ideas and ideals, especially one which forms the basis of economic or political theory and policy (e.g., socialism, conservatism).
