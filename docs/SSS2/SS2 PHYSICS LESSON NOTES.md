WEEK 3 - SOUND WAVES
MEANING OF SOUND AND SOUND WAVES
Sound is a form of energy produced by vibrating objects and transmitted through a medium (solid, liquid, or gas) in the form of longitudinal mechanical waves.
Sound Waves are longitudinal waves that propagate due to the compression and rarefaction of particles in a medium.
Characteristics:
They require a material medium to propagate (i.e., cannot travel in a vacuum).
They are mechanical and longitudinal in nature.
Sound waves move faster in solids than in liquids and gases.

PRODUCTION OF SOUND
Sound is produced by vibrating bodies, such as vocal cords, strings of musical instruments, and loudspeakers.
The vibration causes the surrounding air particles to vibrate, producing compressions and rarefactions.

PROPAGATION OF SOUND
Sound travels by the successive compressions and rarefactions of particles in a medium.
Compression: Region of high pressure where particles are close together.
Rarefaction: Region of low pressure where particles are far apart.
Resonance: This is the effect felt/observed when a body vibrating with its own natural frequency transfers its frequency to the bodies around it, such that they all vibrate with the same frequency.

CHARACTERISTICS OF SOUND WAVES
Frequency (f): Number of vibrations per second (Hz).
Amplitude (A): Maximum displacement of the wave from equilibrium – determines loudness.
Wavelength (λ): Distance between two successive compressions or rarefactions.
Speed (v): Rate at which sound travels through a medium.
V = fλ

RANGE OF HEARING
Infrasonic: Below 20 Hz (e.g., earthquakes).
Audible: 20 Hz – 20,000 Hz (Human range).
Ultrasonic: Above 20,000 Hz (e.g., sonar, medical ultrasound).

BEHAVIOUR OF SOUND WAVES
Reflection of Sound:
Produces echoes when sound reflects off a hard surface.
Echo time gap must be ≥ 0.1 sec to be heard separately.
Refraction of Sound:
Occurs when sound passes through different layers (e.g., warm to cold air).
Changes direction due to change in speed.
Diffraction of Sound:
Bending of sound waves around obstacles or through openings.
Explains why we can hear sounds around corners.
Interference of Sound:
Occurs when two waves superimpose to form a resultant wave.
Leads to loud (constructive) or soft (destructive) sounds.

APPLICATIONS OF SOUND WAVES
Sonar (Sound Navigation and Ranging):
Used in ships/submarines to detect underwater objects using ultrasonic waves.
Ultrasound in Medicine:
Imaging of internal organs (e.g., foetus in the womb).
Industrial Non-Destructive Testing:
Detecting cracks or flaws in materials.
Sound Communication:
Speech, telecommunication, public address systems.

IMAGE FORMATION IN THE HUMAN EYE
Light from an object enters the eye through the cornea, passes through the aqueous humor, pupil, lens, and vitreous humor, then falls on the retina.
The lens converges light rays to form a real, inverted, and diminished image on the retina.
The retina converts the image into electrical signals, which are carried to the brain via the optic nerve.
The brain interprets the image as upright and properly oriented.

Accommodation is the ability of the eye to adjust the focal length of the lens to focus on near or distant objects.
For near objects, the ciliary muscles contract, making the lens thicker and more convex.
For distant objects, the ciliary muscles relax, making the lens flatter.

WEEK EIGHT - ELECTRIC CHARGES

DEFINITION AND NATURE OF ELECTRIC CHARGE
Electric Charge is a fundamental property of matter that causes it to experience a force when placed in an electromagnetic field. It is responsible for electric and magnetic phenomena.
Microscopic View:
Atoms consist of protons (+), electrons (−), and neutrons (neutral).
Protons and electrons carry equal and opposite charges.
The net charge of an atom is normally zero (neutral).
When electrons are gained or lost, the body becomes charged.SI Unit: Coulomb (C)
Symbol: q or Q

TYPES OF ELECTRIC CHARGES
Positive Charges (+): Due to the loss of electrons. Found in protons.
Negative Charges (−): Due to the gain of electrons. Found in electrons

BASIC LAWS OF ELECTRIC CHARGES
Like charges repel each other.
Unlike charges attract each other.
Law of Charges: Like charges repel, unlike charges attract.

METHODS OF CHARGING A BODY
Charging by Friction
When two different materials are rubbed together, one loses electrons, the other gains.
Examples:
Glass rod + Silk → glass becomes positively charged.
Ebonite rod + Fur → ebonite becomes negatively charged.
Charging by Conduction (Contact)
A charged object touches a neutral object.
Charges flow from the charged to the neutral body.
The neutral body becomes similarly charged.

Charging by Induction
A charged object is brought close to a neutral object (without touching).
Charges in the neutral object redistribute.

The object is earthed (grounded), allowing electrons to move.
On removing the original charged body, the neutral object becomes oppositely charged.

PROPERTIES OF ELECTRIC CHARGES
Two types only: Positive and negative.
Additively: Charges add algebraically (e.g., +5 C + −3 C = +2 C).
Conservation of Charge: Charges can neither be created nor destroyed, only transferred.
Quantization of Charge:
Charge exists in discrete units.
Smallest unit is elementary charge (e): e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
q = ne, where n is an integer
Example:
q = 5 × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
Forces between charges: Charges exert attractive or repulsive electrostatic forces
governed by Coulomb’s Law.

LAW OF CONSERVATION OF CHARGE
The total electric charge in an isolated system remains constant.
In chemical reactions, decay, or charge transfer, the total charge before and after remains unchanged.

WEEK NINE - DESCRIPTION AND PROPERTIES OF FIELDS MEANING OF FIELD IN PHYSICS
In physics, a field is a region of space where an object experiences a force due to the presence of another object without physical contact.

Key Points:
Fields explain action at a distance.
Fields may be scalar or vector quantities.
Scalar field: Only magnitude (e.g., temperature field).
Vector field: Has both magnitude and direction (e.g., gravitational, electric, and magnetic fields).

TYPES OF FIELDS
Gravitational Field
Source: Mass.
Acts on: Other masses.
Nature of Force: Always attractive.
Example: Earth’s gravitational pull on objects.
Electric Field
Source: Electric charge.
Acts on: Other electric charges.
Nature of Force: Attractive or repulsive depending on the charges.
Example: Electrostatic force between charged balloons.
Magnetic Field
Source: Moving charges or magnets.
Acts on: Magnetic materials or moving charges.
Nature of Force: Can attract or repel.
Example: Interaction between two bar magnets or between a current-carrying wire and a compass needle.

WEEK 2 - MAGNETIC FIELDS MEANING OF MAGNETIC FIELD
A magnetic field is defined as the region of space surrounding a magnet, current-carrying conductor, or moving charged particle where a magnetic force is exerted on other magnetic materials or moving charges.

SOURCES OF MAGNETIC FIELD
Permanent Magnets

Naturally occurring or artificially created magnets with permanent magnetic properties.
Examples: Bar magnets, horseshoe magnets, neodymium magnets.
Fields are strongest at poles.
Current-Carrying Conductors

When electric current flows through a wire, it generates a magnetic field around it.
The direction of the field is given by the Right-Hand Grip Rule.

Solenoids and Electromagnets

A solenoid is a long coil of wire; when current flows through it, it acts like a bar magnet.
Electromagnets consist of solenoids with iron cores to enhance the magnetic field.

Moving Charges
Any moving electric charge produces a magnetic field (basis for electromagnetism).

Uses of electromagnets
Generators /electric motors
Separation of iron scraps from debris
Cranes for transporting heavy magnetic materials
Electric bells /telephone earpieces
Industrial robots[ in automobile assembly plants]
Electromagnetic switches [magnetic relays ]
Medical diagnosis [magnetic resonance scanners ]

MAGNETIC FIELD LINES (LINES OF FORCE)
This is an imaginary lines used to represent the direction and strength of a magnetic field. Properties:

They originate from the North pole and terminate at the South pole outside the magnet.
They form closed loops, continuing through the magnet from south to north internally.
Field lines never intersect each other.
The density of lines indicates field strength – closer lines mean stronger fields.
Tangent to the field line at any point gives the direction of the field at that point.
Magnetic field lines exhibit tension (they try to contract) and repulsion (they repel each other sideways).

PATTERNS OF MAGNETIC FIELD AROUND SOURCES
Bar Magnet:

Field lines are strongest at poles.
Shape: Symmetrical loops from North to South outside the magnet.

Straight Current-Carrying Wire:

Field lines form concentric circles around the wire.
Use Right-Hand Grip Rule: Thumb points in current direction, fingers curl in field direction.

Circular Loop of Wire:

Field lines are circular, but stronger and denser at the center of the loop.

Solenoid:

Resembles field of a bar magnet.
Strong and uniform field inside; weak and divergent outside.
Polarity depends on direction of current.

WEEK 3 - ELECTRO-MAGNETIC FIELDS MEANING OF ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELD

An electromagnetic field is a physical field produced by electrically charged objects, consisting of both electric and magnetic components, which interact with other charged particles.

Definition:

An electromagnetic field is the region of space where both electric and magnetic forces act simultaneously due to the presence of moving electric charges (such as electric currents).

COMPONENTS OF AN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELD

Electric Field (E):
A field produced by stationary electric charges.
Direction: From positive to negative charges.
Unit: Volt per meter (V/m)
Magnetic Field (B):
Produced by moving charges (current).
Direction: Given by the right-hand rule.
Unit: Tesla (T)

These two fields are mutually perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation in electromagnetic waves.

ORIGIN OF ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELD
From Moving Charges:

A stationary charge creates only an electric field.
A moving charge (current) creates both electric and magnetic fields, i.e., an
electromagnetic field.
From Accelerated Charges:

When charges accelerate, they emit electromagnetic radiation (e.g., radio waves, X- rays).

DYNAMIC NATURE OF ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELD

According to Maxwell's Equations, a changing electric field produces a magnetic field, and vice versa.

This interaction gives rise to self-propagating electromagnetic waves, such as light.

PROPERTIES OF ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS

Consists of oscillating electric and magnetic fields.
Fields are perpendicular to each other.
Fields propagate together in a direction perpendicular to both electric and magnetic fields.
Travel at the speed of light in vacuum (c = 3×108 m/s)
Can exert forces on other charges (via Lorentz force).
Can carry energy and momentum (e.g., in radiation pressure).

ELECTROMAGNETIC WAVE STRUCTURE

Electric Field (E) oscillates in one plane.
Magnetic Field (B) oscillates in a perpendicular plane.
Direction of wave propagation is perpendicular to both E and B.

This structure forms the basis of all forms of electromagnetic radiation: radio, microwave, infrared, visible light, UV, X-rays, and gamma rays.

WEEK 8 - SOLAR COLLECTOR DEFINITION OF SOLAR COLLECTOR
A solar collector is a device that absorbs solar radiation and converts it into heat energy, which is then used for heating fluids like water or air.

It is a key component in solar thermal energy systems, as opposed to photovoltaic panels that convert sunlight into electricity.

WORKING PRINCIPLE OF A SOLAR COLLECTOR
The basic working principle is based on the conversion of solar radiation (light energy) into
thermal energy (heat). Process:
Absorption of sunlight by a dark surface (absorber plate).
Conversion of light energy into heat.
Transfer of this heat to a working fluid (air, water, or antifreeze liquid).
Heated fluid is then used directly (e.g., water heating) or stored in an insulated tank.

MAIN TYPES OF SOLAR COLLECTORS
Flat-Plate Solar Collector
Most common type for domestic use.
Consists of an insulated box with a transparent cover and dark-colored absorber plate.
Heat is transferred to fluid (usually water) passing through tubes attached to the absorber.

Evacuated Tube Collector

Series of parallel glass tubes with vacuum between two layers to reduce heat loss.
More efficient in colder climates.

Concentrating (Parabolic) Solar Collector

Uses mirrors or lenses to focus sunlight onto a small area.
High temperatures achieved; used in industrial or power plant applications.

COMPONENTS OF A FLAT-PLATE SOLAR COLLECTOR

Transparent Cover (Glazing):
Allows sunlight in but prevents heat loss.
Absorber Plate:
Painted black to absorb maximum solar energy.
Tubes or Channels:
Fluid (water/antifreeze) flows through these to collect heat.
Insulation Layer:
Prevents heat from escaping from the back and sides.
Casing (Enclosure):
Protective box for durability and support.
Storage Tank:
Stores the heated fluid for use when sunlight is not available.
Pump (optional):
Circulates fluid between collector and storage tank (used in active systems).

WEEK 1 - ELECTRICAL CONTINUITY TESTING DEFINITION OF ELECTRICAL CONTINUITY
Electrical continuity refers to the uninterrupted flow of electric current through a conductive path or circuit.

A conductor or component is said to be continuous if it forms a complete, unbroken path for electrons to flow from one terminal to another.
A discontinuity (such as a cut wire, broken fuse, or faulty switch) interrupts the circuit, resulting in zero or no current flow.

Continuity Testing: This is a diagnostic procedure used to verify the presence or absence of a complete path for current flow within an electrical or electronic circuit.

ELECTRICAL PRINCIPLE BEHIND CONTINUITY TESTING
The concept is based on Ohm’s Law:

V = IR

In continuity testing, the goal is to determine if resistance RRR is low enough (close to 0 ohms) to allow current to pass.
A low or zero resistance implies continuity (closed path), while a very high or infinite resistance implies an open circuit (discontinuity).

In continuity mode, a multimeter sends a small voltage/current across the circuit and detects whether a return path exists.

TOOLS USED FOR CONTINUITY TESTING
Digital Multimeter:

Most reliable instrument for testing.
In continuity mode, it produces a beep if continuity exists.
Can also be used to measure resistance to determine continuity indirectly.
Analog Multimeter:

Uses a needle to display low resistance values.
Absence of deflection means no continuity.

Continuity Tester:

A simple handheld tool with a built-in battery and bulb or buzzer.
Lights up or buzzes when the circuit is complete.

Test Lamp:

Basic tool made from a bulb, battery, and two leads.
Lights up if current flows through the path (i.e., continuity exists).

PROCEDURE FOR CONTINUITY TESTING (USING MULTIMETER)
Safety First:

Always turn off the power supply to the circuit/device before testing.

Steps:

Set the multimeter to continuity mode (or lowest ohm range if not available).
Insert the probes into the multimeter (black to COM, red to Ω port).
Touch the two probes together to confirm the meter is working (it should beep).
Touch the probes to both ends of the component or wire being tested.
Observe the reading or listen for the beep.

WEEK 2 - ELECTROPLATING DEFINITION OF ELECTROPLATING
Electroplating is the process of depositing a thin layer of one metal onto the surface of another metal object using electric current and an electrolyte solution. It is a type of electrolysis used mainly for coating, protection, decoration, or improving conductivity of objects.

BASIC PRINCIPLE OF ELECTROPLATING
Based on electrolysis, where direct current (DC) passes through an electrolyte solution containing metal ions.
The metal to be deposited is connected as the anode, and the object to be coated is the
cathode.
When electricity flows, positive metal ions from the electrolyte are reduced at the cathode and form a coating.
Meanwhile, anode dissolves to replenish metal ions in solution (if it's made of the plating metal).

COMPONENTS OF AN ELECTROPLATING SYSTEM

ELECTROCHEMICAL REACTIONS IN ELECTROPLATING
Example: Electroplating a spoon with copper

Electrolyte: Copper(II) sulfate solution (CuSO₄)
Anode: Copper plate
Cathode: Iron or steel spoon
At Cathode (-):
Cu2++2e−→Cu(s)

Copper ions are reduced and deposited as metallic copper on the spoon.

At Anode (+):
Cu(s)→Cu2++2e−

Copper from the anode dissolves to replace ions in the solution.

The thickness of the deposited metal depends on current, time, concentration, and
temperature.

WEEK 3 - APPLICATION OF ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELD DEFINITION OF ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELD (EMF)
An electromagnetic field (EMF) is a physical field produced by electrically charged objects. It affects the behavior of charged particles in its vicinity. The field consists of two components:

Electric field (E-field): Produced by stationary charges. It exerts force on other electric charges in the field.
Magnetic field (B-field): Produced by moving charges (current). It exerts force on other moving charges.
The electromagnetic field is governed by Maxwell’s equations, which describe how electric and magnetic fields are generated and altered by each other and by charges and currents.

Interaction Between Electric and Magnetic Fields
Electric and magnetic fields are inherently linked. When an electric charge moves (i.e., a current flows), it produces a magnetic field. Similarly, a changing magnetic field induces an electric field (as described by Faraday’s Law of Electromagnetic Induction).
This interaction forms the basis of electromagnetic waves (e.g., radio waves, light). In an electromagnetic wave, the electric and magnetic fields oscillate perpendicular to each other and to the direction of wave propagation.

LAWS OF ELECTROMAGNETIC INDUCTION:
Faradays law states that whenever there is a change in the magnetic lines of force (e.m.f) is induced, the strength which is proportional to the rate of change of the flux linked with the circuit.

Lenz's law states that the induced e.m.f is in such a direction as to oppose the motion or change producing it.

COMMON APPLICATIONS OF ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS IN EVERYDAY LIFE
Electromagnetic fields are pervasive in modern society. Some common applications include:
Electric Power Transmission: Transformers and high-voltage lines rely on alternating EMFs to transmit electricity.
Household Appliances: Devices like microwaves, toasters, and induction cookers use EMFs for heating or operation.
Communication Systems: Radio, television, mobile phones, and Wi-Fi depend on electromagnetic wave propagation.
Medical Equipment: MRI scanners and diathermy machines use controlled EMFs for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes.
Security Systems: Metal detectors, RFID tags, and airport scanners operate using electromagnetic principles.

WORKING PRINCIPLES OF ELECTROMAGNETIC DEVICES
Eddy currents are loops of electrical current induced between conductors by changing magnetic field in the conductor. Eddy current flows in closed loops within conductors in planes perpendicular to the magnetic field. Eddy flows in the form of Eddies , in such a direction so as to oppose the motion of the conductor in the field.
Mutual Inductance: This is the flow of induced current or voltage in a coil due to an alternating current in a neigbouring coil.

Electric Motors:
Convert electrical energy into mechanical energy. When current flows through a coil in a magnetic field, a force (Lorentz force) acts on the coil, causing it to rotate (based on Fleming’s Left-Hand Rule).

Electric Generators:
Convert mechanical energy into electrical energy using electromagnetic induction. Movement of a conductor (coil) in a magnetic field induces current (based on Fleming’s Right-Hand Rule).
Transformers:
Transfer electrical energy between circuits via mutual induction. They consist of primary and secondary coils wound around a magnetic core. A changing current in the primary induces a voltage in the secondary.
Loudspeakers:
Convert electrical signals into sound. An alternating current in a coil causes vibration in a diaphragm attached to the coil, producing sound waves. This relies on the interaction between the coil and a permanent magnet.
Electromagnetic Relays:
Electrically operated switches. A small current activates an electromagnet, which moves a switch to allow a larger current to flow through another circuit.

TECHNOLOGICAL APPLICATIONS OF ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS
Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):
A diagnostic tool in medicine that uses strong magnetic fields and radio waves to produce detailed images of internal body structures. The magnetic field aligns the hydrogen nuclei in the body, and the radio waves disturb this alignment. The return to equilibrium emits signals used to construct images.
Induction Heating:
Uses electromagnetic induction to heat electrically conductive materials (e.g., metals). An alternating current produces eddy currents in the material, generating heat due to electrical resistance. It is widely used in metallurgy and cookware (e.g., induction stoves).
Wireless Power Transfer:
Transfers electrical energy without wires using oscillating magnetic fields (resonant inductive coupling). Common in wireless charging of phones, electric toothbrushes, and even electric vehicles.

WEEK 8 - ENERGY QUANTIZATION INTRODUCTION

In classical physics, energy was thought to vary continuously. However, early 20th-century discoveries in atomic and quantum physics revealed that energy, especially at the atomic and subatomic levels, exists in discrete packets, not continuous forms. This led to the concept of energy quantization, a fundamental idea in quantum physics that radically transformed our understanding of atomic structure and electromagnetic radiation.

DEFINITION OF ENERGY QUANTIZATION

Energy quantization refers to the concept that energy levels of electrons in an atom are not continuous, but rather exist in discrete (quantized) energy states. This means that an electron in an atom can only possess certain specific energy values and not just any arbitrary amount.

These energy states are associated with orbitals or energy levels (shells) around the nucleus. An electron must absorb or emit a fixed amount of energy, called a quantum, to move from one energy level to another.

DISCRETE ENERGY LEVELS IN ATOMS

Atoms have a set of fixed energy levels, denoted by quantum numbers (n = 1, 2, 3, …). These energy levels are like rungs of a ladder — an electron can "stand" on one rung or another, but cannot exist between rungs.

Ground state: The lowest energy level (n = 1), where the electron is most stable.
Excited state: Any energy level higher than the ground state (n = 2, 3, …), where the electron has gained energy.

Each level corresponds to a specific amount of energy, and the difference between any two levels represents the energy an electron must absorb or emit during a transition.
ELECTRON TRANSITIONS AND QUANTA OF ENERGY

Electrons can move between energy levels by either:

Absorbing energy: Moving to a higher energy level (excitation).
Emitting energy: Falling to a lower energy level (de-excitation).

The energy exchanged during these transitions is in the form of electromagnetic radiation (like light), and it is always a discrete quantum of energy, not a continuous flow.

This energy is given by:

ΔE = Ehigher – Elower = hf

Where:

ΔE = energy absorbed/emitted,
h = Planck’s constant (6.626×10−34 J
f = frequency of the absorbed/emitted radiation.

PLANCK’S HYPOTHESIS AND DERIVATION OF E = hf
In 1900, Max Planck introduced the idea that electromagnetic energy is quantized. His hypothesis stated that:

"Energy is emitted or absorbed in discrete units or quanta, and the energy EEE of each quantum is directly proportional to the frequency fff of radiation."

Mathematically, this is expressed as: E = hf
Where:

E = energy of a single quantum of radiation (in joules),
h = Planck’s constant,
f = frequency of radiation (in hertz, Hz).

This formula marked the beginning of quantum theory, breaking away from classical physics, which treated energy as continuous.

THE PHOTOELECTRIC EFFECT
The photoelectric effect is a phenomenon where electrons are ejected from the surface of a metal when light of sufficient frequency shines on it.

Experimental Observations:
No electrons are emitted unless the light’s frequency is above a threshold frequency
(f0f_0f0	), regardless of intensity.
The kinetic energy of emitted electrons depends on the frequency of the light, not its intensity.
The number of electrons emitted depends on the intensity (brightness) of the light, if frequency is above threshold.
The emission of electrons is instantaneous when the threshold frequency is reached.

These observations could not be explained by classical wave theory, but were successfully explained by Einstein using Planck’s quantum hypothesis.

Einstein proposed that light is made up of particles called photons, each carrying energy E=hf. When a photon strikes an electron, it transfers energy:

hf = ϕ + 1/2mv2
Where:

ϕ = work function (minimum energy needed to free an electron),
1/2	mv2 = kinetic energy of the emitted electron.

This confirmed that energy is quantized, further validating Planck’s idea.
QUANTIZATION AND SPECTRAL LINES

When electrons in an atom move between energy levels, they emit or absorb specific amounts of energy. These energy changes correspond to particular frequencies or wavelengths of light.

Each element emits light at specific frequencies, producing a line spectrum (as opposed to a continuous spectrum). This is known as an atomic emission or absorption spectrum.

For example:

Hydrogen atom emits visible light at specific lines in the Balmer series, corresponding to electron transitions to the n=2n = 2n=2 level.

These spectral lines serve as fingerprints of elements, used in chemical analysis and astronomy.

SIGNIFICANCE OF ENERGY QUANTIZATION

It explains stability of atoms — electrons cannot spiral into the nucleus as they can only occupy fixed levels.
It accounts for discrete spectral lines in emission and absorption spectra.
It supports the understanding of photoelectric effect, blackbody radiation, and atomic models like Bohr’s model.
It forms the basis for quantum mechanics, modern electronics, lasers, and semiconductors.

WEEK 9 - DUALITY OF MATTER INTRODUCTION

In classical physics, matter and energy were treated as distinct entities—matter exhibited particle-like properties, while energy (e.g., light) displayed wave-like behavior. However, experiments in the 20th century revealed that both light and matter can exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties depending on the context. This fundamental concept, known as wave-particle duality, forms the bedrock of modern quantum physics.

CONCEPT OF WAVE-PARTICLE DUALITY AND HISTORICAL CONTEXT

The wave-particle duality refers to the quantum mechanical concept that every particle or quantum entity can exhibit both wave and particle characteristics. This idea was first introduced in the early 20th century through two critical observations:

Photoelectric Effect (1905): Albert Einstein explained how light, traditionally considered a wave, could behave like a stream of particles (photons) when it knocks electrons off a metal surface. This demonstrated the particle nature of light.
Electron Diffraction (1927): Electrons, usually treated as particles, were found to produce interference patterns when passed through a crystal lattice or a double-slit apparatus—behaviors typical of waves. This demonstrated the wave nature of matter.

These discoveries suggested that both light and particles of matter (like electrons, protons, etc.) can behave as both waves and particles, challenging the classical distinction between the two.

DE BROGLIE’S HYPOTHESIS AND DE BROGLIE WAVELENGTH

In 1924, Louis de Broglie, a French physicist, extended the wave-particle duality of light to material particles such as electrons, protons, neutrons, and even larger objects theoretically. He proposed that every moving particle has a wave associated with it, called the matter wave.

EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE FOR WAVE NATURE OF PARTICLES

One of the most compelling pieces of evidence for the wave-like nature of particles came from electron diffraction experiments.

Davisson-Germer Experiment (1927):

IMPLICATIONS OF WAVE-PARTICLE DUALITY

Limits of Classical Physics:
The duality showed that classical physics couldn’t fully describe the behavior of subatomic particles. It led to the development of quantum mechanics, a new framework to explain such phenomena.
Probability in Quantum Physics:
Since particles behave like waves, their positions and momenta cannot be precisely predicted. Instead, they are described in terms of probability waves (wave functions).
Uncertainty Principle:
Werner Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle emerges naturally from wave-particle duality. The more precisely we know a particle's momentum, the less precisely we can know its position, and vice versa.
Atomic Structure:
The wave nature of electrons explains why electrons exist in discrete energy levels around the nucleus and not spiral into it, as classical physics would suggest.

TECHNOLOGIES AND APPLICATIONS BASED ON WAVE-PARTICLE DUALITY

Electron Microscope:
Utilizes the wave properties of electrons to resolve structures much smaller than what light microscopes can detect. Since electrons have much smaller wavelengths than visible light, they offer higher resolution.
Scanning Tunneling Microscope (STM):
Relies on quantum tunneling of electrons, a direct result of wave-like behavior, to image surfaces at the atomic level.
Quantum Mechanics & Quantum Computing:
The entire field of quantum physics is built upon the principles of wave-particle duality. Quantum computers leverage the dual nature of particles (qubits) to perform complex computations more efficiently than classical computers.
Particle Accelerators:
Use wave-particle properties to accelerate subatomic particles to high energies and study their interactions.
Semiconductor Physics:
The behavior of electrons in semiconductors, which power all modern electronics, is described using wave mechanics.

WEEK 10 - ENERGY AND SOCIETY MEANING AND FORMS OF ENERGY
Definition of Energy
Energy is the capacity to do work or cause change. It is a fundamental physical quantity and is required in all activities of life, from motion and growth to production and communication.
Energy exists in various forms and can be transformed from one form to another.

FORMS OF ENERGY

Kinetic Energy: Energy possessed by a body due to its motion. KE=1/2mv2 where m is mass and v is velocity.
Potential Energy: Energy stored in a body due to its position or configuration.
PE=mgh
where m is mass, g is acceleration due to gravity, and h is height.
Thermal Energy: The internal energy of an object due to the random motion of its particles. It is related to temperature.
Electrical Energy: Energy caused by the movement of electric charges (electrons). It powers electrical devices and systems.
Chemical Energy: Energy stored in chemical bonds of substances. It is released during chemical reactions, such as combustion or metabolism.
Nuclear Energy: Energy stored in the nucleus of atoms, released during nuclear fission or fusion.
Sound Energy: Energy carried by sound waves, resulting from vibrations of particles.
Radiant or Light Energy: Energy carried by electromagnetic waves, especially visible light, X-rays, UV rays, etc.

Non-Renewable Sources – Finite and depletable:
